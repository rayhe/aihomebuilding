Fannie Mae Will Count Your Backyard Cottage as Income. The Cap Is 30%.

By Catherine Chen September 12, 2026

On October 8, 2025, Fannie Mae published a document with the unglamorous title Selling Guide Update SEL-2025-08, the kind of release that moves no markets on the day and rewrites thousands of household budgets over the following year. Inside was a sentence that changed the arithmetic of buying a house with a backyard cottage: rental income from an accessory dwelling unit could now be counted toward mortgage qualification. Before that date, most lenders would not count a dollar of ADU rent. No landlord experience, no refinance, no income. A first-time buyer planning to rent out the granny flat to afford the mortgage was, in underwriting terms, imagining income that did not exist.

Now it exists. Sort of.

Mechanics first. Lenders count 75 percent of the ADU's market rent toward qualifying income, the standard haircut for vacancy and expenses, with the number established by a signed lease or the appraiser's rent schedule, Form 1007 or 1025, which keeps it honest because a licensed appraiser pulls the comps, not you. Only one ADU's income counts even if the property has three, and everything sits under the cap that qualifying ADU income cannot exceed 30 percent of total qualifying income.

Caps always have a distributional conscience, and this one's is uncomfortable.

The Math, Worked Honestly

Work the example. A buyer earns $6,000 a month in base qualifying income; the backyard cottage rents for $2,200, the Bay Area median for new ADUs in the Terner Center's statewide owner survey. Seventy-five percent of that rent, $1,650, counts, and against a new total of $7,650 it lands at 21.6 percent, comfortably under the cap. Qualifying income becomes $7,650, and at a 36 percent debt-to-income ceiling the monthly housing budget rises from $2,160 to $2,754, a $594 increase that at 6.75 percent on a 30-year note, roughly $6.49 of principal-and-interest per $1,000 borrowed, supports about $91,500 more in mortgage principal.

Real money. Now the case the rule was supposedly written for.

Take a buyer earning $3,500 a month with the same $2,200 cottage rent: uncapped, the $1,650 in qualifying rent would be 32 percent of the $5,150 total, breaching the cap, so the qualifying income gets solved downward until it equals 30 percent of $3,500 plus itself, which works out to $1,500. That is $150 a month of the rent's underwriting value erased by a formula. Borrowers who most need the income boost receive the smallest boost, while Fannie Mae's stated rationale, affordability and small-scale infill, points the other way. Nobody hid any of this; it is just in a selling guide nobody reads except the people paid to read it.

What the Cottage Costs

What the cottage costs is the next question, and the data situation is embarrassing. UC Berkeley's Terner Center survey from 2021 remains the most rigorous source available: median California ADU construction cost $150,000, about $250 per square foot, 71 percent of projects under $200,000, median rent $2,000 a month, figures that are five years old. A September 2026 review of city fee schedules confirmed no newer academic study exists. Everything current is contractor-published. Marketing with numbers attached.

For what it is worth, a Los Angeles builder publishing transparent 2026 pricing puts detached ADUs at $300 to $500 per square foot all-in and garage conversions at $180 to $320, with a 750-square-foot detached one-bedroom landing at $250,000 to $375,000. Design, engineering, permits, and Title 24 compliance eat 10 to 20 percent before a wall goes up, and then homeowners get ambushed by site and utility work, the category the glossy estimates skip: sewer and water extensions run $5,000 to $20,000, a panel upgrade $4,000 to $12,000. Price the lateral before the finishes, because budgets die underground.

Take the middle of the range, where a 750-square-foot cottage at $350 a foot costs $262,500 all-in and at $2,000 a month in rent, $24,000 a year, gross yield is 9.1 percent. Strip roughly 35 percent for taxes, insurance, maintenance, and vacancy, and the net is about $15,600 a year, which puts simple payback near 17 years, a horizon that tests most homeowners' patience and most lenders' imaginations. Borrow the full cost on a 7 percent HELOC and the interest-only payment runs $18,375 a year against $24,000 in rent, leaving roughly $5,600 of positive carry before a dollar of principal is repaid. In high-rent markets and at garage-conversion prices, the numbers sing. At the $400,000 Bay Area extreme the Terner researchers flagged, they wheeze.

The AI Told You Your Lot Qualifies. Maybe.

Before any of this math matters, someone has to answer the first question: can you legally build the thing. A small industry of AI zoning tools has grown up to answer it, the pitch being that you type in an address and receive a feasibility report covering setbacks, height limits, lot coverage, and hazard overlays. FutureLot sells exactly this to builders, with a human review layer and a few free reports for homeowners. At the architecture firm cove, the proprietary Vitras.ai engine sits inside feasibility studies so architects confront the constraints in the first conversation instead of week six.

This year brought the honest evaluation these tools needed. Researchers at the Urban Institute tested a retrieval-augmented generation system against Minneapolis's 467-page zoning code, using two personas: a professional developer on a multifamily project, and a single-family homeowner asking about an ADU. Their finding, reported without varnish: even with customization and system prompts, the AI's answers were often not helpful, and a zoning answer can be confident, well-structured, and wrong all at once, missing a site-specific exception, an overlay district, or an amendment adopted last month. Read that twice. As the researchers put it, zoning interpretation is not a search problem but a reasoning problem.

Treat every AI feasibility report accordingly, as a first pass rather than a determination, because the planning counter's word is the one that counts, and budget for the possibility that the report missed the thing that kills your project, since in 2026 testing that happened often.

The Appraisal Is the Bottleneck

Fannie Mae unlocked income, not value, and valuing a house with a cottage requires comparable sales of similar properties, which run thin where ADUs are new. Qualify on the income, then watch the deal die on appraisal: a $262,500 cottage does not automatically add $262,500 to appraised value, because appraisers record what comparable buyers paid, not what you spent.

Fannie Mae knows this: its expanded ADU eligibility, up to three ADUs on a one-unit property, manufactured homes carrying ADUs, two- and three-unit properties adding ADUs up to four total, is gated behind the redesigned Uniform Appraisal Dataset 3.6, the data plumbing meant to fix the comp problem. The mandatory deadline is November 2, 2026, seven weeks from this article's publication, and until then lender implementation stays uneven: eleven months after publication, trade reporting calls adoption "underway," which is the industry's word for incomplete.

A Case Against Building It

Steel-man the case against building. A first-timer stretching into a house with a cottage can qualify on income and fail on value in the same transaction, because the rule changed qualification math, not appraisal math. Lender overlays lag the policy by a year or more, while AI feasibility tools produce confident, well-structured, sometimes wrong answers about the single most project-killing variable. Per the Terner survey only 8 percent of California ADUs are short-term rentals, so this is a long-term-tenant income story carrying full landlord risk, not an arbitrage play. The 30 percent cap and the one-ADU limit bound the upside by design, and the payback table assumes rent arriving every month for 17 years, a premise landlords will smile at.

Against all of it: rents are real, the shortage is real, and a $120,000 garage conversion renting at $1,800 a month pays back in under six years on a gross basis. SEL-2025-08 did not create that math; it finally let the mortgage system see it.

What This Means for Your Project

If you are buying a home with an existing ADU, ask the lender before you fall in love with the house whether SEL-2025-08 is live in their underwriting system and whether their appraisers order UAD 3.6 reports. "Fannie Mae allows it" and "our bank does it" are different sentences. Get market rent documented on Form 1007 or 1025 early, because the 75 percent calculation runs on the appraiser's number, and a thin comp set, which is most comp sets for ADU properties in 2026, cuts both ways.

If you are building, run the AI feasibility report on your address and then verify every material claim at the planning counter before spending design money, and consider a pre-approved standard plan from your city or the state, where plan check compresses because the structural and code work is already vetted. And do the cap math on your actual income before assuming the rent buys you the bigger house, because for a $3,500-a-month buyer the 30 percent rule is not a detail. It is $150 a month of phantom income.

The code does not care about your feelings. This once, it noticed your cottage. Check whether your lender did too.

Limitations

Start with the data's age: the most rigorous ADU cost figures come from the 2021 Terner Center survey, and construction costs have moved since. The 2026 pricing numbers are contractor-published or journalist-compiled, not peer-reviewed, so the payback table is directional rather than a quote. Underwriting math here assumes a 36 percent DTI ceiling and a 6.75 percent rate; actual lender overlays, caps, and rates vary, and the $91,500 figure covers principal and interest only, while taxes and insurance on the larger purchase consume part of the budget increase. Policy mechanics are summarized from trade-press reporting and Fannie Mae's UAD 3.6 policy document; the full Selling Guide text governs, and the November 2, 2026 UAD 3.6 deadline should be confirmed against current Fannie Mae announcements. Urban Institute RAG findings are summarized from a secondary analysis; consult the underlying study for methodology. No independent testing of commercial AI zoning tools was performed.
