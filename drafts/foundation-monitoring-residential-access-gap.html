A $500 Sensor Catches Your Foundation Moving Before the Cracks Start. The Only Way to Buy One Is to Call the Company That Fixes Foundations.

By Jake Kowalski · July 25, 2026

In March, a homeowner in Plano, Texas, noticed a thin diagonal crack running from the corner of a bedroom window down toward the baseboard, maybe a sixteenth of an inch wide. She Googled it, read that most cracks are cosmetic, and caulked it shut. By August the front door wouldn't latch, and the crack had opened to a quarter inch, which is when she called a foundation repair company that drove steel piers under the slab and handed her a bill for $14,200.

A sensor buried near that slab could have caught the movement in January, two full months before the crack appeared, and a simple watering adjustment to the surrounding soil might have prevented the shift entirely. That technology exists today. Two companies sell continuous foundation monitoring systems to residential homeowners, and both of those companies are foundation repair contractors, which means the only path to a residential foundation monitor runs through the firms that profit when the monitor finds a problem.

What Actually Exists on the Market

Olshan Foundation Repair, a Texas-based company with operations across the Sun Belt, sells a system called ORLY that collects movement data hourly, transmits readings to a homeowner app, and flags shifts that could signal a developing structural problem. Olshan's own structural technicians also monitor the data feed and reach out when they see something concerning, which the company markets as a "check engine light" for your home that can increase resale value by giving buyers confidence that the foundation has been under continuous professional watch.

ORLY is patented by Steadfast Foundation Technologies LLC and sold exclusively through Olshan's contractor network.

G.L. Hunt Foundation Repair, headquartered in Dallas-Fort Worth, sells a competing system called Slabsure that uses patented sensors installed below ground level to monitor foundation movement continuously, delivering real-time diagnostic analysis through the same kind of early-warning pitch: detect the problem before it becomes expensive. G.L. Hunt holds exclusive distribution rights in the Dallas-Fort Worth, Austin, and San Antonio markets, meaning that in those cities, exactly one company can sell you a foundation monitor, and that company also happens to sell foundation repair.

Both systems work, and both measure real movement in real soil against real slabs using legitimate engineering instruments adapted from the kind of structural health monitoring that bridges and commercial buildings have relied on for decades. Neither system is available to a homeowner who doesn't want to call a foundation repair contractor first.

A Distribution Channel Built on Conflict

Olshan sells ORLY, and Olshan also sells foundation repair at an average cost the company itself puts at roughly $11,950 in 2026, with their proprietary Cable Lock ST Plus pier system running $1,825 to $3,350 per pier on jobs that typically require several. G.L. Hunt sells Slabsure, and G.L. Hunt also sells the structural repairs that follow when Slabsure detects movement. No independent monitoring option exists, no third-party subscription service operates in this space, and no device sits on a shelf at Lowe's that a homeowner could install and have monitored by a firm with no financial stake in the diagnosis.

Consider what this arrangement looks like from the other direction: imagine your car's check engine light was manufactured, installed, and monitored exclusively by the dealership's service department, so that every warning the light produces lands on the desk of someone whose revenue depends on recommending expensive repairs. You would want a second opinion before authorizing a $12,000 job. With residential foundation monitoring, there is no mechanism for a second opinion because the sensor, the diagnosis, and the repair invoice all originate from the same corporate entity.

Why No Independent Product Exists

Commercial structural health monitoring is a mature, multi-billion-dollar global industry built on accelerometers bolted to bridge pylons, strain gauges embedded in skyscrapers, and fiber optic cables threaded through dam walls, all of it capable of detecting sub-millimeter movement in concrete, and all of it getting cheaper every decade as the sensors shrink and the wireless protocols improve. Residential construction never adopted any of it, and the reasons are structural rather than technical.

No building code in the United States requires foundation monitoring on a single-family home. The International Residential Code, which governs how most American houses get built, specifies foundation construction standards in exhaustive detail but says nothing about monitoring one after occupancy. Commercial building codes reference ongoing structural assessments; residential codes assume the slab is fine until someone notices a crack, by which point the damage has already happened and the repair bill has already started climbing.

Foundation repair is also a fragmented, regional business where companies like Olshan and G.L. Hunt operate across a handful of Sun Belt states rather than nationally, which means a product like ORLY has no national distribution partner because the industry that would distribute it does not operate at national scale. No Home Depot category exists for "structural health monitoring, residential," and the buying pattern cannot develop because the product category doesn't exist, which persists because no building code requires it, which continues because nobody has lobbied for the requirement, which holds because the companies that would benefit from the mandate already control the only distribution channel.

What 146 Studies Reveal About the Research Gap

A 2026 systematic review published in Applied Sciences analyzed 146 peer-reviewed publications on AI-based crack detection in buildings, spanning 2015 to 2025, and found that the overwhelming majority of studies focus on detecting cracks after they have already formed, using computer vision models like YOLO trained on photographs of existing damage. Almost none of the 146 papers address predictive diagnostics, meaning the ability to forecast where and when a crack will appear before the concrete actually fails, which is exactly the gap the residential market exhibits: detection after damage rather than prevention before it.

Meanwhile, a team publishing in Scientific Reports this year demonstrated that a 12-megapixel smartphone camera combined with a printed calibration board and a deep learning model can measure crack widths on residential concrete to sub-millimeter precision, validated on actual homes experiencing differential foundation settlement. Cost of the sensor is whatever your phone already cost; cost of the calibration board is a few dollars. But the system measures cracks that already exist, and it cannot catch the soil movement that precedes them, which is the function a continuous monitoring sensor performs when it catches movement before the movement becomes visible structural damage.

A Patent That Points Toward Prevention

US Patent Application US20240271384A1, published for examination in 2024, describes a system that moves beyond detection into active prevention. Pressure sensors positioned against or within a home's foundation track movement over time, and when the system detects that soil has pulled away from the slab, it opens valves in the home's watering system to irrigate the affected area, re-hydrating the soil before the gap widens into a structural problem.

No product based on this patent is for sale, but the engineering is straightforward: a pressure sensor, a microcontroller, a solenoid valve, and a connection to an existing sprinkler line, with total hardware cost for a basic implementation sitting comfortably under $300 and an algorithm that amounts to simple threshold logic where a sustained pressure drop triggers targeted irrigation. A system like this would be most valuable in the expansive clay soils that cover much of Texas, Oklahoma, Colorado, and the Southeast, which happen to be precisely the regions where foundation repair is a multi-billion-dollar industry and where companies like Olshan and G.L. Hunt draw their revenue.

What a Homeowner Should Actually Do

If you live on expansive soil and your home sits on a slab-on-grade foundation, continuous monitoring is worth considering, but go in with your eyes open about who is selling it to you and what they stand to gain if the sensor detects something actionable. Ask whether the monitoring data can be shared with an independent structural engineer who has no financial relationship with the monitoring company. Ask whether you own the raw data or whether the monitoring company retains it. Ask what happens to your monitoring service if you choose a different contractor for the eventual repair.

If you are building a new home in a region with expansive soils, ask your builder about embedding monitoring sensors during construction, when installation cost is trivial compared to a retrofit that requires excavation around an existing slab. Most builders will respond with a blank stare, and that blank stare is the market gap reduced to a single facial expression.

Limitations

This analysis relies on publicly available product information from Olshan and G.L. Hunt, and neither company discloses the installed cost of their monitoring systems. Our claim that no independent residential foundation monitor exists at retail is based on searches of major home improvement retailers and Amazon as of July 2026, though a niche product may exist that this review did not find. Foundation repair cost data cited here reflects national averages that may not represent any specific homeowner's situation, and the patent discussed has not been independently evaluated for technical feasibility beyond the claims in the filing. Direct cost comparisons between commercial structural health monitoring and potential residential systems should account for differences in sensor density, monitoring duration, and liability structures that make these fundamentally different engineering problems even when the underlying sensor technology is identical.
