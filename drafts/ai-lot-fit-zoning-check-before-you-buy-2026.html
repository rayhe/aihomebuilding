Your Lot Is 6,000 Square Feet. The Code Only Lets You Build 2,400.

By Catherine Chen · September 21, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Every planning counter in California hosts a version of the same scene. A couple unrolls a site plan for the teardown lot they just bought, all cash, no contingencies, and the planner spends ninety seconds with a scale ruler before sliding it back across the counter. The house does not fit: not the style, not the budget, the geometry. Nobody told them until the money was gone.

Zoning is the least romantic part of building a home and the part most likely to detonate the project. Setbacks, lot coverage, floor-area ratios, height limits, daylight planes: a lattice of numbers, written in municipal code, that quietly decides how much house your land can hold long before an architect draws a line. Buyers shop for lots by price per square foot and fall in love with plans by the photos. The code does not care about either.

A small crop of software now promises to run this check before the heartbreak. Type in an address, get a verdict: which plans fit, what the setbacks allow, whether the dream survives contact with the ordinance. It is a seductive pitch. The code, as always, has opinions.

The incredible shrinking lot

Run the numbers on an ordinary parcel and the shrinkage is startling. Take a 6,000 square foot lot, 50 feet wide by 120 deep, in a standard R-1 single-family district. Monterey Park, California publishes its development standards in a clean table, so we can do this honestly: front setback 25 feet, rear setback 25 feet, side setbacks 5 feet on the first floor, widening to 10 feet on the second.

Subtract the setbacks and the buildable footprint is 40 feet by 70, or 2,800 square feet. That is 46.7 percent of the lot. More than half the parcel you paid for is legally unbuildable before design begins, consumed by the strips of side yard, front yard, and rear yard the city requires you to maintain as open space.

53% of a typical 6,000 sq ft R-1 lot is erased by setbacks alone, before a single design decision is made.

But here is the part buyers miss, and the reason this article exists. The same code caps total floor area at a 0.40 floor-area ratio on lots this size. On 6,000 square feet, that is 2,400 square feet of house, both floors combined, full stop. The setback envelope suggests you could mass up to 4,900 square feet across two stories. FAR says 2,400. The binding constraint is not the one you checked.

ConstraintMathResult
Buildable footprint (1st floor)(50−5−5) × (120−25−25)2,800 sq ft
Buildable envelope (2nd floor)(50−10−10) × (120−25−25)2,100 sq ft
Total envelope, both floors2,800 + 2,1004,900 sq ft
FAR cap (0.40 × 6,000)code maximum2,400 sq ft

Consider what that means for a buyer holding a 3,600 square foot plan, 1,800 per floor. Each floor fits comfortably inside its setback envelope. The design sails through every dimensional check and dies on the FAR line. Envelope compliance is necessary but not sufficient, and nobody discovers this at the open house.

Software that reads the code

The most on-the-nose product in this category is LotMatch.ai, from Paramount Construction: enter any teardown lot address and it shows which home plans from its catalog fit, with estimated construction costs. Its stated workflow is exactly the check described above, buildable footprint derived from zoning, setbacks, and lot dimensions, matched against plan dimensions. Its pitch line could be the thesis of this article: know what fits before hiring an architect.

One level up in sophistication sits TestFit, the Dallas-based feasibility platform that generates site configurations from zoning parameters and reports the financial implications in real time. In September 2026 it added MCP support so Claude or ChatGPT can drive its engine conversationally. A developer tells it to hit a yield target; a civil engineer tells it to solve the grading. Pricing runs roughly $3,000 to $5,000 per seat per year, which prices it for professionals, not buyers.

Around these two orbits a supporting cast: ReadyPermit AI, which launched in early 2026 with a parcel-level "Buildability Score" drawn from zoning ordinances plus FEMA, USGS, and EPA data; Prophetic's SiteAI, which auto-generates subdivision yield studies its vendor claims replace $3,000 to $10,000 engineering reports; ArchiWise AI, which answers pre-development questions grounded in cited planning documents. This category is real, funded, and multiplying.

What the honest vendors admit

Now the part the landing pages skip. The most useful review of TestFit, published by ArchiGen AI, contains a sentence every buyer should memorize: the "AI" framing in the marketing is generous, because the underlying solver is closer to mature constraint programming than to learned generative models. When a unit count drops, you can point to the setback change rather than shrugging at a black box.

That is not an insult. Deterministic arithmetic is exactly what you want from a zoning check, and it is why these tools can be trustworthy in a way chatbot oracles cannot. But buyers should not pay AI premiums for long division, and they should understand what the solver cannot see.

Start with data vintage. Every lot-fit verdict is only as current as the zoning database behind it, and municipal codes change constantly. Overlays are the silent killers: hillside districts, historic designations, fire severity zones, daylight planes, view corridors. A tool that models base-zone setbacks but misses the overlay gives you a confident wrong answer, which is worse than no answer. Before trusting any verdict, ask the vendor two questions: when was your code data last synced for this jurisdiction, and which overlays do you model?

Then follow the money. LotMatch.ai is a builder's lead funnel; the email capture asks for your budget range and preferred home style before it shows you plans. The fit analysis serves Paramount's catalog and pricing, not your independent interest. That does not make the math wrong, but it makes the "fit" verdict a sales-qualified lead wearing a lab coat. Independent verification against the city's published table costs you twenty minutes.

The research frontier is honest about its own limits, which is more than most vendors manage. A 2025 paper demonstrated LLMs generating code-compliance checks inside Revit for a single-family home against IRC regulations, and it worked, on well-structured models. It also reported that two of the four models tested, Llama 3.1-405B and Copilot, could not generate working checks at all. A 2019 prototype for automated residential permit checking classified building rules by how hard they are to translate into machine-readable form and essentially concluded that the easy rules were automatable and the hard ones were not. Seven years later, that is still roughly where things stand.

What to do on Monday

If you are shopping for a teardown lot, run the setback and FAR math yourself before the offer. Pull the city's zoning table, it is public, and spend twenty minutes with the four numbers that matter: setbacks, coverage, FAR, height. This single exercise kills more bad lots than any software subscription.

Check FAR before setbacks. On small urban lots the floor-area cap almost always binds first, which is the opposite of what intuition suggests. Buyers fixate on whether the footprint fits; the code evicts them on total area.

If you use a lot-fit tool, interrogate its data the way a planner would. Ask for the code vintage, the overlay coverage, and the section numbers behind each figure. A vendor that cites chapter and verse is selling diligence. One that shows a green checkmark is selling comfort.

Book the free pre-application meeting. Nearly every planning department offers one, and thirty minutes with the counter planner who will eventually review your project beats any algorithmic verdict. Bring the lot address and the plan dimensions. Planners love this meeting because it prevents the ninety-second scale-ruler scene described at the top.

If you are a custom builder, move the lot-fit screen to the go/no-go gate before schematic design, not the plan-check scramble after it. A feasibility study that costs a few thousand dollars, Prophetic's vendor-claimed range for engineering yield studies is $3,000 to $10,000, is cheap insurance against a redesign that costs multiples of that and months you cannot bill.

What this analysis cannot tell you

The worked example uses Monterey Park's R-1 table because it is cleanly published and current through a 2024 ordinance. Your jurisdiction will differ, sometimes wildly: Atherton requires 60-foot front setbacks, and unincorporated San Mateo County has no single R-1 setback at all, with dimensions set by mapped combining districts. The 53-percent shrinkage figure is illustrative, not universal.

I did not independently test LotMatch.ai's fit accuracy; its claims are vendor-reported, and its business model is lead generation for a builder. TestFit's pricing comes from a third-party review, not a published price list. Prophetic's $3,000 to $10,000 figure describes subdivision yield studies, not custom-home feasibility, and is a vendor claim used here as an order-of-magnitude anchor. The arXiv paper's LLM results are from a controlled research setting on well-structured BIM models; messy real-world models perform worse, and half the models tested failed outright.

No software replaces the planner at the counter. The code is public, the math is twentieth-century, and the most sophisticated model in this category is still, at bottom, a very fast intern with a scale ruler. Use it that way and it earns its keep. Worship it and you will learn what the couple in the first paragraph learned, ninety seconds too late.
