Your Contractor's License Looks Clean. 10,719 Complaints Were Closed Before You Could See Them.

By Catherine Chen · September 20, 2026 · 7 min read

Anchored Tiny Homes was selling ADUs across California when the state revoked its license in November 2024. The Contractors State License Board had received complaints about the company taking clients' money and dropping projects. When NBC Bay Area checked the public record, it showed ten complaints. The board had actually received around 249.

That ratio is not a clerical quirk; it is the system working as designed, and once you understand the mechanism, the reassuring license database starts to look like a stage prop, because the record consumers are told to trust only lists the complaints the board decided were worth its time. When a contractor settles a complaint for cash, the CSLB does not investigate, and the complaint never becomes a matter of public record. The state, in its own chief of public affairs' words, just moves on. NBC's estimate of complaints the board closed without investigation between 2020 and 2024 runs to 10,719, including cases where customers accused contractors of breaking the law. None of those complaints surface on the database every consumer guide tells you to check, which means the standard advice to check the license is really advice to check the fraction of the record the board felt like showing you.

So the first piece of advice every remodeling guide prints is built on a file with the good parts redacted. Secrets stay with the state. Receipts stay with the permit office.

The permit record does not settle

There is a second public record that cannot be negotiated away. Every permitted job leaves a paper trail at the building department: who pulled it, what it was for, when it passed final inspection. BuildZoom has spent years aggregating that trail into a single database covering 3.5 million licensed contractors, 300 million permits, and 135,000 property-owner reviews. Its contractor score weighs license status, insurance status, verified work history, standing with consumer interest groups, and verified reviews against that permit history.

This is the data the settlement trick cannot touch. A complaint disappears when a check clears, while a permit does not disappear, because the building department never got a phone call. Volume is not quality, and I will get to the caveats, but a contractor with 400 permitted jobs and a clean license is a different proposition than one with 400 permitted jobs, a clean license, and twelve complaints the state decided not to investigate, because the first one survived scrutiny and the second one bought its way out of it. From the permit count you get the denominator nobody else publishes, which means you can compute the rate.

Do the arithmetic yourself, because no vetting tool will print it for you. Contractor A has twelve complaints across 400 permitted jobs: a 3 percent complaint rate. Contractor B has six complaints across twelve permits: 50 percent. Raw counts make A look worse, while the rate says B is the one to avoid, which is exactly the inversion you want before you sign anything. Pull the complaint count from the state license check, pull the permit count from the BuildZoom profile, divide one by the other, and you have done more real due diligence than most homeowners do in the entire process of hiring a contractor. It takes ninety seconds, it costs nothing, and it inverts the story the raw numbers tell.

What the numbers say about the risk

Complaints are not rare. The CSLB's enforcement division took in 9,317 complaints against licensed and unlicensed contractors in roughly nine months of its current fiscal year, and its intake and mediation staff recovered $11.7 million in consumer restitution over the same stretch, numbers the board reports to its own enforcement committee while the settled cases behind them vanish from the public file. In late 2023 the board created a Multiple Offender Unit specifically for contractors with ten or more open complaints, and after hiring three more analysts, the referral threshold drops to five. The unit's 22 accused licensees cover 84 consumer complaints, and nearly a quarter of those licensees lost their licenses, which is either a sign the system works when it finally focuses or a sign of how much goes unfocused the rest of the time.

Zoom out and the pattern is national: American consumers reported 81,925 home improvement scams in 2024, with a median loss of $1,800 that makes it the fourth-costliest scam category the BBB tracks. Nearly one in ten Americans has experienced a home improvement scam. Against that backdrop, California's backstop is a $25,000 contractor bond, and the board's own staff acknowledge there is no general fund that makes consumers whole when a contractor vanishes with the deposit, which means your real insurance policy is the half hour of homework this article describes.

What the tools cannot tell you

Now the lawyer in me ruins the sales pitch. BuildZoom's scoring methodology is proprietary, which means it cannot be audited, challenged, or appealed, and a homeowner who loses a bid dispute with a number has no hearing, no ombudsman, and no one to call. Permit data is patchy in rural counties and small cities that still run on paper; a contractor who works mostly without permits looks pristine on paper, which is the exact opposite of what the paper should mean. High permit volume with zero complaints can signal a well-run operation, or a well-lawyered one. And the vetting tools are point-in-time: a clean license check today says nothing about a lapse last quarter.

There is a darker failure mode nobody in the screening business advertises. Scores collapse context into a number, and homeowners will treat the number as truth. One contentious remodel, one client who refused to pay, one year when everything went sideways can generate complaints that haunt a contractor long after the dispute was settled and the full story sits in a filing cabinet nobody digitized, for the simple reason that nobody in the screening business is paid to read filing cabinets. An algorithm that protects your family from a $64,000 loss can quietly bankrupt an honest small contractor, and unlike the state board, the algorithm has no appeals process. A low score is not a conviction, so treat it as a reason to ask harder questions rather than a verdict. Scores are not justice.

The thirty-minute playbook

Before you sign anything

  1. Run the state license check yourself. Verify the license directly, not through the link the contractor sends you, and check classifications, bond status, and workers' comp. In California the CSLB's instant license check is free, but understand what it excludes: settled complaints.
  2. Pull the permit history. A BuildZoom profile shows permitted job volume by trade and jurisdiction, and a GC doing $2M a year of work with no permits on record is a finding, not an absence of data.
  3. Compute the complaint rate. Divide complaints by permitted jobs, compare rates instead of counts, and stay skeptical of both a zero and a headline number.
  4. Get three written bids, and read the low one twice. The state license board and the permit-data platforms agree on this one: an abnormally low bid usually means the scope is wrong, not that you found a bargain.
  5. Mind the deposit, because California caps home-improvement down payments at 10 percent or $1,000, whichever is less, and the CSLB prints it in its consumer guides, so a contractor who demands more is telling you something. Believe them.
  6. Never pay in cash. Cash is the settlement medium of choice precisely because it leaves no trail, while paper checks and cards create the records that make every step above possible.

What this piece did not prove

A few honest boundaries before the close. I did not run live license checks or permit queries against named contractors for this article; the complaint-rate arithmetic is a method illustrated with example figures, not findings about specific firms. CSLB complaint and restitution figures are California-specific; most state boards publish less, and many publish nothing comparable. Permit-data coverage varies by jurisdiction, so the denominator will be weaker in places that still run on paper files. BBB scam counts reflect reported incidents; the true incidence is higher. And the Anchored Tiny Homes complaint figures come from NBC Bay Area's reporting on board records, not from the board's own published statistics, because the board does not publish them.

The uncomfortable bottom line is that consumer protection in residential construction runs on records that were designed for regulators, not homeowners. The license database tells you who the state punished. The permit record tells you who actually did the work. Put the two together and divide, and you get the one number nobody in the process volunteers: how often this contractor's jobs ended in a complaint. That arithmetic will not catch every bad actor. It will catch the ones the state already knew about and declined to show you, which, as of this writing, is ten thousand of them.
