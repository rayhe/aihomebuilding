Your Mother-in-Law Is Moving In. An Algorithm Drew the Second Kitchen.

By Elena Vasquez · September 17, 2026

Stand in the kitchen of a house built for one family and listen. Two dishwashers are running. One belongs to the couple who bought the house. The other belongs to the mother who lives down the hall, in the wing the builder called a suite, behind a door that locks from both sides, and the sound of both machines at once is the sound the American home makes now. This is what it has quietly become. Two households. One envelope. No going back.

Pew Research Center's analysis of census data puts the scale plainly: 18 percent of Americans live in multigenerational households, more than double the share in the early 1970s, with nearly a third of adults aged 25 to 29 living with parents or grandparents. This year, the National Association of Realtors reported that 14 percent of 2025 homebuyers purchased a multigenerational home, and most of them did it for one reason: caring for aging parents. Realtor.com's 2026 research counts roughly 4 million owner-occupied multigenerational households and finds the pace of growth picking up, with the typical household at five people sharing a four-bedroom home on a $131,000 median income, which is the demographic reality behind every one of these floor plans. A Veterans United survey of 900 prospective buyers found 28 percent planning a multigenerational purchase, and whatever you call it, the nuclear-family box is no longer the default container.

Production builders figured this out fifteen years ago, and the answer has been sitting in model-home brochures ever since, in floor plans that have barely changed because the underlying human problem has barely changed. Lennar created its Next Gen "home within a home" design in 2011 as a direct response to rising multigenerational living, and the formula has barely changed since: an attached private suite with its own separate entrance, living room, bedroom, bathroom, and kitchenette. Plus one architectural detail that carries the whole typology on its back, a move so small it fits in a hardware-store bag. An interior door, lockable from both sides, between the suite and the main house. Jeff Roos, Lennar's regional president for the West, put the appeal in plain language: families value being able to see one another every day, whether it is grandparents acting as full-time nannies, young adults still at home, or aging parents who need closer supervision, which is the entire demand curve in one sentence. That door is what makes it bearable, because it modulates, with a single piece of hardware, between one house and two households.

The Kitchenette Is a Legal Device

Now the part the machines get wrong. In many American cities, a full second kitchen, one with a range or cooktop, quietly reclassifies the suite, and the reclassification is where the money disappears. It stops being a bedroom wing and starts being an accessory dwelling unit or a duplex, and that reclassification drags in parking minimums, setback requirements, sometimes separate utility connections and sewer fees, a cascade of costs that no floor-plan generator has ever once mentioned. A kitchenette, meaning a wet bar, a microwave, an undercounter refrigerator, no range, stays inside single-family zoning. One appliance separates the two. In permitting, the difference can be tens of thousands of dollars.

Ask a generative floor-plan tool to "add a second kitchen" and it will oblige in about thirty seconds, sink in the wrong place, range and all, and not one of them will ask which zoning district you are in. Exploration has gotten radically cheaper, these services run a few dollars to fifty dollars a plan against the $2,400 to $14,000 an architect charges to design an addition, per Angi, but the cheapest phase of design is now the phase that used to force the expensive conversation. A thirty-second sketch never has to confront the planning department; construction documents do.

So the first architectural move in a multigenerational project is not a layout; it is a phone call, the kind you make before the first sketch, not after the third revision, because everything downstream, from the plumbing stack to the permit set, depends on which side of the kitchenette line your jurisdiction puts you on. Ask the planning counter where your jurisdiction draws the kitchenette-versus-kitchen line, because that answer decides whether the suite is a legal bedroom wing, a permitted ADU with fees attached, or a project the city will stop at the counter. Design from that answer outward, and every AI-generated layout that follows it starts from a code line instead of a floor plan, which is the one ordering that survives contact with a permit reviewer.

Where the Water Goes

The second move is older than any software: stack the wet rooms. A suite bathroom and kitchenette belong within ten to fifteen feet of the main plumbing stack, because every additional foot of new drain line is plumber time at $150 to $275 an hour, and rough-in plumbing for an in-law suite runs about $6,500 before a single fixture is purchased, according to HomeAdvisor's 2025 cost data. This is the constraint generative tools genuinely handle well, and almost the only one. Fix the stack location, ask for ten kitchenette-and-bath arrangements clustered near it, and an hour of exploration replaces a week of revision cycles with an architect who charges by the hour and answers email on Tuesdays. Knowing the constraint exists, and insisting the tool respect it, is the human contribution, and it is the only part of the process that cannot be automated away, because judgment about what matters is not a parameter you can tune.

What nobody models is the acoustics. A shared wall between the suite and the main house wants serious sound separation, the kind that means the 11 PM news stays in one household and stays out of the other, because nothing ends a multigenerational arrangement faster than hearing everything. Budget for a properly insulated partition with staggered framing, not a standard interior wall. Put the suite on its own mini-split zone while you are at it; the most common multigenerational conflict after noise is temperature, and a $1,500 mini-split is cheaper than a decade of thermostat negotiation.

There are quieter moves worth specifying. Zero-threshold entries and a curbless shower cost little during construction and matter enormously when the suite's resident is 78, which is the age when a quarter-inch lip at the shower becomes a fall risk and a fall becomes a hospital stay. A second exterior exit makes the suite bedroom legal under egress rules, and it doubles as the dignified front door the separate entrance promises, the one that lets a grandparent come and go without filing past the main household's breakfast table. Sightlines deserve a look too, because privacy is often a diagonal, not a wall, and a suite whose living-room window stares directly into the main kitchen will feel surveilled no matter how good the floor plan reads on paper.

The Money

Angi's 2026 data puts the average attached in-law suite at $82,750 nationally, which works out to roughly 450 square feet at a mid-range $185 per square foot, squarely inside the published $150-to-$400-per-square-foot band for ADU-class construction. In Los Angeles the same suite runs $165,500 to $190,400, which is why the payback math below has a geography problem. Set that against the expenses the suite replaces: a parent's $2,000-a-month apartment is $24,000 a year, which puts the national-average suite at a 3.5-year simple payback. Full-time daycare at $1,300 a month is $15,600 a year, a 5.3-year payback, and grandparents who provide the care erase the line item entirely. In Los Angeles you need both offsets firing at once to make the math close, and even then you should price the financing, because these are simple paybacks, not discounted ones.

There is also the uncomfortable finding from Realtor.com's 2025 analysis: multigenerational homes are significantly more expensive than standard properties, which means the affordability story the trend pieces tell is really a story about spending more to spend less. That cost-savings motive, cited by 29 percent of NAR's multigenerational buyers, is partly an illusion, because you pay a premium to buy the very arrangement that is supposed to save you money. NAHB's buyer research adds a cultural wrinkle: 39 percent of all buyers want a multigenerational design, but the preference runs 53 percent among Hispanic buyers, 50 percent among African-American buyers, and 46 percent among Asian buyers, against 35 percent of white buyers. This market is not evenly distributed, and builders who treat multigenerational design as a niche option are misreading their own customers.

What the Machines Flatten

My skepticism of the generators is not about the geometry; it is about everything they leave out, which is everything that makes a suite livable. They draw in two dimensions and the failures live in the third: the ceiling height that decides whether the suite feels like a home or a basement, the window placement that governs both light and privacy, the section through the shared wall where acoustic insulation either exists or does not. A plan view cannot tell you whether the suite smells like the main kitchen's frying pan. Only the people living there can. Smell is a section, not a plan.

And the strongest argument against the whole enterprise deserves its full weight. NAR's multigenerational buyer share fell from 17 percent to 14 percent in a single year even as the trend stories kept coming. Caregiving, which drives 41 percent of this demand, does not care about payback tables; a family moving a parent in because of a fall is not running a discounted cash flow. Layout is not the binding constraint; the planning counter is, and the sewer connection fee, and the jurisdiction that bans second kitchens outright. An AI that draws thirty floor plans for a suite your city will not permit is not a design tool; it is a very fast way to fall in love with an illegal building.

What We Did Not Prove

The cost figures come from Angi and HomeAdvisor's national averages, and the Los Angeles numbers show how violently those averages move by market; get local bids before trusting any of them. That kitchenette-versus-kitchen distinction varies by jurisdiction, which is precisely the point, but no single code section is cited here because no single one governs. This payback math is simple and undiscounted, ignores financing costs, and assumes the offset expense is real, which means a parent who was not renting elsewhere changes the arithmetic entirely. Pew's 18 percent figure comes from its 2022 census-data analysis, the most recent comprehensive treatment we could locate, and the demographers who would have a newer number are busy counting a trend that keeps outrunning the surveys. And the generative tools described here produce exploration sketches, not construction documents; nothing in this article suggests AI output is buildable as generated, a distinction the vendors blur and the building department does not.

Get the moves right, the lockable door, the wet rooms stacked on the main drain, the phone call to the planning department before the first sketch, and one house can hold two lives at once. A drawing that gets there starts with a phone call to the planning department. The prompt comes later.
