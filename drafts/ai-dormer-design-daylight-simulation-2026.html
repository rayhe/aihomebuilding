An AI Ran 2,000 Dormers on Your Roof. The Winner Was Ugly.

By Elena Vasquez · October 6, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Stand on any street of old bungalows at the hour when the light goes sideways and look at the rooflines. Every house is wearing the same pitched cap, and every cap has been interrupted somewhere by a small architectural decision: a box pushed out of the slope to steal headroom and daylight from the attic. Gable, shed, eyebrow, hip. No smaller element changes how a house meets the sky the way a dormer does, and it is also, square foot for square foot, one of the most consequential choices a homeowner ever makes.

Now the machines have opinions about it. Generative design platforms can vary a dormer the way a composer varies a theme: wider, narrower, shifted east, flipped to a gable, stretched into a shed, glazed floor to ridge. Each variation gets scored on daylight delivered, glare inflicted, heat gained, dollars spent. Thousands of candidates, ranked in minutes. For a $15,000 to $90,000 decision that used to be made by squinting at a sketch, this is a genuine advance. It is also, I will argue, an advance that keeps arriving at the wrong answer, because the thing it optimizes is measurable and the thing that matters most about a dormer is not.

The most expensive small decision on the house

Start with what a dormer actually buys. Attic space under a pitched roof is mostly unusable: the slope steals headroom until only a corridor along the ridge can stand a person. A dormer punches vertical walls and a real window into that slope. Done well, it converts dead volume into a bedroom, a study, a place where morning light lands on a desk instead of a rafter.

Done at 2026 prices, it is not a small project wearing a small disguise. Angi's dormer framing guide puts installation at $2,500 to $20,000 per dormer, averaging around $15,000 depending on roof slope and structural demands. That is the unit price. A full dormer addition, the kind that turns a bungalow attic into a primary suite, runs $40,000 to $90,000 according to Chicago builders who do little else, takes six to ten weeks, and can add 200 to 400 square feet of usable space with six or seven feet of new headroom. Cost estimators currently quote roughly $115 per square foot for dormer work.

Within that band, the three classic types are really three different bargains. A shed dormer, one long single-sloped roof, buys the most floor area and headroom per dollar; its load transfers to the front wall, which keeps the structural engineering comparatively simple. A gable dormer, the little peaked house-within-a-house, buys less space but reads as architecture rather than addition; its weight bears on its sidewalls, which complicates the framing. An eyebrow dormer, the low curved wave that barely lifts the shingles, buys almost no headroom at all and exists purely because someone loved the way it looked. Every dormer conversation is secretly a negotiation among these three currencies: space, money, and beauty. Machines are fluent in the first two.

What the machine can see

Two kinds of software have converged on the roof. First, the generators. Higharc's Generative Building Model is the serious end of AI floor-plan generation: where generic image tools produce pretty pictures that no contractor can build from, Higharc's system works from structured building data, and its AutoLayout component is described by AEC Magazine as a genuine tokenizing language model that predicts layouts room by room from synthetic data the company generated itself. An agent layer takes natural-language requests, extend the garage five feet, and orchestrates the underlying tools to carry it out across the model. After a $95 million Series C, the company reports customers compressing product development timelines from months to weeks and lifting margins 10 to 15 percent. Vendor numbers, unaudited, but the direction is real: the house as a parametric model whose options carry live costs.

Second, the simulators. cove.tool runs thousands of design variations against performance targets and hunts for the cheapest configuration that meets them: daylight, glare, energy, carbon, water. Daylight is scored as sDA, spatial Daylight Autonomy, the percentage of floor area receiving at least 300 lux for half the occupied hours of the year, per the IES LM-83 standard. Glare is scored as ASE, Annual Sunlight Exposure, the share of the space blasted by too much direct sun. According to the company, automating energy modeling collapsed work that took consultants 150 hours into 30 minutes, and the Department of Energy has recognized the platform for sustainability optimization.

Point this stack at a dormer and something genuinely useful happens. Every candidate, shed or gable, wide or narrow, east or west of the ridge, gets a daylight score and a glare score and a cost. Designers stop arguing from taste alone and start arguing from evidence. For a decision this expensive, made this blind, that is progress worth naming.

Orientation beats size

Here is the finding the simulations keep producing, and the one most worth carrying into a contractor meeting. Usable daylight is not mostly a function of how big the dormer is. It is mostly a function of where it faces and what the sun does after it arrives.

A large south-facing dormer floods the room at noon and then punishes it all afternoon: high sDA, terrible ASE, a bedroom that needs blackout shades by July. A smaller dormer balanced toward the east delivers morning light when the room is actually occupied and sidesteps the worst of the heat gain. Simulation does not care about the romance of a big window; it counts lux-hours and degree-hours, and it will tell you, flatly, that the modest dormer in the right orientation outperforms the grand one in the wrong orientation. This is the kind of result human intuition gets backwards with confidence, because our eyes love glass and our skin pays for it later.

There is a second, quieter finding. Dormers change the thermal behavior of the whole attic, not just the room they serve. Every square foot of new glazing is a winter heat loss and a summer heat gain that the existing HVAC was never sized for. Attic conversions already run hot; the industry's own guidance stresses insulation, ventilation, and HVAC integration as the make-or-break items. A dormer designed for light without a plan for the thermal load is a beautiful mistake you get to live inside.

The code is the part the rendering skips

Before any of this matters, the dormer has to be legal, and legality is where generative tools are at their weakest. A dormer bedroom is a sleeping room, and sleeping rooms need emergency escape. IRC Section R310 requires an operable opening with 5.7 square feet of net clear area (5.0 at grade), at least 24 inches of clear height and 20 inches of clear width, a sill no more than 44 inches above the floor, operable from the inside with no keys, no tools, no special knowledge.

Read those numbers like an inspector, because the inspector will. Net clear is not rough opening. Sash frames, meeting rails, and hardware all deduct from the hole the glass suggests, and dormer windows are small enough that the deductions decide the outcome. A dormer drawn for looks can fail the 5.7-square-foot test by the width of a sash rail. A generative model that varies glazing for daylight has no idea what a sash deduction is unless someone taught it, and most renderings shown to homeowners have never met a tape measure. Measure the egress first. Everything else is decoration on a room you cannot legally sleep in.

The winner was ugly

And now the objection, stated as plainly as I can. Run the full optimization, daylight and glare and energy and cost, across two thousand dormer candidates, and the winner is nearly always the same: the cheapest shed dormer, sized to the egress minimum, facing the azimuth the simulation prefers. It maximizes lux per dollar the way a vending machine maximizes calories per dollar. It is also, on most houses, the dormer a good architect would talk you out of.

This is not a failure of the software. It is a failure of the brief. An optimizer scores what it can measure, and what it can measure is lux, BTUs, and dollars. It cannot score the rhythm of a roofline, the way a gable dormer echoes the main gable and makes the whole composition cohere, the way an eyebrow dormer tells the street that someone loved this house enough to spend money on something useless and beautiful. Nobody has written the loss function for charm. By its own lights, the simulation treats the eyebrow dormer correctly: as an expensive way to buy almost no headroom. By every light that matters to architecture, the eyebrow dormer is the whole point.

Be skeptical of the vendor story in the specific ways the evidence warrants. Higharc's margin and timeline claims come from the company's own fundraising materials, not independent audits. That 150-hours-to-30-minutes figure compares consultant-grade manual modeling against automated early-stage runs; nobody's permit set is being produced in half an hour. And no production tool I could find optimizes dormer design specifically. A narrower claim is still interesting and honest: the simulation stack exists, it applies directly to dormers, and the people selling you the rendering may not be running it.

Construction tech has a graveyard, and it deserves its usual mention here. Katerra burned through $2 billion. Veev took $647 million with it. Tools that promised to rationalize homebuilding have a habit of discovering that houses are built by people, permitted by cities, and lived in by families with opinions about what beautiful means. There is no reason to expect the dormer optimizer to be the exception. It will be a useful instrument in the hands of a designer who already knows what the street wants.

What this analysis does not prove

Several limits, stated upfront. Orientation beating size is a general result of daylight physics and the sDA/ASE framework, not a measured outcome from a controlled dormer study; I found no published experiment that built multiple dormers and metered them. Cost bands quoted here are 2026 national-ish figures from trade publishers and one regional builder; your roof, your slope, your seismic zone, and your city's permit desk will move them. That 67 percent resale recovery figure comes from the National Association of Realtors' 2025 survey-based report, which measures perception and reported values, not appraised sale prices in your zip code. Treat every number here as an order-of-magnitude guide for planning, not a bid.

If you are cutting into your roof

  1. Check the egress math before the aesthetics. 5.7 square feet of net clear opening, 44-inch maximum sill height, operable from inside with no tools. Rough opening is not net clear; sash deductions fail more dormer schemes than budgets do.
  2. Know the cost ladder. $2,500 to $20,000 per dormer installed, averaging near $15,000; full dormer additions $40,000 to $90,000 over six to ten weeks. Shed buys the most space per dollar, gable buys architecture, eyebrow buys beauty at premium labor.
  3. Ask for the daylight simulation, not just the rendering. Specifically sDA (useful light) and ASE (glare and heat). A smaller well-oriented dormer beats a big poorly-oriented one, and no rendering will tell you that.
  4. Price the thermal load. New glazing changes the attic's heating and cooling demand. If the bid does not mention HVAC integration, insulation upgrades, or ventilation, it is incomplete.
  5. Expect 67 cents back on the dollar. Per NAR's 2025 report, attic conversions recover about 67 percent at resale. You are buying light, headroom, and a legal bedroom, not an investment.
  6. Run the simulations, then hire the architect anyway. What the optimizer hands you is the cheapest shed dormer that passes the math. Only the architect knows whether your street wants the eyebrow.

Two thousand dormers, ranked by light and money, and the machine picks the shed. It is not wrong. It is just answering a different question than the one the house is asking. What the house is asking is what it should look like for the next hundred years. On that question, the algorithm has no data, because beauty was never in the training set. Stand on the street at golden hour and look at the rooflines. The dormers that stay with you are never the optimal ones.
