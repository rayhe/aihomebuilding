Your West-Facing Glass Wall Costs $13,700. The Simulation Is Free.

By Elena Vasquez · September 23, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Come back at a quarter to six in August. That is when the rendering lies to you. In the rendering, the great room glows: fourteen feet of western glass, the sun dissolving into the hills, the whole house exhaling into the evening. In August, at 5:47, the room is 84 degrees, the shades are drawn against a white-hot rectangle, and the air conditioner has been running since three. You are paying to cool a view you cannot see, because the shade you bought to survive the glass stays closed from June through September, and the room behind it bakes anyway.

Nobody priced that moment, and window packages get decided the way most aesthetic decisions get decided on a custom home: the architect draws what photographs well, the owner approves what feels generous, and the energy model, if one exists at all, arrives months later as a compliance document rather than a design tool. Glass gets specified by the elevation, in service of the photograph, rather than by the sun path, which is strange because the sun path is the one consultant on the project who never sends an invoice and never gets ignored by physics.

Here is what makes it stranger. California already wrote the answer into law, in a table of the energy code that almost nobody reads at the design meeting where the glass gets approved.

What the code already knows

Open the 2019 California Building Energy Efficiency Standards, Table 150.1-A, the prescriptive path for low-rise residential, where two numbers sit side by side. Maximum total fenestration: 20 percent of conditioned floor area in every climate zone, a generous allowance that lets a thoughtfully designed house feel luminous without ever leaning on the one elevation the code distrusts. Maximum west-facing fenestration: 5 percent of conditioned floor area. That cap applies in climate zones 2, 4, and 6 through 15, which is to say everywhere summers require cooling (CEC Building Envelope compliance manual). Four times as much glass is permitted overall as is permitted on the west, which tells you everything about how the code's authors rank the elevations, even if the plan set in front of you ranks them by photograph. Even the code has opinions. Somebody in Sacramento did the math years ago, ran the simulations, argued it through committee, and the math said the west elevation is where glass goes to cost you money, which is why the cap sits there like a quiet warning nobody heeds.

This is not a California eccentricity: a 2025 simulation study of single-family homes in a hot climate found cooling energy more than doubling with facade orientation alone: roughly 30.3 kWh per square meter per year with the main facade facing east or west, against about 14.2 facing north or south (MDPI Buildings). A residential study out of China reached the same verdict in plainer language: east and west windows drive energy use hardest, and their window-to-wall ratio should be limited first (Journal of Engineering). Orientation is not a detail; it is the second-biggest decision in the envelope after insulation, and it gets made with a pencil, on a site plan, before anyone has modeled anything.

5% The maximum west-facing glass allowed under California's prescriptive energy code in cooling climate zones, measured against conditioned floor area, while total glass may reach 20%. On a 2,000-square-foot house, the code's answer is 100 square feet of western glass, and most glass-wall great rooms carry double that.

The math nobody runs

So let us run it on a specific house, because specific is the only kind of math that changes a decision: a 2,000-square-foot single-story home in Sacramento. Scheme A, the rendering: 200 square feet of west-facing glass, a proper glass wall, 10 percent of the floor area, twice the prescriptive cap. Scheme B, the tuned version: 100 square feet of west glass, at the cap, with the other 100 square feet moved to the south facade under a real overhang.

Start with what the glass costs to buy, because installed energy-efficient windows run $325 to $2,000 per unit, averaging around $400, with premium options at $40 to $90 per square foot installed (Angi, July 2026). Take the midpoint of the premium range, $65 per square foot. The extra 100 square feet of western glass prices at roughly $6,500, before anyone has discussed framing, flashing, or the structural header a fourteen-foot opening demands. That figure leans on replacement-market pricing, which runs hotter than new-construction packages, so treat $6,500 as the high side of honest. It is still real money for glass that faces the hottest sun of the day. Real money.

Then the cooling, where the numbers get sharper. That hot-climate simulation study tested shading configurations against the unshaded baseline. The best-shaded west and east facades cut cooling energy from 30.30 to 21.69 kWh per square meter per year: a 28 percent reduction achieved with nothing but shade design. Scale the 8.61 kWh per square meter difference across this house, 2,000 square feet is about 186 square meters, and the tuned scheme saves on the order of 1,600 kWh a year, year after year, for as long as the glass faces west. At thirty cents a kilowatt-hour, a plausible Sacramento-adjacent rate, that is $480 a year; at fifteen cents, $240. Call it $240 to $480 annually. Every year. For the life of the house, in exchange for glass the code was already telling you to shrink.

Add the install premium to fifteen years of cooling at the higher rate and the glass wall is a $13,700 decision. There is also a quieter cost: at 200 square feet of west glass, the prescriptive path is closed to you, which means the performance path, which means a full energy model and a consultant's invoice to prove the house complies at all. The code does not forbid the glass wall; it just makes you pay a professional to argue for it, which is the code's polite way of asking whether you have really thought this through.

What the optimizer would do

This is the part where the software earns its keep, and the software is not mysterious. Parametric toolsets like Ladybug and Honeybee, plugins that run on Rhino's Grasshopper platform, couple daylight simulation, solar radiation mapping, and EnergyPlus energy modeling in a single workflow; architects have used them to tune window placement, overhang depth, and shading for everything from Swedish schools to whole residential districts optimized by genetic algorithm (Archiroots survey of energy analysis tools). Feed one of these tools your massing model. An afternoon's computation returns the tradeoff surface: every orientation's glass priced in kilowatt-hours before a single window is ordered.

What it would tell you about this house is almost embarrassingly consistent with the old passive-solar textbooks, the ones that prescribed all of this decades before the software existed, back when the analysis was done with sun-angle diagrams and a sharp pencil. Move glass south, where a fixed overhang is genuinely intelligent: deep enough to shade the high summer sun, shallow enough to admit the low winter sun, a piece of geometry that works without motors or sensors. Researchers testing overhang depth against window-to-wall ratio found exactly this asymmetry: south overhangs pay, north overhangs do nothing at all, and east and west overhangs help only with deeper projections, because the morning and afternoon sun arrives low and slides underneath shallow shade (E3S Web of Conferences, 2023). The optimizer's verdict on the glass wall would be a shrug and a south elevation full of well-shaded glass doing the same luminous work for a fraction of the cooling load.

What the optimizer can't see

Now the part the software gets wrong, stated at full strength, because it matters more than the math: the view is the product. Nobody specifies a west glass wall because they cannot do arithmetic; they specify it because at 6:15 on a clear October evening the sun drops into the Pacific, or the hills, or the neighbor's oak, and for eleven minutes the room is the most beautiful place they own. Eleven minutes. A parametric optimizer with a pure energy objective would delete every west window in the state. A house with no west windows is not an optimized house. It is a lesser one, and any tool that cannot see that is a calculator wearing an architect's clothes.

This is my standing quarrel with the optimization mindset, and I will not soften it. These tools flatten design into a single-variable problem, and buildings are not single-variable problems. Daylight has documented effects on mood, sleep, and the feeling of a room that no kilowatt-hour ledger captures, which is exactly why the optimizer's answer, delete the glass, is the wrong answer even when its arithmetic is right. Sunset-facing glass is a luxury good, so price it like one. Put it where the view actually is, shade it properly, keep it small enough to honor, and spend the rest of the glass budget where the sun is polite. Tune the glass; do not ban it.

There is also a practical skepticism worth voicing before anyone buys a product with "AI facade" in the pitch deck. Those simulation engines are real and validated. The "AI" wrapped around them is mostly a friendlier interface on the same EnergyPlus calculations researchers have run for decades. Most residential architects do not work in Rhino and Grasshopper at all. The workflow that produces these answers lives in a software ecosystem the people designing your house may never open. And the performance path still requires a licensed energy consultant to sign the compliance documents. The tool does not replace the professional; it gives the professional something to do before the window order instead of after.

What we did not prove

Honest accounting, as always. The cooling math leans on a hot-climate simulation study, not a Sacramento field measurement; the orientation ordering holds across every study cited, but the absolute kilowatt-hours will run lower in milder zones and higher where summers are brutal. Site energy, what your meter actually records, would be smaller than the primary-energy figures quoted, since primary energy bakes in generation and transmission losses. The install premium uses replacement-market pricing, which overstates new-construction window costs, so $6,500 is the high side of the range by design. No vendor's facade-optimization marketing was independently tested for this article; the tools are described from their published capabilities and validation literature, not from a bake-off. And the fifteen-year figure assumes energy prices sit still, which they never do.

The call

If you are building, or drawing, or signing off on a window package this year, here is the sequence, in the order that actually changes outcomes.

  • Ask for the energy model before the window order, not after: if your architect cannot show you cooling load by orientation, you are buying glass blind. A Ladybug or ClimateStudio run on the massing model takes an afternoon, costs a fraction of one change order, and returns the one number nobody on the project currently has: what each elevation's glass costs in kilowatt-hours before a single window is ordered.
  • Hold west glass to the code's number: 5 percent of conditioned floor area in cooling climates is the prescriptive cap, and it is a good design target even where the performance path lets you exceed it. On a 2,000-square-foot house, that is 100 square feet, so spend it deliberately.
  • Move the glass budget south and shade it with geometry: a properly sized fixed overhang on the south facade admits winter sun and rejects summer sun with no moving parts, while north glass needs no overhang at all. West glass needs shade deeper than looks reasonable, because the afternoon sun comes in low.
  • Keep west glass only where the view earns it: one framed sunset is worth more than a whole elevation of glare. Price the remaining west glass as the luxury it is: smaller, shaded, and placed with intent, the way you would hang a painting you paid too much for and do not regret.

Buildings are the slowest technology we have, and the glass you approve this spring will face the 5:47 sun for fifty Augusts. An afternoon of simulation is the cheapest hour on the entire project. The code has been whispering the answer the whole time: face the sunset, by all means, but do the math first.
