Your Builder's 'Quick Question' Costs $1,080. The Answer Was Already in the Plans.

By Frank DeLuca · October 4, 2026

At 6:45 on a Tuesday morning, a superintendent I know stood in front of a framed wall with a phone in one hand and a plan set in the other, because the window schedule said one thing and the structural detail on sheet S-4 said another, while his framer was due at seven, the window rep was not answering, and the architect was three time zones into a vacation scheduled before anyone noticed the conflict, which is how these things always go.

He did what every super has done since the invention of the telephone: texted the architect a photo of both details, typed "which one wins," and waited for an answer that would decide whether the framer worked that day or stood around billing hours. That text was question number forty-something on a $640,000 custom home. Asked formally, through the proper channels, with the paperwork the contract requires, that same question costs about $1,080.

That number is not mine: Navigant Construction Forum analysts went through more than a million RFIs across 1,362 projects and put the average cost of processing a single request for information at $1,080 in labor alone (CMAA). Hughes and colleagues, extending the work in later conference papers, found the average project generates roughly 800 RFIs, at a global rate of 9.9 RFIs for every million dollars of construction, with responses taking an average of 12.2 days in the Americas (ASC 2016). On large projects, the research puts the total RFI burden at $800,000 or more in delays and costs. All of that is for one document: a formal written question from the contractor to the design team, asking what the drawings meant.

Twenty years of running projects has taught me that the RFI is the most honest document in construction, because it is the only one nobody writes to impress anyone: not the schedule, which is a wish, not the budget, which is a negotiation, but the RFI log, the diary of everything the drawings failed to say clearly, priced at a thousand dollars a confession.

Scale it down to a house

Nobody has published a proper study of RFIs on custom homes, so let me do the arithmetic the published data allows and mark clearly where the estimating starts. Take the global rate of 9.9 RFIs per million dollars of construction and apply it to a $650,000 custom home. That gives you about six and a half formal RFIs. At $1,080 apiece, the formal RFI tax on the project is roughly $7,000 in processing labor, and nobody budgets this line item. It hides inside the superintendent's salary and the project manager's evenings, which is why nobody notices it until you add it up.

The commercial data misses what every residential builder already knows: on a custom home, almost nothing travels as a formal RFI. Questions move by text message, by phone call, by a conversation in the driveway while the concrete truck idles, because nobody files RFI-047 about the beam pocket; the framer yells across the slab. His electrician does not submit a written request about the panel location, he calls the PM from the truck, and that is why a custom home does not generate six questions but sixty, fifty-four of which never touch a log.

Trunk Tools, the construction AI company, measured what one of those informal questions costs in pure lookup time: an average of 36 minutes every time a worker digs through project documents for an answer (GlobeNewswire, June 2026). Run that against a conservative count of fifty informal questions across a seven-month build with a dozen trades, and you get thirty hours of superintendent and PM time spent finding answers that were already written down somewhere. At a loaded cost of seventy-five dollars an hour, that is another $2,250 walking out the trailer door. The formal tax was $7,000. The informal kind rides shotgun.

The waiting is the expensive part

The labor cost, it turns out, is the smaller problem, because schedule cost is the one that ruins projects: a question unanswered is work unstarted, work unstarted on the critical path is the whole job standing still, and a job standing still is the most expensive thing a builder can own.

American contractors wait an average of eight days for a response to even a basic RFI, with the global average stretching to twelve (Navigant, via industry summary). Contracts usually allow the architect seven to fourteen days to respond. Everyone involved treats that window as normal, the way everyone treats a 45-minute dentist wait as normal, right up until you multiply it across a whole project and watch the calendar bleed. Census data puts the average new residential build at 7.2 months, roughly 155 working days, against which five critical-path RFIs at eight days apiece represent forty working days of serial drag, more than a quarter of the entire build calendar, and that assumes the answers come back right the first time, which anyone who has managed a project will tell you is a bold assumption.

Most RFIs do not sit on the critical path, and most get answered while other work proceeds because a good super sequences around them the way a good cook works around a slow oven. But the ones that land badly, the footing detail, the beam pocket, the thing the inspector flags on Friday afternoon, stop everything behind them cold. And roughly one in five RFIs never receives a formal answer at all, according to the Navigant analysis cited by SmartPM. Twenty percent of the questions that cost $1,080 to ask evaporate into voicemail. Gone. I have seen jobs where the most expensive RFI was the one nobody answered, because the crew guessed, guessed wrong, and the rework bill arrived two months later wearing a change order costume.

The machines that read drawings

This is the bottleneck the AI industry has finally decided to aim at, and to its credit it picked the right target, because most construction software spent the previous decade trying to replace 2D drawings with 3D models. It declined, for reasons of practicality and legal risk that Engineering News-Record's Bryan Gottlieb laid out plainly: on real jobsites, the documents remain the single source of project truth. So the new generation of construction AI reads the documents instead.

Trunk Tools spent four years building Cortex, an AI layer trained on construction drawings, markups, and the visual conventions of the trade, launched in June 2026 (ENR). Its TrunkRFI agent does three things my younger self would have paid real money for: it checks whether a question is already answered somewhere in the project documents or in a previous RFI response before it becomes a formal RFI at all; it auto-drafts the RFIs that are genuinely needed; and it converts a design-team response into a recommendation plus trade impact, schedule impact, and next-step tasks. Trunk Tools says its models detect hundreds of object types on drawings and can spot changes that fall outside the architect's revision clouds, citing one electrical issue it estimates carried about $60,000 in project impact. Separately, SmartPM now pulls RFI and submittal data from Autodesk Forma Build and maps each open RFI against the schedule activities it threatens, so the super can see which unanswered question is about to detonate which week.

Early enterprise evidence is genuinely interesting. Gilbane signed what Trunk Tools called a seven-figure agreement and rolled the agents across more than 200 projects (ENR, September 2025). In the first three months, Gilbane teams ran more than 2,000 submittals through the AI review and found that 72 percent were non-compliant, 25 percent partially compliant, and 3 percent fully compliant. Read that again. Fewer than one submittal in thirty arrived matching the spec. By flagging the bad ones early, Gilbane reported cutting resubmission cycle times nearly in half and preventing the RFIs, delays, and quality issues that non-compliant submittals breed. On the Baird Center project, the company's case study says workers asked the AI 246 questions across 37 working days, got correct answers 87 percent of the time as validated by the project team, saved 20 to 40 minutes per query, and avoided more than $100,000 a month in rework.

If you have run projects for twenty years, you read numbers like these the way you read a subcontractor's resume: interested, but waiting for the references. Which brings us to the part the vendors leave out of the brochure, the part with the asterisks and the footnotes nobody reads on the demo call.

What the brochure leaves out

Start with the accuracy number, because it cuts both ways in a manner the marketing copy never quite gets around to mentioning. Eighty-seven percent correct, on the vendor's own case study, validated by the project team that bought the software, means thirteen percent wrong. On a jobsite, a wrong answer does not sit politely in a chat window. It gets installed. A framer who acts on a wrong AI answer about a beam pocket has built rework at full labor cost, and the thirteen percent is not distributed evenly: the AI is most likely to be wrong on exactly the ambiguous, poorly documented details where a human would have picked up the phone. Nobody has published an independent audit of any of these accuracy claims.

Then there is the price, or rather the absence of one: Trunk Tools publishes no pricing, and Gilbane's deal was described only as seven figures. SmartPM and Autodesk Forma price for portfolios, not projects. This is the same pattern this publication has documented with Buildots and ALICE: the AI that solves the problem is built for contractors running $50-million portfolios, and the custom builder doing eight to twelve homes a year cannot buy it at any published price. Residential gets the keynote slides; enterprise gets the software.

There is also a document-quality problem nobody mentions: AI reads what is written, and residential construction documents are famously thin. Many custom homes go to permit with a plan set and no specifications at all, just notes on the drawings and the builder's memory of what the last house used. An AI trained with the largest general contractors, on commercial document sets with thousand-page spec books, arrives at a residential job the way a sommelier arrives at a barbecue: technically overqualified, practically underfed. Questions a residential super asks are often unanswerable from the documents because the documents never contained the answer. That is not a lookup problem. That is a design problem.

And the underlying research is old. That $1,080 figure and the response-time data come from Hughes and colleagues, published in 2013, built on commercial and infrastructure projects, thirteen years ago, which in construction-technology years is roughly a geological era. Thirteen years is a long time in an industry that now runs on Procore and group text, and which way the bias runs is unknowable: formal RFI discipline may have improved, or the informal question load may have grown faster than anyone measured. I used the numbers because they are the best available, not because they are fresh.

Objections, at full strength

Now the case for the defense, because the RFI has one and it deserves to be heard. An RFI is not waste. It is the paper trail that keeps everyone honest, and in twenty years I have never seen a project dispute resolved in favor of the party with the thinner documentation.

A formal RFI with a written architect response is a legal document, executed under the contract machinery for reporting errors and interpreting requirements, establishing what was asked, what was answered, and when, in language both sides’ lawyers can read. It establishes what was asked, what was answered, and when, under the machinery of AIA A201, which obligates the contractor to report errors and omissions in the documents (section 3.2.2) and obligates the architect to interpret the requirements (section 4.2.12). A texted photo answered with "use detail 4" is a future deposition exhibit for both sides, and I have watched lawyers read text threads aloud in conference rooms with the delight of archaeologists finding a new tomb. When the industry talks about killing RFIs with AI, it is worth asking what exactly is being killed: the redundant question already answered on sheet A-6, which deserves to die, or the documented question-and-answer record, which does not.

A deeper objection is about blame. That "preventable RFI" framing points the finger at the asker, but the asker is usually the last person at fault. If the flashing detail on sheet S-4 agreed with the window schedule, nobody would have texted the architect at 6:45 in the morning. RFIs are a symptom and bad drawings are the disease: an AI that answers questions faster does nothing for the detail that was never drawn. There is a real risk here of optimizing the wrong thing: making it cheaper to ask questions instead of making it unnecessary to ask them.

Finally, the liability question is genuinely unanswered: if a superintendent relies on an AI-drafted RFI that misstates the issue, or acts on an AI answer that turns out wrong, no standard contract allocates that failure. AIA documents were written before any of this existed, and the insurance market has moved in the opposite direction: standardized AI exclusions for commercial general liability policies took effect in January 2026. An industry that cannot insure the AI's mistakes should think carefully about how much authority it gives the AI's answers. A super who picks up the phone and asks the architect is doing quality assurance. Automate the lookup all you want. The question itself is the job.

What this analysis does not prove

The $9,250 question tax is a model, not a measurement. That $7,000 formal component scales the Navigant global rate (9.9 RFIs per million dollars, commercial projects, 2013 data) onto a $650,000 custom home; no published study counts RFIs on residential projects specifically, and residential practice varies enormously by builder, region, and contract type. Fifty informal questions is my estimate, informed by two decades of watching supers work, not a surveyed figure. Trunk Tools' 36-minute lookup figure is a measured average on commercial projects using its software, not a general property of questions.

Trunk Tools' accuracy (87 percent), rework avoidance ($100,000 per month), and Gilbane's cycle-time improvement (nearly 50 percent) are vendor-reported figures with no independent audit. That $60,000 unflagged-revision anecdote is a single vendor-supplied example. No public pricing exists for Trunk Tools, SmartPM's RFI agent, or the Forma AI features, so the ROI case for small builders cannot be closed with published numbers. Yet. What to do Monday morning

If you run residential projects in the half-million to two-million range, you are paying the question tax whether you log it or not, so start logging it: one shared RFI log, every question in it, including the texted ones, with dates asked and dates answered. What gets measured gets answered, and what gets answered in writing does not become a dispute in month nine.

A free version exists, and it has three parts. First, one complete current plan set in the trailer, not three revisions deep in the truck; half of all RFIs I have ever seen were answered on a sheet the asker had not looked at. Second, a fifteen-minute document review before each new trade mobilizes, walking the relevant sheets with the foreman while the questions are still cheap. Third, a rule that no question gets asked twice: the answer goes in the log with the sheet number, and the next person with the same question gets pointed at the log.

The math justifies the discipline. Preventing ten RFIs saves roughly $10,800 in processing labor at the Navigant rate, and that is the small money. One prevented critical-path RFI, the eight-day kind, is worth more than all the labor savings combined, because it is the difference between handing over keys in October and handing them over in December.

If you evaluate an AI document assistant, demo it on your messiest plan set, not the vendor's showcase set. Ask who owns a wrong answer, contractually, in writing. Ask what it does when there are no specifications, because on most custom homes there are none. And get pricing in writing before the pilot, because "enterprise" is a polite way of saying "you cannot afford it" until proven otherwise.

If you are having a home built, ask your builder one question at the first meeting: how are questions tracked on my project? "We just call the architect" means your schedule lives in someone's voicemail. A builder with a written RFI log is a builder whose delays have names and dates, and in this business, that is most of what accountability looks like.

That superintendent got his answer at 7:20, by the way. Detail 4 won, and framing started at seven-thirty. Somewhere in a trailer, nobody wrote any of it down, and the next house with the same detail will ask the same question all over again. That is the whole problem, and no software has ever fixed a habit.
