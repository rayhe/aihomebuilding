Your Geotech Report Had Three Foundation Options. You Only Heard About the Expensive One.

By Frank DeLuca · September 5, 2026

This is how the schedule usually goes. On a Tuesday, the boring rig shows up, drills two ten-foot holes, and sends a bag of soil cores to a lab; that is week three. Week six brings the report: forty pages, three foundation options, and a recommendations section the engineer will actually read in about ten minutes. By week seven the concrete is scheduled, the rebar sub is booked, and the middle option has exactly zero minutes of engineering time behind it.

Nobody decided to skip the middle option; that is the thing about schedules, which kill options without anyone signing the death warrant.

A geotechnical study for a single-family home costs between $1,500 and $3,500, covering borings, lab analysis, and the engineering report with foundation recommendations (EngineerFix; Angi). Read the whole document and you will find something most owners never learn: the report almost always lists two or three acceptable foundations, ranked by conservatism. A Houston geotechnical work plan for residential structures lays out the ladder explicitly, from structural slabs with piers (lowest risk) down through post-tension slabs to conventionally reinforced slabs, with the candid note that repair costs dwarf the cost of the study itself (Geotech Engineering and Testing). Houston's own procurement documents use the same ranking: structural slab with piers or helical piles at the low-risk end, floating stiffened slabs at the cheap end (City of Houston).

Here is where the money quietly moves, and the numbers are not subtle. Foundations account for 11 percent of residential construction costs, according to the National Association of Home Builders' cost survey, which works out to roughly $29,700 of a $450,000 home's construction budget (NAHB). On expansive clay, the spread between the report's options is enormous. In Texas, a post-tension slab runs $5.50 to $8.00 per square foot; a structural slab on drilled piers adds 25 to 35 piers at $500 to $1,000 apiece, or $12,500 to $35,000 on top of the slab (CountBricks; US Patriot Steel). One Austin builder's 2026 field data shows real foundation bids on ordinary homes ranging from $25,000 all the way to a $130,000 stem-wall reveal, with the explicit advice to get a site-specific geotech report before buying the lot (The First Million).

Put midpoints on a typical 2,000-square-foot Texas home and the arithmetic gets uncomfortable. Post-tension slab runs about $13,500, while the structural slab with piers lands near $37,250. That middle option, a slab on grade with imported select fill that one Colorado structural firm calls the cost-competitive alternative on expansive soils, lands around $21,000 (EVstudio). Between the conservative pick and the middle option, the gap is roughly $16,000. All three options lived in a report that cost $3,500 at most, so do the division: that study pays for itself four times over the moment someone prices option two.

So why does option two never get priced? Because the report arrives in week six and the slab pours in week seven. Behind the desk sits an engineer holding the professional stamp and carrying the liability, and the most conservative option is the one that cannot get them sued. Modeling the middle option properly takes days the schedule does not have. None of this is a conspiracy; it is what happens when a liability-bearing decision meets a schedule with no slack. What kills the middle option is the calendar, not the clay.

This is, finally, a problem AI can actually touch: not the foundation, but the reading.

Aldoa AI, used by more than 100 geotechnical and materials-testing organizations, turns raw field data into boring logs, compaction reports, and calculation sheets up to ten times faster than manual workflows, by its own vendor-stated claim (Aldoa AI). RSLog added an AI OCR feature that converts paper borehole log PDFs, even handwritten ones, into searchable database records with SPT values, strata descriptions, and sample depths assigned automatically (Geoengineer.org). Researchers at the University of Texas at Austin found that large language models can read DIGGS files, the industry's standard geotechnical data format, and interpret CPT and SPT data correctly, though the same study found the models still need step-by-step guidance to do bearing-capacity math without supervision (T&UC Magazine). Oak Ridge National Laboratory's FLAT tool uses machine learning on 360-degree laser scans to find uneven spots on fresh slabs, cutting foundation measurement time by more than 90 percent at two housing developments (Newswise).

Notice what is missing from that list, because the pattern is instructive. Every tool reads faster, and none of them prices the middle option or holds the stamp. Liability still lives with a licensed engineer, and no software company is volunteering to absorb it. AI has read the geotech report, but it has not redesigned your foundation, and it will not be in the courtroom if the slab moves.

Now the counterargument, stated at full strength, because it deserves it. Set the metaphor aside: the conservative foundation is not a tax. It is insurance, and the actuarial case is brutal. Expansive soils have disturbed one in four residences in the continental United States, and the American Society of Civil Engineers puts annual building and infrastructure damage above $15 billion, more than twice the destruction of all other natural disasters combined (HUD Cityscape; Scientific Reports). One $40,000 foundation repair erases the $16,000 savings and then some. Dallas once listed 120 foundation repair companies in the phonebook against 15 firms doing residential foundation design, which tells you which side of this bet pays better. That engineer is not padding the bid; they are pricing the probability that your house becomes one of the one-in-four, and their license is the collateral.

Fair. But insurance you cannot comparison-shop is not insurance; it is a surcharge.

If you are building, here is the playbook. First, order the geotech study before plan review is finished, not after. Two borings and a lab report take two to four weeks, and the study is the one item on the critical path that nobody manages because it happens off-site. Second, when the engineer recommends the most expensive of the report's options, ask for the middle option priced in writing. It is in the report, though most owners never see it. Third, if your lot sits on USDA-mapped moderate shrink-swell soil, say so at the first structural meeting. It reframes the conversation from worst case to which case. Fourth, get per-option pricing on the record and do not sign the foundation authorization until all three numbers are on the page: no comparison, no signature.

That $16,000 did not disappear into the clay. It disappeared into a week of schedule pressure and a paragraph nobody read, so read the paragraph.

Limitations: The $16,000 conservatism-tax figure is a bounding exercise built from contractor-reported cost ranges, not a controlled sample; it illustrates the size of the spread, not a measured outcome. Foundation bids vary widely by market, and Austin's 2026 ranges do not transfer to Houston or Oklahoma without adjustment. No published case study demonstrates an AI-read geotech report changing a foundation type selection on a specific home; the mechanism is plausible but unverified at the individual-project level. Aldoa's "ten times faster" claim is vendor-stated and has no independent verification. At UT Austin, researchers found LLMs need guided prompting for bearing-capacity calculations, so AI interpretation of boring data remains a reading aid, not engineering analysis. Expansive-soil damage statistics ($15 billion, one in four homes) are national aggregates that overstate risk on stable soils and understate it on severe clays like Houston's Beaumont formation.
