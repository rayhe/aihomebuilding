By Marcus Washington · August 4, 2026

At the Savannah River Site in Aiken, South Carolina, workers dismantling Cold War-era nuclear waste facilities strap SlateSafety armbands to their forearms every morning. Each device monitors heart rate, tracks skin temperature, and calculates something called heart rate limit value, a ratio of current cardiac output against the maximum their body can sustain. If that ratio exceeds 100 percent for more than five minutes, a supervisor receives an alert on a web dashboard, the worker stops, cools down, and returns only when the armband clears them.

To the U.S. Department of Energy, this is basic workplace safety in 2026.

Three states away, a four-person framing crew is sheathing a two-story house outside Dallas in July heat that settled at 109 degrees by noon. Their heat safety protocol is a five-gallon water cooler on the tailgate of a pickup truck and a foreman who grew up in Monterrey and has spent twenty-three summers building houses in Texas. He watches his crew the way his father watched his. When somebody looks wrong, he tells them to sit down.

That instinct has kept people alive. It has also missed people who died.

The Technology Already Exists

At least three commercial systems can predict dangerous heat strain in a worker's body before symptoms appear. Kenzen, founded in 2014, sells a wearable armband that monitors heart rate, physical activity, skin temperature, and ambient conditions. Its algorithms fuse that data to predict core body temperature, the metric that determines whether someone lives or dies when heat stroke sets in, and then the device vibrates against the worker's arm while an app pings their phone and a supervisor dashboard lights up, all of it happening before the worker feels anything wrong.

SlateSafety, the system on the nuclear workers' arms in South Carolina, does something similar with heart rate limit value. At a Cardinal Glass factory in Wisconsin, where four masons maintain a furnace that reaches 3,000 degrees Fahrenheit, the company paid $5,000 for five armbands, software, and air-monitoring hardware. One employee's two heat-related emergency room visits had already cost the company $15,000, which means the sensor suite paid for itself before the second month.

BeeInventor, a Hong Kong-based construction technology company, has built AI heat-stroke prediction directly into a smart helmet. Built-in sensors measure heart rate and estimate core body temperature while the worker is already wearing required PPE. Researchers at the Illinois Institute of Technology validated the approach, demonstrating that machine learning models fed sensor data can predict heat stress events before clinical onset.

These are not prototypes but commercial products with paying customers on active jobsites across multiple continents.

Who Wears Them and Who Does Not

A clear pattern emerges. Kenzen's customers are "large industrial conglomerates" in mining, oil and gas, power generation, and renewable energy. DOE deploys SlateSafety on federally funded nuclear remediation sites. Perrigo, a consumer products company, outfitted more than 100 employees at its infant formula plants with the armbands. Cardinal Glass protects four masons who tend a single furnace.

What connects these deployments is scale: large employers with dedicated safety departments, structured compliance budgets, and workers' compensation data sophisticated enough to calculate the ROI on a $200-to-$400 wearable device, because at that scale the math is obvious. One heat-related fatality generates six-figure OSHA fines, seven-figure litigation exposure, and a workers' compensation claim that reprices the employer's insurance for a decade.

Residential construction operates in a different universe where a framing crew is three to eight workers, often subcontracted through a general contractor and frequently paid as 1099 labor. A GC's margin on a tract home is thin enough that a $1,000-per-worker safety technology investment, even one that pays for itself in prevented claims, requires a budget line item that does not exist, and there is no safety department or dedicated compliance officer standing between a crew lead's gut instinct and a 911 call.

Meanwhile, the National Council for Occupational Safety and Health named D.R. Horton, the largest homebuilder in the United States, to its 2026 Dirty Dozen list, identifying it as a company that "put workers' lives at risk through unsafe practices, inadequate protections, and systemic neglect." Nearly 28,000 workplace injuries per year are linked to hot weather. More than 40 percent of the workers who die from heat on the job are building things.

No Federal Standard. Never Has Been.

There is no federal heat safety standard for construction workers in the United States and there has never been one.

OSHA proposed a rule in August 2024 that would have required employers to develop heat illness prevention plans, monitor conditions, provide mandatory rest breaks at a heat index of 80 degrees, and implement acclimatization protocols for new and returning workers. Public comments closed in January 2025, an extended post-hearing comment period closed in October 2025, and no further action has been taken since, with not a single entry in the most recent federal regulatory agenda including a target date for final action, and finalization under the current administration appearing unlikely.

What exists instead is a revised National Emphasis Program, issued April 10, 2026 and effective through 2031, that targets 55 high-risk industries, construction among them. It is an enforcement prioritization framework, not a binding standard. OSHA heat inspections have jumped from roughly 200 per year to approximately 2,400. That sounds aggressive until you consider the denominator: an estimated 11.4 million construction workers in the United States.

Congress has seen the Asunción Valdivia Heat Illness and Fatality Prevention Act, named for a California farmworker who died from heat stroke in 2004, though its passage remains uncertain.

Heat Stroke Has a Mortality Rate. It Also Has a Timer.

One medical fact underlies this entire gap, a number that should be printed on every hard hat and taped to every cooler: heat stroke kills 80 percent of the time when treatment is delayed. With early diagnosis and immediate active cooling, that mortality rate drops to 10 percent. Between those two numbers sits a window measured in minutes, not hours, almost entirely determined by whether someone recognizes what is happening before the worker's own body stops sending intelligible signals.

AI-powered wearables address exactly that gap by measuring the precursors that a human observer cannot see: the ratio of cardiac output to sustainable maximum, the trajectory of skin temperature against ambient conditions, the acceleration of physiological strain over a work period. A foreman watching his crew can spot a stumble, slurred speech, confusion, but by then the timer has already started, and an armband catches the inflection point twenty minutes earlier, when intervention is rest and shade instead of an ambulance and a hospital and a phone call to a family.

A Gap That Is Measurable and Widening

Disconnection between technology adoption in commercial and residential construction is measurable and widening. ServiceTitan's 2026 State of AI in the Trades report found that AI adoption among commercial contractors more than doubled in a single year, with 38 percent now reporting measurable business impact, up from 17 percent. Residential contractors lag far behind, and a DEWALT study published in April 2026 found that 90 percent of construction professionals believe AI will become indispensable within five years while only eight percent currently use it in their daily work, a gap between aspiration and practice that concentrates at exactly the scale where workers are most exposed.

Residential construction lost 43,600 jobs over the past twelve months, the eleventh consecutive annual decline. NAHB estimates the economic damage at $10.8 billion per year, and the workers who remain are older, more experienced, and performing more physical labor per person than at any point in the industry's recent history, which means they most need the sensors and are least likely to have them.

Privacy Is a Real Concern

Not everyone wants their boss watching their heart rate in real time. Researchers publishing in the American Journal of Industrial Medicine in 2026 explicitly recommended that raw physiological data from heat wearables should remain exclusively accessible to individual workers, warning that employer access could enable discriminatory employment practices. Older workers, workers with higher BMI, workers taking certain medications all run hotter. A dashboard that flags those workers disproportionately could become a tool for constructive termination dressed up as safety compliance.

That concern is legitimate and the engineering solutions are known: Kenzen's alert system sends a binary signal to supervisors indicating this worker needs a break or this worker is clear, while the underlying biometric data stays with the worker. Whether residential contractors operating without HR departments or privacy policies would maintain that boundary is an open question, because on a five-person crew where the foreman is also the employer, the distinction between "your sensor says you need rest" and "your sensor says you're slower than everyone else" can vanish in a single sentence.

What This Means If You Are Building a House

If you are hiring a crew this summer, ask your general contractor whether the framing, roofing, and concrete subcontractors use any form of physiological heat monitoring, and expect the answer to be no. Ask what their heat illness prevention plan looks like, because many will describe water, shade, and experience, the same protocol the industry relied on in 1985.

If you are a residential builder running five to fifteen workers through a Texas, Arizona, or Florida summer, the math on a $1,000-per-worker sensor investment is not complicated: one workers' compensation claim for a heat-related ER visit will cost you more, and one fatality will cost you everything. Technology to prevent it is commercial, proven on sites handling radioactive waste, and available on a subscription basis that requires no upfront capital commitment beyond the device itself.

Kenzen sells per-worker-per-month, SlateSafety has demonstrated an all-in deployment cost of $1,000 per worker at Cardinal Glass, wearable device hardware runs $200 to $400 per unit across the market, and cloud-based monitoring software subscriptions run $0.50 to $1.50 per worker per month.

Nuclear waste cleanup workers in South Carolina are wearing them right now, and their employer considers it obvious, so ask yours why they do not.

Limitations & Gaps

  • Kenzen subscription pricing is not publicly available, and device costs of $200-$400 per unit and software subscriptions of $0.50-$1.50/month are drawn from a market research report on warehouse heat monitoring, not construction-specific deployments, so actual residential construction pricing may differ.
  • Accuracy of wearable core temperature estimation in dynamic outdoor construction environments remains less validated than in controlled industrial settings. A 2022 research review noted that age, gender, and humidity variability make reliable prediction challenging outside laboratory conditions.
  • No published data quantifies wearable heat monitor adoption rates specifically in residential construction. Gaps described here are inferred from deployment patterns (industrial clients only), residential industry structure (small crews, thin margins, no safety departments), and interviews in trade publications.
  • State-level heat regulations are not accounted for here; California, Oregon, Washington, and several other states have adopted heat rules that exceed the stalled federal proposal, potentially narrowing the gap in those jurisdictions.
  • OSHA fatality data does not distinguish between residential and commercial construction at the NAICS 4-digit level, so construction's 36.8% share of heat deaths includes all construction sectors.
