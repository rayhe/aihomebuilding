Embers Ignited 90% of the Homes That Burned. Your Vents Are Holding the Door Open.

By Elena Vasquez · October 9, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

In the 2018 Woolsey Fire, a single ember landed on a small door of the ADU behind Nate Siggard's family home. Ten minutes later the ADU was fully engulfed. His brother flagged down a passing fire truck and the main house survived, a lesson that did not leave him.

Seven years later, Siggard launched WyldSafe, a free app that studies a video walkaround of your home's exterior and tells you where the fire will get in. Its computer vision reads each frame the way a building scientist would, cataloging combustible vegetation against the siding, a vulnerable roof material, a wood fence bolted to the garage, an unscreened vent, and then it prices each fix and ranks them by urgency. It is, in a sense, the first app that looks at a house the way fire does.

Fire, it turns out, rarely comes through the front. As many as 90 percent of homes destroyed in wildfires were first ignited by embers or by fires those embers started, not by the main fire front. Researchers at the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety proved the split physically: they cast embers at a duplex in a South Carolina test chamber, one half built to resist embers, the other built the ordinary way, and only the ordinary half ignited, because embers ride the wind for miles ahead of the flames and probe every opening they find. To an ember, your house is a face full of small open mouths. Embers do not knock.

Architecture is the art of the opening, and every vent the building code requires for ventilation is a hole the fire reads as an invitation. A typical 2,000-square-foot house has a dozen or more of them: soffit, gable, foundation. Usually those holes wear quarter-inch mesh screens, a size chosen for airflow, not warfare. An ember smaller than the mesh sails straight through, settles onto forty years of attic dust and insulation, and waits. San Rafael's fire department puts it plainly: vents are one of the easiest ways for embers to get inside a home, which makes them one of the most effective things to fix.

Run the numbers and the hierarchy of interventions rearranges itself. Sixteen vents at roughly $35 each for baffle-style ember-resistant hardware, the kind that passes the ASTM E2886 ember test and now retails between $25 and $50 per vent depending on style, comes to about $560 in parts plus a few hundred for installation. Call it a thousand dollars to seal the pathway behind the great majority of ignitions. Metal gutter guards for the roofline run roughly $900 to $1,500 installed, and matter because gutters packed with dry leaves are among the most frequent ignition points IBHS has measured, while vinyl gutters make the geometry worse by melting under ember exposure and dumping burning debris directly onto the siding below. Separating the wood fence where it touches the house costs $800 to $2,000. Clearing the first five feet around the structure, now legally Zone 0, costs a few hundred to $1,500. A Class A roof, the intervention everyone leads with, starts at $15,000.

Notice the inversion: the Safe Homes grant program that California launched in January prioritizes replacement roofs and the five-foot perimeter, while the embers prioritize the vents.

California's 2025 WUI Code is catching up. Effective January 1, 2026, it consolidates the old Chapter 7A into a dedicated wildland-urban interface code with ignition-resistant standards for roofing, siding, and vents, and bans cedar shakes outright. Colorado adopted its first statewide wildfire resiliency code the same year. Architects now draw details that the fire reviews.

The honest objections deserve their full weight. A hardened house in an unhardened neighborhood can still burn when the conflagration arrives; IBHS's duplex saved only the treated half, and neighborhoods are not built in halves. Three million dollars in Safe Homes seed funding against roughly two million at-risk California structures is a rounding error, and an insurer can still non-renew you after you spend the money, a fact the program's own eligibility rules, which demand an admitted-carrier or FAIR Plan policy, quietly acknowledge. No phone camera sees behind a vent screen, so WyldSafe grades the visible facade while the actual ignition happens in the attic, on fuel it cannot photograph, and the company's accuracy claims have no published third-party validation. The most powerful intervention remains free: clean the gutters, move the firewood, clear the dead leaves, which is where the ladder starts. Everything after is probability purchased by the dollar.

What this analysis did not prove, stated plainly. These vent counts and labor figures assume a typical 2,000-square-foot California home and Bay Area labor; count your own vents before budgeting. The 90 percent figure is IBHS's 2019 finding, widely cited but not a per-pathway breakdown, since nobody has published what share of ember ignitions enter through vents versus gutters versus roofs, and the retrofit math is California-anchored rather than national. Safe Homes grant amounts were still undetermined in early 2026 reporting, and the application portal was only a target, with the department's own spokesman describing a March goal that had not yet produced a live application when the reporting closed.

What to do this month: walk your house and count the vents. A pencil eraser fits through quarter-inch mesh; if it does, the mesh is not ember-resistant. One-eighth inch is the minimum the fire services accept, and baffle or multi-layer ASTM E2886-tested hardware is the standard. In San Rafael's high fire zones the city will pay a contractor to screen your existing vents for free. If you are drawing plans now, specify the vents on the drawings: the delta over standard mesh is a rounding error at plan stage and a four-figure retrofit later. Run the free assessment, and take its grades the way you would take a contractor's bid, with a second opinion.

The house is a breathing thing, and every breath it takes through those small dark openings is a negotiation with the outside world. For a century architects drew the breath, in section and in detail, without ever designing for the exhale, because the fire was always assumed to be someone else's problem until it wasn't. The embers taught the lesson first. Now the code, the grants, and a phone camera have finally started listening to it.
