The Invoice Looked Exactly Right. The Bank Account Belonged to a Stranger.

By Frank DeLuca · September 10, 2026

A homeowner in Tasmania hired a local construction company to renovate her house. The emails looked right, and so did the invoice, with every line item matching the original quote down to the letterhead.

Only one thing was wrong: the bank account number belonged to a stranger.

She wired $120,000, and by the time anyone noticed the money was missing, it had been drained through accounts the police could not reach. Gone. Australia's Federal Police published the case in a construction-industry warning, and their summary was brutally simple: because she waited to report it, the money was not recoverable.

What happened to her has a name in the fraud world: invoice diversion, a flavor of business email compromise. Criminals insert themselves into a payment thread, swap the bank details on a pixel-perfect invoice, and wait. And construction, it turns out, is their favorite hunting ground.

Construction Is the Softest Target in the Economy

In September 2025 alone, British victims of invoice fraud lost £3,908,086 across 83 reported cases, according to the UK fraud reporting center. That works out to roughly £47,000 per case. Invoice fraud accounted for 85 percent of all payment diversion fraud losses that month. When analysts broke down which industries got hit hardest in 2024 and 2025, construction and manufacturing topped the list at 25 percent of cases, more than any other sector pair. Bad enough, in fact, that the National Crime Agency teamed up with the National Federation of Builders on an industry-wide warning campaign aimed at accounts payable staff.

Assistant Commissioner Richard Chin of the AFP Cyber Command described the exposure plainly: “The construction sector, with its high-value transactions and complex subcontracting chains, has become an attractive target for organised cybercrime groups operating both domestically and offshore.” Small, family-run firms are the softest targets of all. They push high-value payments through ordinary email, they have no dedicated finance team, and everyone on the job is too busy to scrutinize the one email that actually matters, which is exactly why criminals who have done their reconnaissance prefer them to larger firms with separation of duties and a controller who asks uncomfortable questions.

Per the Association of Certified Fraud Examiners, the median construction fraud loss is $250,000, among the top five of any industry, and schemes run a median of 12 months before detection, which is a full year of money walking out the door before anyone thinks to look. Twelve months.

America's Numbers Are Uglier

The FBI’s Internet Crime Complaint Center reported that real estate fraud losses hit $275.1 million in 2025 across 12,368 complaints, a 59 percent jump from the year before. Business email compromise overall cost victims $3.04 billion. More than 22,000 complaints referenced AI, with losses topping $893 million. FBI analysts describe the mechanism directly: “Chat generators can quickly create official-sounding emails mimicking a company’s CEO or other officials. These emails can contain phishing links or directions to wire funds. Voice cloning can also be used to request wire payment.”

Here is a calculation nobody in the coverage bothered to do. Divide the FBI’s real estate fraud losses by its complaint counts, year by year, and the per-victim trend tells a story the totals hide.

Complaint volume in 2025 even exceeded the 2022 peak count, though total dollars did not. Translation: more victims than ever, and each one losing more than the year before. That pattern is exactly what you would expect if every attempt is getting more convincing, which is what the FBI says AI is doing to these schemes.

For scale, the average business email compromise complaint in 2025 cost $122,744 ($3.04 billion over 24,768 complaints), roughly five and a half times the real estate average, which makes real estate fraud the high-volume, mid-ticket version of a crime whose enterprise variant routinely clears six figures per victim.

How the Scam Actually Works

Understanding the mechanics matters, because the defense is procedural. Reconnaissance comes first: malware from a clicked link captures email logins, after which criminals watch real threads for weeks, sometimes months, while hidden mailbox rules automatically forward or delete any message containing words like invoice, purchase, or payment, until they know your vendors, your cadence, and your tone.

Then comes the switch, when a spoofed domain one letter off (lawfirm.com versus lawflrm.com), or the genuinely compromised account itself, sends the invoice: an exact replica of the legitimate one, except the payment details now route to a criminal-controlled account, sometimes followed by a voice-cloned “vendor” calling to confirm the new details.

Finally comes the drain, as funds hit a domestic account and scatter, sometimes offshore, within hours. Per the AFP, victims typically realize what happened only after the money has already moved through multiple international accounts. In Queensland, investigators traced one such operation to offshore syndicates that had researched a construction company’s client relationships in detail before impersonating it, with total losses exceeding $1 million.

Same AI, Pointed the Other Way

Now that same technology is sold as the shield. CertifID, which verifies identities and bank accounts for real estate wires, says it has protected more than 1.4 million transactions, blocked $283 million in attempted fraud, and recovered $118 million working with the US Secret Service. Its models score roughly 150 fraud markers per transaction. On the builder side, accounts-payable platforms have added AI anomaly detection: Medius claims its monitoring cuts payment fraud and error rates by 90 to 95 percent, and Routable shipped an AI agent in early 2025 that scans invoices for anomalies, duplicates, and data-entry errors before anything gets paid. Ramp’s fraud agent specifically flags unexpected banking-detail changes, which is precisely the invoice-diversion tell.

None of those vendor claims has been independently audited, so treat them as marketing until proven otherwise, but the direction is right: machines are better than tired humans at noticing that a vendor’s bank details changed for the first time in six years, or that an invoice number falls outside the sequence a supplier has used for a decade.

What the Software Vendors Won't Tell You

And yet the most effective defense in this entire story cost nothing. In New South Wales, a construction company paid $41,800 on spoofed supplier invoices, then texted the remittance to the supplier’s known mobile number, learned the details were wrong, and reported it immediately, recovering the full amount. The AFP's official guidance does not lead with software. It leads with this: “No matter how legitimate a request may appear, always confirm payment instructions through a secondary communication channel, such as a trusted contact you have previously engaged with.”

A written rule, bank details never change by email, enforced by a three-person office, stops nearly every variant of this scam. Software vendors have every incentive to make the threat sound like it requires their product, but it does not: it requires a phone call.

There is one hole in the callback defense, and it is worth naming. If the criminals have cloned your vendor’s voice, a phone confirmation fails too. Which is why the callback must go to a number you already had in your contacts, never the number printed on the suspect invoice. That Tasmania invoice was perfect, which means the phone number printed on it would have been perfect too.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you run a building company, or you are an owner-builder managing your own subs, here is a protocol that costs nothing, takes about four minutes per payment, and happens to be the only control in this article with a documented case of recovering every dollar at stake.

One. Write the rule down: no bank detail changes accepted by email. Not from the CEO, not from the vendor, not from anyone. Verbal confirmation only, documented in the job file.

Two. Confirm through a channel you already trust: call the number in your phone, not the number on the invoice, or text the mobile you have used for two years. A text message saved that company, not a software license.

Three. Split the duties so that one person approves a new payee while a different person releases the wire. Two sets of eyes is the oldest control in accounting, and it still works.

Four. Put a 48-hour hold on first-time payments to new accounts. Urgency is the criminal’s best tool; a cooling period is yours. No legitimate sub has ever gone bankrupt because a first payment took two extra days.

Five. Report within hours, not days: in 2023, the FBI’s Recovery Asset Team froze $538 million of $758 million in kill-chain cases, a 71 percent hold rate, but only when victims reported fast enough for the money to still be sitting in the first account. One victim waited and the other did not, and that was the entire difference.

The expected value of the protocol is zero dollars, against an expected cost of skipping it that runs to £47,000 per incident in the UK data, $120,000 in the Tasmania case, and a year of explaining to your sub why you paid somebody else their money and still owe them theirs.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The IC3 figures are US-only reported losses, and the FBI’s own reporting-rate estimates (12 to 15 percent historically) mean every number here is a floor, possibly a low one. Sector-specific invoice data comes from the UK and Australia; the United States publishes no construction-specific invoice fraud dataset, so the sector ranking may not transfer cleanly. Vendor claims from Medius, CertifID, Routable, and Ramp are self-reported and have not been independently audited. And no public dataset isolates residential homebuilding from commercial construction, so the exposure of a custom home builder versus a commercial GC is inferred, not measured.

Twenty years of watching jobs go sideways teaches you to respect the boring disasters. Foundations do not fail because of dramatic, unforeseeable events. They fail because somebody skipped the compaction test on a Friday afternoon. Invoice diversion is the compaction test of the back office. The invoice was perfect. The account was not. One phone call, to a number you already had, is the entire difference between the New South Wales ending and the Tasmania ending.
