The $1.1 Billion Settlement Is Closed. The Poison Drywall Is Still for Sale.

By Marcus Washington · September 25, 2026
← Back to articles

In the spring of 2006, a drywall crew could hang an entire house outside New Orleans in a single day. Twelve-foot sheets went up at a sprint, screwed off every twelve inches, mudded and taped by Friday. Some of the bundles came off the truck with Chinese characters stenciled on the back paper. Nobody on the crew asked about it, because work was work, the city was rebuilding, and the money was the best it had been in years.

Nobody asked what was in the walls.

Within two years, some of those same houses started to smell like rotten eggs. Air conditioner coils that should have lasted a decade died in months. Silverware tarnished inside drawers while mirrors went black around the edges. Copper wiring turned the color of a penny left in a fountain, then worse. Families who had rebuilt after Katrina watched their new walls eat their new appliances, and then they watched the builders go bankrupt.

The walls were eating the house.

Hydrogen sulfide, confirmed

Washington spent more than $3.5 million finding out why. Investigators at the Consumer Product Safety Commission called it the largest investigation in agency history, committed roughly fifteen percent of the staff, and collected complaints from more than 2,000 homeowners across 32 states. Its testing contractor compared 41 complaint homes against 10 control homes built around the same time in the same neighborhoods. Published in November 2009, the verdict was blunt: hydrogen sulfide gas, off-gassing from the wallboard, was converting copper and silver into sulfides, and the proof sat in plain sight because bare metal test strips hung in complaint homes corroded significantly faster than strips in the control homes while ground-wire corrosion came back statistically significant, a result nobody could honestly call subtle.

Buried in the same report was the detail that matters most today. Using handheld X-ray fluorescence and infrared instruments, investigators could identify problem drywall sheet by sheet, through paint, without tearing anything open. A reliable detection method has existed since 2009. Hold that thought, because it becomes important later.

Who hung it, and who paid

Here is the part of the story that keeps getting skipped. Somebody hung 550 million pounds of this material between 2006 and 2009, enough for roughly 60,000 homes. That somebody was the boom-era drywall workforce: crews paid by the sheet, moving at a dead run, many of them immigrant laborers who had followed the reconstruction money to the Gulf, and they breathed the dust for years. When the scandal broke, the entire conversation became homeowners and manufacturers and lawsuits. I have yet to find a serious study of what the hangers inhaled. Health questions were asked about the people who lived with the walls. Nobody asked about the people who built them.

Nobody studied their lungs.

Then came the second wave of labor, remediation crews stripping houses back to the studs under a court-ordered protocol that held nothing back. Out came every sheet of problem drywall, all the wiring, every outlet and switch and breaker, the gas piping, the smoke alarms, the sprinkler heads. A house gets gutted to its framing and rebuilt from the inside, and the crew doing it wears respirators the original hangers never had: same walls, different lungs, different decade.

Do the settlement math

Knauf, the German manufacturer behind most of the bad board, settled for about $1.1 billion covering roughly 4,500 homeowners, a number that only makes sense once you price what it actually costs to gut a house to the studs and rebuild its electrical, plumbing, and alarm systems from scratch. Divide it out and you land near $244,000 per home. Now set that against the remediation cost ladder. In the court's own bellwether trial, remediation was priced at $81.13 per square foot of footprint. A court-supervised pilot program that fixed 300 homes ran $40 to $60 a square foot. Investor forums put competent contractor-managed remediation near $25. For a 2,000-square-foot house, that ladder runs from $50,000 to $162,000, which means the settlement paid well above the top of the ladder. That is what "made whole" actually costs: temporary housing, moving and storage, new appliances, and the years of life the thing consumed.

That is what a won lawsuit looks like. What followed was quieter: the settlement closed, a second manufacturer paid $248 million on its own, a Florida distributor's insurers paid $55 million, and then the door shut. In 2020, a federal judge denied a second class of late-discovering homeowners outright, while an earlier ruling left post-settlement buyers who never checked before buying with nothing at all. All of that money is gone.

The door shut.

Meanwhile the walls are still out there, changing hands in ordinary resale transactions.

A phone camera and a $400 test

This is where the technology story belongs, and it is not the story the vendors tell. Insurer Nationwide now offers policyholders a guided smartphone photo survey; its partner's models are trained on more than 400 known failure points and flag corrosion, bad wiring, and fire hazards from ordinary pictures, and the pitch is prevention. Point the same capability at a 2005 ranch in Cape Coral and it triages the visible evidence: blackened copper at the panel, a prematurely dead condenser coil, tarnished fixtures. None of that amounts to a diagnosis, but every bit of it is a reason to spend a few hundred dollars on the XRF test that has existed since 2009.

That asymmetry is the whole story: detection costs hundreds while remediation costs $80,000 to $165,000. Not looking was always the most expensive part of this disaster, and a Senate hearing said the quiet part out loud back in 2010: witnesses called it a "silent hurricane," testifying that the damage per home ran into the hundreds of thousands, that insurance and builder warranties were refusing the claims, and that for the families involved it was worse than the storm because at least a hurricane announces itself.

Look before you buy.

Your five-point screen (2004-2008 homes, Gulf Coast and Southeast)
  1. Read the back of the drywall in the attic or garage. "Knauf Tianjin," "Made in China," or no marking at all are all red flags.
  2. Smell for sulfur or rotten eggs, especially with the AC off. Sulfur gas concentrates when the air is still.
  3. Check the metals: AC coils replaced years early, silver tarnishing in weeks, mirrors blackening at the edges, green-black copper at the panel.
  4. Ask about symptoms: respiratory irritation or nosebleeds that ease after a few days away from the house.
  5. If any flag trips, order the XRF or lab test before you drop the inspection contingency, since it costs less than the appraisal. Then disclose the result or walk away.

Honest objections

The strongest case against the alarm goes like this, and it deserves a fair hearing. HUD's best estimate put affected homes near 11,000, not the 200,000 an advocacy group extrapolated from import volumes. Most problem homes have been remediated, settled, or demolished across two full turnovers of the housing stock. AI photo screening cannot diagnose wallboard chemistry, because it flags corrosion and corrosion has other causes, from coastal air to dissimilar metals to plain age, which means treating a phone scan as a verdict will kill good deals and frighten good buyers for no reason. Every word of that is fair, yet the rebuttal is arithmetic rather than alarm. Eleven thousand homes is a small number nationally and a very large number if you happen to be buying one of them, and the screening costs less than the appraisal. The disaster does not need to be common. It only needs to be findable, and since 2009, it has been.

What this article did not prove

Remediation costs come from 2009-2015 court records and contractor reports; 2026 pricing will differ and no current national survey exists. That 200,000-home figure is an advocacy extrapolation, presented as such. Nobody has measured how many unremediated homes remain; the inference comes from sales turnover, not a census. Health effects were never conclusively established beyond irritant-level findings, so this article treats corrosion as the proven harm and health complaints as reported, not established. AI inspection tools are described from vendor announcements; no independent accuracy study of drywall-specific screening was found.
