You Closed Every Window. Your House Still Inhaled 23% of the Smoke.

By Priya Greenwood · September 6, 2026

On the worst smoke day of last September, my neighbor did everything right. She checked the AQI on her phone, 187 and climbing, closed every window in the house, latched the dog door, and set the thermostat fan to run around the clock. Then she glanced at the little air quality monitor on her kitchen counter and watched the number climb anyway. 34. 48. 61. Outside, the sky was the color of a bruised apricot. Inside her sealed house, it was getting in.

She was not imagining it, and she was not doing it wrong. When UC Berkeley researchers pulled data from more than 1,400 paired indoor and outdoor air sensors across California during the 2020 fire season, they found that indoor PM2.5 nearly tripled on fire days compared with clean days. The average home admitted 23 percent of the outdoor smoke, an infiltration factor of 0.23. A quarter of the homes admitted more than 36 percent. Closing the windows helped enormously, but it did not make the house a shelter.

Run that 0.23 through a bad week and it stops sounding abstract. An AQI of 250, a bad but no longer rare September day, means roughly 200 micrograms of PM2.5 per cubic meter outside your front door. Multiply by 0.23 and your closed-up living room sits near 46 micrograms, unhealthy for sensitive groups and climbing. At the 75th percentile of leakiness, it is 72 micrograms, unhealthy for everyone, indoors, with every window shut. Your house is not a bunker but a sieve with good intentions, and the leakier the envelope, the faster the sieve fills.

Newly built homes did much better in the data, and so did homes with central air conditioning, because both tend to be tighter and to filter the air they recirculate. Here is the twist that should worry coastal California in particular: in a follow-up study of nearly 4,000 homes with more than 10,000 monitor-years of data, the temperate marine climate zone, our mild coastal strip, had the highest infiltration factors in the country, because we leave windows open more. Our beautiful weather is a ventilation habit, and during smoke season that habit becomes a liability.

That same dataset carries the good news, and it matters more than any gadget in this story. On fire days, people closed up and filtered, and infiltration dropped to roughly half of what the buildings would otherwise have allowed. Information worked. When people knew the smoke was coming, they acted, and the sensors proved they were doing it effectively. It is worth sitting with what that implies for every smart-home pitch in this space, because the single most effective intervention the researchers documented was not a device or an algorithm but millions of ordinary people reading an AQI number on their phones and acting on it the same afternoon. Everything that follows is optimization on top of that one decision.

The thermostat that fights back

That behavioral response, close up and filter, is exactly what a team at UC Berkeley's Center for the Built Environment is trying to automate. Using donated data from nearly 8,000 ecobee smart thermostats in California homes, the researchers are building control software that watches outdoor air quality feeds and, when smoke arrives, overrides your HVAC without asking: ventilation off, recirculation on, fan running even with no call for cooling, plus a notification explaining what it did and why, because most homeowners never think of their HVAC as a smoke defense system even though it already is one and it just needs to be told when to fight. Nobody sells this yet.

This is no longer a lab curiosity but a standards-backed engineering discipline: last year ASHRAE published Guideline 44, the first consensus playbook for protecting building occupants during wildfire smoke events, covering filtration, HVAC operation, and occupant protection strategies. The engineering establishment has officially decided this is a building systems problem, not a weather problem, which means the next decade of residential HVAC design will be argued less over SEER ratings and more over how fast a house can seal itself and scrub its own air when the sky turns the color of a warning light. The question for the rest of us is what to buy and what to do before the next orange sky, ranked by what each dollar actually buys. Start with free.

What works, ranked by dollars

The free moves are running the fan and killing the exhaust: set the thermostat fan to ON or CIRCULATE during smoke advisories so indoor air keeps moving through your filter even when the system is not heating or cooling. And go easy on the range hood and bath fans. Every cubic foot of air they throw outside gets replaced by smoky air leaking in through the walls to fill the vacuum, which means ventilation, normally your friend, becomes a straw into the haze during a smoke event.

The standard one-inch MERV 8 filter in most homes catches dust and pet hair but does almost nothing to PM2.5, which is why the roughly $20 move is a filter upgrade: a MERV 13 in the same slot captures smoke-range particles dramatically better. One caution, and it is real: the denser filter raises pressure drop, and older systems with weak blowers can suffer, so if your furnace is old, ask your HVAC tech before upsizing the filter, because a filter that starves the blower helps nobody.

For about $80, build the ugly box: four MERV 13 filters taped into a cube around a box fan, the Corsi-Rosenthal design that came out of the pandemic and never left. It is loud on high and it looks like a science fair project. It also moves more clean air per dollar than anything you can buy. I ran the numbers: a roughly $80 build delivers a clean air delivery rate north of 600 cubic feet per minute, which works out to about 13 cents per CADR. The $207 Coway Airmega AP-1512HH, a genuinely good unit with a smoke CADR of 234, costs about 88 cents per CADR. The $411 Blueair with a smoke CADR of 455 lands near 90 cents.

In the $100 to $300 range, buy the feedback loop: a portable HEPA purifier only works if it is big enough for the room, and the rule of thumb from AHAM is simple, the smoke CADR should be at least two-thirds of the room's square footage. A 400-square-foot living room needs a smoke CADR around 267. That stylish little unit rated for 181 square feet, sitting in your open-plan great room, is doing half a job. Consumer Reports' testing found the best HEPA units cut particle concentrations by up to 85 percent, but also found that many units underperform badly at low fan speeds, which is how everyone actually runs them. Size up, then run it on high when the smoke is bad and tolerate the noise. An indoor PM2.5 monitor in the $100 to $300 range closes the loop: run the purifier, watch the number fall, know the money worked.

The case against the gadgets

Now the strongest argument against everything above, stated fairly, because it deserves full strength. The Berkeley data says the biggest lever was never a device; it was behavior. Closing up and filtering cut infiltration in half, and a $20 filter plus shut windows did most of that work. The AI thermostat, the $300 sensor, the app with the colored dial: these are marginal gains stacked on top of basics that most people still skip. There is a real risk the gadget layer becomes a substitute for the boring layer, a $411 purifier humming away in a house with the windows cracked, which is the connected-home equivalent of buying a gym membership instead of going for a run, and no sensor on the wall can distinguish between the two. Technology that lets you feel protected without being protected is worse than no technology.

Low-cost sensors have their own credibility problem, because they are known to read high on wildfire smoke particles specifically. The published studies apply correction factors; your kitchen countertop unit does not. A sensor that cries wolf by 30 percent is still useful as a trend line, rising or falling, but do not treat its number as a lab result, and do not reorganize your life around a single spike.

Filtration also has a hard boundary that no marketing copy mentions: it does nothing for the gases in smoke. Carbon monoxide. Volatile organics. A carbon filter takes the smell out, which feels like safety and is not the same thing. Smell is not safety. And be skeptical of the $875 in-duct "air scrubber" pitches now circulating in smoke country. The efficacy claims are vendor-generated, with no independent CADR-equivalent testing behind them. HEPA is the boring technology with independent test data. Boring wins.

What to do before the next orange sky

Pick one clean room and over-filter it. Bedrooms matter most because you spend eight hours there breathing deeply. Seal it as best you can, put the biggest purifier there, and keep the door closed during smoke events. EPA guidance has backed the clean-room strategy for years.

Size the purifier with the two-thirds rule, then buy one size up. Manufacturers rate for ideal conditions, while your room has a hallway, an open kitchen, and a dog door someone forgot to latch.

Put two MERV 13 filters in the closet now. During a bad smoke week they sell out, and a clogged filter from last season is barely a filter. Write the install date on the cardboard frame with a marker.

Learn your thermostat's fan-ON setting today, not during the emergency. On most smart thermostats it is two taps, on older ones it is a physical switch, so practice once, either way.

If anyone in the house has asthma, heart disease, or is pregnant, treat smoke season as a medical planning exercise, not a comfort project. Talk to your doctor about a personal action plan before September, because the week the sky turns orange is the wrong week to start.

Limitations of this analysis

The infiltration numbers come from PurpleAir crowdsourced data: self-selected sensor owners, heavily California, heavily weighted toward the 2020 season. That is not a randomized national housing sample, and sensor owners who buy air monitors may behave more protectively than average, which would bias infiltration factors downward. The dollars-per-CADR comparison is my own math on September 2026 retail prices, and it assumes rated CADR holds at fan speeds people can tolerate; Consumer Reports notes many units fall short at low speed, which flatters the store-bought numbers, not the DIY box. I could not find any clinical trial showing portable purifiers prevent health outcomes during smoke events; the health case rests on PM2.5 exposure science, which is strong but not the same thing. And the automated wildfire thermostat is still research software at Berkeley. No consumer product ships a true wildfire mode today, so treat that section as a preview, not a shopping list.

Your house will never be a clean room. The data says it can be a much better filter than it is, and the difference between 23 percent and 10 percent of a smoke plume is measured in the air your kids breathe while they sleep. Close the windows. Run the fan. Size the purifier. The sky is going to turn orange again, and when it does, the difference between a house that was prepared in September and one that was not will be written in the lungs of everyone sleeping inside it.
