Your Smoke Alarms Are Painted Shut and Nine Years Old. The Fix Costs $30.

By Frank DeLuca · October 8, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

I have watched painters paint over smoke alarms on eleven projects. I counted, because after the third one I started keeping a list, and after the eleventh I stopped being surprised and started putting it in the pre-paint walkthrough notes in capital letters. It never works. Somewhere between the second coat and the touch-ups, a $30 life-safety device becomes a $30 ceiling ornament with a film of eggshell sealing the sensor grille.

How the trades sequence through a house is how the alarm plan falls apart, and by the time the homeowner takes possession, the most important safety system in the building is the one nobody verified. Not the technology, which is fine. Not the code, which has specified exactly where every alarm goes for decades.

Start with the stakes, because they are not abstract. Per the National Fire Protection Association, almost three out of five home fire deaths happen in homes with no smoke alarms or no working smoke alarms, and the risk of dying in a reported home fire is 54 percent lower in homes with working alarms. In half the fires where alarms were present but silent, the batteries were missing or disconnected. People do not die because the sensor technology failed. They die because someone took the battery out after the third false alarm during dinner, and nobody ever put it back.

What the Code Actually Requires

Section R314 of the International Residential Code is admirably specific, which makes the field failure rate almost insulting. Alarms go in each sleeping room, outside each separate sleeping area in the immediate vicinity of the bedrooms, and on every additional story including basements and habitable attics, and where more than one is required they must be interconnected so that one sounding sets off all of them, though listed wireless alarms are exempt from physical interconnection and primary power comes from the building wiring with battery backup. Section R315 adds carbon monoxide alarms outside sleeping areas on levels with fuel-burning appliances or an attached garage.

Count it for a typical four-bedroom, two-story house with a basement: four in the bedrooms, one or two outside the sleeping areas, one per story, one in the basement, seven or eight smoke alarms plus two or three CO alarms, all for roughly $30 apiece in sealed 10-year units, which means full coverage costs less than the door hardware on the front entry. Money was never the problem.

Where the Sequence Breaks

The electrician roughs in the alarm boxes early, which is correct, and then the drywall crew buries two of them, which is traditional, and then the painter coats whatever is visible, including on one memorable project the hallway alarm, the bedroom alarm, and, I am fairly certain, the painter's own radio. At trim-out, the electrician installs the alarm heads, tests the horn on each one, and almost never tests whether actuation of one triggers all the others, which is the entire point of interconnection. Your inspector presses the test button, hears the beep, and signs off. The test button only proves the horn works. It says nothing about the sensor, nothing about the interconnection, and nothing about the paint now sealing the grille.

Remodels get their own special failure, because existing areas are exempt from the interconnection requirement when alterations do not open up wall or ceiling finishes, so an addition gets a lone battery alarm that will never join the chorus: legally compliant, functionally a soloist in a house that needed a choir. Then the homeowner takes over, and the statistics take over with them, because cooking nuisance alarms are the leading reason people disable alarms, which is why the eighth edition of UL 217, effective June 2024, added a dedicated cooking-nuisance test requiring alarms to ride through broiling hamburger patties at ten feet without sounding. It is a real engineering response to a real behavioral problem. But the installed base of pre-2024 alarms will chirp at bacon for another decade, and every chirp is another battery pulled, another unit silenced, another house quietly joining the three-in-five.

3 minutes Estimated escape time in a modern home fire, down from about 17 minutes in 1978, per UL research. Synthetic furnishings burn hotter and faster, and when the three minutes start, the alarm is the only warning system most homes have.

Carbon Monoxide Kills Differently

CO deserves its own paragraph because it kills differently: silently, and often in houses whose smoke alarms work perfectly. CPSC estimates an average of 238 non-fire CO deaths a year associated with consumer products, with portable generators alone accounting for about 73, and the agency puts total unintentional CO deaths above 400 annually alongside more than 20,000 emergency department visits. R315 is the code's answer, but the field reality is that CO alarms get even less attention than smoke alarms at final inspection, and combination units confuse the replacement math, because a smoke/CO combo is a 10-year device while a standalone plug-in CO alarm is often a 5- to 7-year device, and nobody labels which is which on the wall.

What Got Better

Three things, and I will give credit where it is due. First, sealed 10-year lithium alarms eliminated the most common failure mode, the missing battery, by making the battery unremovable. Second, the UL 217 eighth edition multi-criteria sensors discriminate cooking aerosols from fire smoke, attacking the nuisance problem at the source instead of at the homeowner's patience. Third, AI plan review has reached the life-safety discipline: UpCodes launched AI-native plan review in June 2026 that runs discipline-specific analyses, including life safety, against locally adopted codes across more than 6,000 jurisdictions, flagging missing alarm locations on the drawings before the electrician ever pulls wire, which is exactly the kind of boring, high-leverage verification this trade has needed for thirty years.

What Has Not Gotten Better

Now the steelman against my own optimism, stated at full strength. AI plan review checks drawings, and the painted-over alarm happens after the drawing is approved, because no software has ever stopped a painter with a roller and a deadline. Smart interconnected alarms with voice alerts and phone notifications cost two to three times the basic sealed unit, and the households at highest risk, low-income and elderly, are the least likely to have them, which is precisely why the CPSC is spending grant money installing CO alarms directly. Because of that exception, most of the American housing stock will stay non-interconnected indefinitely, because nobody opens finished ceilings to run a three-wire. And I found no field data on what share of existing homes actually meet R314 placement today; the NFPA fatality statistics are the proxy, and they are a grim one.

There is also the 10-year clock, which nobody tracks, because that date sits on the back of the unit, which means you have to take it down to read it, which means nobody reads it. Pressing the test button feels like maintenance. It is not maintenance. It is a horn check on a sensor that has been breathing cooking grease and drywall dust for nine years.

$210–$270 Approximate cost to bring a typical two-story, four-bedroom home to full R314/R315 alarm coverage with sealed 10-year units: seven to nine alarms at roughly $30 each. Smart interconnected combos run about $80 each, or roughly $600 for the house.

Your Walkthrough

If you are building, put alarm verification in the pre-paint walkthrough and again at final, and specify the test in writing: actuate one alarm and confirm every unit in the house sounds. If the painter has already been through, look at every alarm grille for paint, because a painted alarm is a failed alarm, and replace it, do not clean it.

If you are buying, take every alarm down and read the manufacture date on the back, and replace anything over ten years old regardless of what the test button says. Count the locations against R314: each bedroom, outside each sleeping area, every story, basement included, and check for CO alarms outside sleeping areas if there is a gas appliance, a fireplace, or an attached garage, then budget $30 per unit and an afternoon.

If you are remodeling and the finishes are staying closed, the code does not require interconnection, but wireless interconnected alarms now exist specifically for this situation, and the incremental cost over standalone units is small, so treat the exception as a floor, not a recommendation.

Eleven painted alarms. I keep the list because the list is the job: the plan was correct on every one of those projects, the drawings showed every location, and the sequence ate it anyway. Check the sequence, not just the plan, and the cheapest life-safety system in the house might actually work when the three minutes start.
