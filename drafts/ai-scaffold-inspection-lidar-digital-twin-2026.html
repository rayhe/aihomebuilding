OSHA Cited Scaffolding 1,725 Times Last Year. The AI Inspector Never Blinks.

By Jake Kowalski · September 22, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Tuesday, 6:40 in the morning, and the scaffold that went up Friday for the siding crew is already wrong. Somebody pulled the midrail on Saturday to swing a bundle of fiber-cement board up to the second lift, and it never went back on, and nobody wrote it down, because under federal law somebody was supposed to walk that scaffold before the shift started, and there was no walk, and there is rarely a walk.

Last week OSHA published its preliminary list of the most-cited violations for fiscal year 2026, and scaffolding landed at number four with 1,725 citations, behind fall protection at 4,041 and ahead of ladders at 1,659, which means the top of the list reads as one story told four ways: American workers keep falling off things.

Analysts at the National Safety Council matched the citation list against Bureau of Labor Statistics data and found that falls tied to those four standards killed 844 people in 2024, including 666 falls to a lower level. Zoom in on scaffolds and the numbers sharpen: NIOSH counts roughly 4,500 scaffold injuries a year, the BLS counted 61 scaffold deaths in 2021, falls from scaffolds make up 18 percent of construction fall deaths, and the NSC estimates that 70 percent of scaffold injuries would never happen if crews followed the rules already on the books.

Nobody multiplies those numbers together, so I will: 844 fall deaths times 18 percent is about 152 scaffold deaths a year, and 70 percent of that is roughly 106: the annual death toll of the compliance gap, stated in people instead of percentages. Put that number in your pocket; we are coming back to it.

An inspection the law already demands

Here is the part that should embarrass the industry: OSHA standard 1926.451(f)(3) already requires a competent person to inspect the scaffold before every shift and after any event that could affect its structural integrity. Nothing is wrong with the regulation. The problem is that reality looks like a foreman glancing up from his coffee and calling it good. The coffee inspection is not an inspection.

Meanwhile the scaffold stops being the inspected structure almost immediately, because braces come off so material can be loaded, planks get shifted, and ties get loosened so the crew can work behind them. A once-a-shift inspection therefore certifies the scaffold's condition for about the first hour of an eight-hour day, and everything after that runs on trust. Trust is not a fall protection system.

A laser that never blinks

A research team at Lulea University of Technology in Sweden decided to stop trusting. Their system starts with a certified LiDAR scan on erection day, and that scan becomes the reference twin, the ground truth of what the scaffold is supposed to be. After that, drones, fixed sensors, or a robot dog rescan the structure on a schedule, cloud AI compares each new point cloud against the twin using graph-based analysis, and a missing brace or a shifted standard gets flagged automatically.

Alerts land on the site manager's dashboard, and an AR overlay paints the problem components orange and red directly onto the physical structure, so nobody squints at a 2D report trying to figure out which bay is bay 14.

Its intellectual frame is the Prognostics and Health Management playbook borrowed from aerospace: predict the failure instead of investigating it. On paper, this is the inspection the regulation wants, running continuously instead of once a shift.

For the record, this is a research prototype, not a product, and nobody has published field-trial injury numbers or a price. But the commercial neighborhood is real: Hong Kong's viAct runs scenario-based vision AI on ordinary site cameras, and Sitemetric launched an AI camera this year that watches PPE, falls, and zone violations with a human monitoring team behind the alerts. Where those cameras watch the people, the Swedish system watches the structure, which is a different problem requiring a different tool, and you should not let a vendor blur the two.

What the inspection costs

Run the labor math and the pitch gets concrete, because a competent-person inspection takes about 20 minutes per scaffold per shift, which at $55 an hour fully loaded is $18 a shift, or roughly $4,600 a year for one scaffold running 250 days, and that number is the bar automated inspection has to clear: not the OSHA fine, but the labor line.

Skip the inspection and one serious violation costs more than the year's labor. Skip the maintenance and one funeral costs more than everything. Put the $4,600 on the bid, because it is the cheapest line item you will ever write.

What the brochure skips

Now the skepticism, because somebody has to say it: this is a compliance-culture problem wearing an inspection-technology costume. Look at the actual most-cited scaffold violations, which are missing guardrails and missing fall protection above 10 feet, the kind of defect you can spot from the parking lot. No point cloud required.

It also needs a certified design model to twin against, which is a reasonable ask on a hospital tower and pure fiction on a two-story residing job, where the scaffold is tube and coupler assembled from twenty years of experience and zero drawings. Meanwhile the actual failure mode is the foreman who pulls the midrail for material loading at 10 a.m. and forgets it by lunch, and no 6 a.m. LiDAR scan fixes 10 a.m., and no drone puts the rail back.

Rank it honestly for the residential builder, which is most of you reading this: disciplined human inspection first, camera systems second, and laser twins a distant third, somewhere after buying decent planks. On a big multifamily or commercial job with tower scaffolds, design models, and a real safety manager, continuous twin comparison is exactly the right tool, because the structure is complex, the modifications are constant, and a failure is catastrophic. Thinking it replaces the clipboard everywhere is the mistake; it replaces the clipboard where the clipboard was already failing at scale.

What to do

Do the inspection the law already requires: before each shift, after any event that could affect integrity, photo-logged with your phone. Twenty minutes, and it remains the highest-value safety action on a scaffold, free if you are already paying the competent person.

Treat guardrail removal as a permitted event, not a casual one, which means the rail comes off for loading and goes back on before anyone works above 10 feet, and you write that rule down, because most citations trace to rails that were "temporarily" removed sometime around the previous administration.

On bigger jobs, run weekly reality capture: a drone or 360-camera pass over the tower scaffolds, compared against erection-day photos, which captures most of the twin's value with a photo album and a calendar reminder.

If you buy an AI safety camera, buy it for what it does, which is PPE, zones, and fall detection. It is not a structural scaffold inspector, and the serious vendors do not claim it is.

Price the inspection into the bid, because twenty minutes a shift is $4,600 a year per scaffold, which is cheaper than one serious-violation penalty and cheaper than one funeral by an amount I cannot calculate.

The scaffold will keep changing during the day. The question is whether anyone is watching when it does.

What this analysis cannot tell you

That 106 figure mixes an all-industry death count with a construction-only percentage, so treat it as a bounding exercise rather than epidemiology, and note that no published field-trial outcome data exists for the Lulea system, which means the mechanism is demonstrated and the life-saving is not. Inspection labor math uses illustrative rates, and union markets and two-person residential crews will differ. I did not solicit vendor quotes for automated scaffold inspection, because a scaffold-specific commercial product does not exist yet. OSHA's FY2026 citation counts are preliminary through August 31. Nobody from this publication climbed a scaffold for this article, though the author has, back when his knees worked.
