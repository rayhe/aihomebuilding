# Research: SB 9's Lot-Split Promise Fizzled — SB 1123 Is the Do-Over (Article #849)

Journalist: Catherine "Code" Chen (policy & regulation beat)
Date: September 13, 2026

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? YES — a California homeowner with a large lot gets: (1) the actual first-year application numbers showing SB 9 lot splits barely happened, (2) the three specific legal tripwires that killed most SB 9 projects, (3) what SB 1123 (effective July 1, 2025) changes — 10 parcels, no owner-occupancy affidavit, ministerial approval — and (4) the catch: SB 1123 mostly doesn't apply to occupied single-family lots, so SB 9 remains the homeowner path.

## Angle
SB 9 was sold as the law that would let ordinary homeowners split their lots and build wealth. Four years of data say the lot-split provision was the least-used part of the law. Now SB 1123 — which amended SB 684 — offers a ministerial path to 10 fee-simple parcels with no owner-occupancy requirement. The twist: it deliberately excludes the occupied single-family homeowner SB 9 was written for. Two laws, two different people.

## Original contribution
1. **The yield-gap calculation.** Terner modeled 714,000 market-feasible SB 9 homes statewide. First-year actuals in LA: 211 unit applications + 28 lot-split applications in a city that permitted ~20,000 homes in 2021. That's roughly 1% of one year's normal permitting volume chasing the headline reform — and 0 of 28 lot splits approved.
2. **The three-tripwire diagnosis.** Synthesizing statute + guidebook + Terner: (a) owner-occupancy affidavit (3-year principal-residence commitment) scares off anyone who might need to sell; (b) the 40%/1,200-sq-ft geometry rule silently disqualifies most suburban lots under ~6,000 sq ft from a clean split; (c) "no adjacent lot split" + "not created by prior SB 9 split" blocks the small-developer assembly play. Together these explain why Terner found only ~10% of new units would come from lot splits.
3. **The SB 1123 contrast table.** Side-by-side: max parcels (2 vs 10), max homes (4 vs 10+ADUs), owner-occupancy (3-yr affidavit vs none), review (ministerial vs ministerial), CEQA (exempt both), eligible lots (occupied SFR ok vs vacant-SFR or multifamily only), avg unit size cap (none vs 1,750 sq ft in counties like Santa Cruz). The punchline: the law that fixes SB 9's three tripwires excludes the homeowner SB 9 was written for.
4. **Worked homeowner example (Sacramento).** 7,500 sq ft R-1 lot, existing 1,400 sq ft home: SB 9 path = split 60/40 (4,500/3,000 sq ft), keep house on one lot, build duplex on the other → 3 total homes, owner must sign 3-year occupancy affidavit. SB 1123 path = not available (lot is occupied, zoned single-family). The honest answer for this homeowner: SB 9 or nothing.

## Strongest counterargument
The low application numbers don't prove the law failed — they may prove it worked as a pressure valve. Terner itself found 97% of homes would be retained and duplex conversions (not lot splits) would dominate; the unit-development track (211 applications in LA) outperformed the lot-split track 7-to-1, which is exactly what the model predicted. And SB 9's ministerial duplex path may have cannibalized lot-split applications: why split when you can build two units without one? Additionally, 2022 was year one of a 30-year land-use shift; ADU law took nearly a decade to hit stride. Judging SB 9 by 11 months of data is premature — though four years on, no newer statewide survey has been published, which is itself a data point about political appetite for measuring it.

## Limitations
- No statewide SB 9 application survey newer than Terner's November 2022 first-year data exists publicly; 2023–2026 uptake is anecdotal (city staff reports like Los Altos: 9 lot splits in 27 months).
- SB 1123 has been effective only since July 1, 2025 — application counts under the amended law are not yet published by any surveyed jurisdiction.
- Terner's 714,000-unit figure is modeled market feasibility, not a forecast; actual uptake depends on financing, which the model assumes is available. SB 9 construction lending products remain rare.
- The worked Sacramento example uses typical R-1 standards; actual setbacks, FAR, and objective standards vary by jurisdiction and can preclude the modeled outcome.
- Breezy's Underbuilt Radar and Acres.com are cited as examples of AI feasibility tooling; their SB 9/SB 1123-specific accuracy has not been independently verified.

## Actionable takeaways (for the article)
- Own a CA single-family lot ≥4,800 sq ft (for a clean 60/40 split at 1,200 sq ft minimums... actually 2,400 sq ft minimum for 50/50; 3,000 for 60/40)? Check your city's SB 9 urgency ordinance first — many added local rules.
- Run an AI feasibility report (Breezy Underbuilt Radar, ~minutes) before paying a land-use attorney $400/hr to read the same code.
- If your lot is vacant and single-family zoned, or multifamily zoned: SB 1123's 10-parcel ministerial path (no 3-year occupancy affidavit) is likely the better vehicle — talk to a civil engineer who runs SB 1123 playbooks (e.g., Riechers Engineering's published approach).
- Budget reality: SF Planning estimated duplex conversion at "several hundred thousand dollars" minimum plus up to 24 months with possible relocation; lot split alone ~12 months before construction.
- Tenant-occupied property in the last 3 years? SB 9's demolition limits likely kill the project.
