Your Sub's Insurance Certificate Is Real. His Coverage Isn't.

A ghost workers' comp policy produces a valid certificate of insurance covering zero employees. California logged 2,932 suspected fraud cases last year while two AI startups raised $30 million to read the certificates nobody reads.

Marcus Washington
September 5, 2026

Two owners of a flooring installation company in California were arraigned on three felony counts of workers' compensation fraud after investigators found unlicensed workers on the company's jobs. Their carrier had discovered unlicensed workers on the company's jobs receiving 1099 forms, classified as independent contractors while doing employee work, and the underreported payroll produced nearly $3.8 million in losses to the insurers, according to the Insurance Journal's coverage of the case. That case made the trade press; thousands of smaller versions never make anything at all.

In the trade, the most common version has a name. It is called a ghost policy, which is a workers' compensation policy written for a company that claims to have zero employees on its payroll and no intention of ever filing a claim. For a true solo owner-operator with no crew, it is legal and unremarkable. For a framing contractor with eight men on your second-floor deck, it is fraud wearing a certificate of insurance as a disguise. A COI from a ghost policy looks exactly like the real thing because, in a narrow technical sense, it is one. A policy exists. A premium was paid. Nobody on your job site is covered. Read that again.

Understanding why it works takes about thirty seconds of arithmetic that every honest subcontractor already knows by heart. Workers' comp for framing, roofing, and concrete crews carries some of the steepest premium rates in the business, because the injuries are frequent and expensive. A sub who insures a real crew pays real premiums on every payroll, while a sub with a ghost policy pays the policy minimum and reports no payroll at all. Put both bids on a homeowner's kitchen table and the fraud wins the job by exactly the cost of the coverage the honest sub carries. He is not more efficient, he is uninsured. That is the entire scheme.

California's Department of Insurance estimates workers' comp fraud costs the state between $1 billion and $3 billion every year. In fiscal 2023-24, its Fraud Division logged 2,932 suspected fraud cases, opened 291 new investigations, made 128 arrests, and sent 156 cases to prosecutors, with potential losses of $157.2 million in a single year. Court-ordered restitution at the State Fund of California, the state's largest comp carrier, climbed from $8.7 million in 2020 to $17.8 million in 2023. Investigators in North Carolina found roughly 40 percent of audited companies had misclassified employees. California and Pennsylvania eventually wrote laws making site owners responsible for coverage for everyone on site regardless of classification, which tells you how thoroughly the classification game was being played before anyone wrote it down.

Start with who gets hurt, because the paperwork conversation always buries this part. First, the worker: a man with a broken ankle and a policy that lists zero employees has no coverage and usually no idea until the claim is denied, and then he has medical bills and no paycheck. Second, the honest sub: he loses bids to fraud for years, then either joins it or leaves the trade, and the trade loses another straight shooter it cannot afford to lose. Third, the general contractor: when the injury lawyer starts asking who hired an uninsured crew, the certificate in the job trailer buys less protection than anyone assumed. My father carried a union card for thirty years, and what he taught me about this business is that the paperwork is never just paperwork. It is the only evidence of who was protected and who was not. Keep it current.

Enforcement, by the numbers

California's Contractors State License Board runs undercover sting operations through its SWIFT unit. In the first seven months of 2024: 26 days of stings across 14 operations, 60 notices to appear in criminal court, 11 citations, 15 stop orders, and 80 cases referred to district attorneys. A two-week coordinated sweep across 26 counties later produced 229 legal actions against licensed and unlicensed contractors, and in one Bakersfield operation 12 people were cited, with six receiving stop orders specifically for lacking workers' comp while running crews. Administrative fines run to $15,000, with misdemeanor criminal exposure on top. That is real enforcement, and it is also a fraction of a $1-to-3-billion problem, which is the part nobody at the press conference says out loud.

Software that reads the certificates

Two venture-backed companies now sell AI that does what nobody in the job trailer does: actually read the certificates. Jones closed a $15 million Series B in January 2025, led by NewSpring Capital with Khosla Ventures and JLL Spark participating, and reports its platform now touches 25,039 properties and projects covering 2.5 billion square feet of U.S. real estate and construction. It is rolling out AI agents trained on what it describes as millions of verified insurance documents to collect COIs, check endorsements against contract requirements, and flag non-compliance without a human opening a PDF. TrustLayer raised $15 million in 2021 from Craft Ventures and a syndicate of insurance brokers, pairs robotic process automation with AI to verify coverage and licenses, and plugs directly into Procore so a GC sees each sub's compliance status inside the project management tool instead of in a folder nobody opens, which means the status on the screen reflects what the AI verified against carrier records that morning rather than a PDF somebody uploaded and forgot half a year ago. Both make the same pitch, and it is a good one: certificates expire mid-project, endorsements get missed, and manual tracking across forty subs is a spreadsheet held together by hope.

Free, and five minutes

Here is the uncomfortable part, and I will not bury it. No product beats a five-minute phone call to the broker whose number is printed on the certificate, and the call costs nothing. Ask whether the policy is active today. Ask what payroll and class codes are actually reported. Ask whether the named insured has had any lapse in the last twelve months. That call catches ghost policies because the broker will tell you the policy covers zero employees, and it requires no software, no subscription, no dashboard. AI products solve the scale problem, hundreds of subs with expiring certs and endorsements to track across a portfolio, but they introduce a failure mode worth naming: the GC who outsources verification to software stops reading certificates entirely, which is precisely the complacency the fraud depends on. Pricing for both platforms is quote-based and undisclosed, customer counts are company-reported, and no independent study has measured whether AI-verified projects suffer fewer uncovered injuries. In the end, the technology moves trust from paper to a screen. It does not change the incentive to cheat.

What to do Monday morning

If you are a homeowner, ask your GC for the sub's COI and call the broker yourself, then run the contractor's license through the CSLB instant license check, which is free, takes ninety seconds, and shows whether the comp coverage the certificate claims actually exists in the state's own records. If you are a GC with a dozen subs, build the broker call into onboarding and re-verify mid-project, because policies get cancelled the month after the certificate is issued and nobody sends you the memo. If you run thirty or forty subs across multiple jobs, that is when the software math starts to work: one uncovered injury, one stop-work order, or one fraud-adjacent lawsuit costs more than any subscription, even one whose price is undisclosed. Put additional-insured endorsements and waivers of subrogation in every subcontract, in writing, before mobilization. Paper is cheap; uncovered injuries are not.

Limitations

This article's fraud statistics come from California, which investigates and reports workers' comp fraud more aggressively than most states, so they describe the best-measured market rather than the national average. That $1-to-3-billion estimate covers all industries, not residential construction specifically, and nobody publishes a residential-only ghost-policy count. Jones and TrustLayer figures are company-reported and unaudited, and neither company publishes pricing, so the ROI case for AI verification is argued from the cost of failure rather than from measured savings. One prosecution is the visible tip; most ghost policies are never detected, which means both the scale of the problem and the effectiveness of every countermeasure remain estimated rather than known.

Steel-manning the other side

Here is the best case against everything above. Overwhelmingly, subcontractors are honest, carry real coverage, and resent being treated as suspects. COI fraud, while real, is a small fraction of a large and mostly honest market. Layering AI verification onto every sub relationship taxes the compliant majority to catch a dishonest minority, and the cost flows straight into home prices that are already out of reach. There is truth in it, and it deserves a straight answer. Most subs are straight, but the honest majority is also the constituency most damaged by the fraud: every job lost to a ghost-policy bid is a job the honest sub never got, and a verification regime that clears the honest quickly is the closest thing this trade has to a level bid table. Nobody here is saying everyone cheats. The ones who do are bidding against your father, and right now they are winning. Verify everything.
