Net Migration Fell 88% in Two Years. Your Builder’s Workforce Plan Didn’t Notice.

Large commercial contractors use AI to model labor supply shocks months in advance. Residential builders managing $2 million projects use a whiteboard and a phone tree. One of those approaches survives what's coming.

By Marcus Washington • July 25, 2026 • 7 min read

Mario Guerrero got the call on a Tuesday morning, mid-pour. A concrete job in the Rio Grande Valley, halfway done, eight yards in the truck and the forms set, and immigration enforcement officers had just arrived at the site. Half the crew walked off before anyone asked them a question, not arrested or detained, just gone, and the pour sat exposed while Guerrero worked the phones trying to figure out who would finish it.

Guerrero runs the South Texas Builders Association, and when he told reporters what happened next, he framed it the way a builder frames everything: in dollars. Interest kept compounding. Short-term construction loans don't pause because your crew did, and the exposed rebar sat through two rain cycles while the replacement crew charged a premium because they knew the builder was desperate, and every dollar of overage landed on the buyer's closing statement.

"We're not saying don't do your job," Guerrero said. "We're saying there's a better way."

Nobody found one.

His story is one site, one pour, one disruption. Scale it nationally and you start to see the contours of the problem that almost nobody in residential construction is planning for.

Two Numbers That Changed Everything

Net international migration to the United States hit 2.7 million people in 2024, according to Congressional Budget Office estimates cited in Bridgit's 2026 Construction Workforce Benchmark Report. By 2026, CBO projects that number drops to roughly 321,000. Eighty-eight percent gone.

Immigration has been what Bridgit's analysts call "the demographic backstop the industry has leaned on," not a supplementary source of workers but a structural dependency without which the math falls apart.

Meanwhile, the Associated Builders and Contractors published its annual workforce analysis in January. Their model says the industry needs 349,000 net new workers in 2026 just to maintain current output. Most of that demand comes from retirement, not growth. About 41% of today's construction workforce will reach retirement age by 2031, according to Procore's Future State of Construction survey of 1,200 decision-makers. One in five electricians is already over 55, per ABC's analysis of BLS employment data.

So the pipeline that supplied new workers is down 88%, while the pipeline draining experienced ones is accelerating. Those two curves crossed. Most residential builders noticed only when they couldn't fill a framing crew.

Money Alone Doesn't Fix It

Wages went up, and it barely mattered. Deloitte's analysis found construction wages rose 21% over a recent multi-year stretch, but employment increased only 8.8%. Money couldn't close the gap.

A 21% raise bought an 8.8% increase in bodies, which means if your business model depends on doubling headcount by doubling pay, you will get less than half of what you need and spend everything you budgeted to get there.

Bridgit's benchmark data tells the rest of the story. Among the 233 companies they analyzed, covering 114,000 workers and nearly 40% of the ENR 400, median attrition hovers just below 20%. In 2025, 46% of those companies saw no net workforce growth whatsoever: 20% actually shrank and 26% stayed flat.

A firm trying to grow by 100 people at 20% attrition needs to hire 125, and at 35% attrition that number climbs to 154, which means you are running on a treadmill that speeds up every quarter while the track underneath it narrows.

What the Big Companies Have That You Don't

Bridgit found something striking in the ENR 400 data. Their top 50 companies face attrition rates roughly identical to the broader industry, with the same turnover, the same retirement pressure, and the same immigration headwinds bearing down on every project. Yet their median growth rate is three times higher.

Not because they pay more, but because they plan around the losses before they happen.

Top-50 firms have a median workforce planning horizon of 6.8 years, while the industry average sits at 4.7. Those companies use tools like Bridgit Bench, which models crew availability across projects, flags attrition risk by role and tenure, and runs scenario analyses when labor supply changes. Procore's Resource Planning platform, built on the former LaborChart product, does something similar: people management, forecasting, analytics, from 10-person shops to 10,000-employee enterprises. Both products are designed for commercial general contractors managing portfolios of multi-million-dollar projects, and both cost accordingly.

Now consider the residential builder running three custom homes and a renovation, with a core crew of nine and a rotating cast of subs. His workforce plan is a contacts list and a gut feeling about which guys are reliable. His scheduling tool might be Workyard at $6 per user per month, Pro Crew Schedule for basic calendar views, or Buildern's AI-assisted timeline generation. Solid products for tracking where people are today.

None of them model where people will be in six months when a regional labor pool contracts by a third and two of your best finish carpenters age out of the trade.

An Academic Finding Nobody Commercialized

Economists Troup Howard, Mengqi Wang, and Dayin Zhang published research examining what happens to construction labor markets after immigration enforcement intensifies. They exploited the staggered rollout of enforcement actions across U.S. counties and found the expected result: treated counties saw large, persistent reductions in their construction workforce and corresponding drops in residential homebuilding.

What they found next upended the standard assumption, and it is the part that no workforce planning software has incorporated into its models.

Undocumented labor is complementary to domestic labor, not substitutive. Deporting undocumented construction workers didn't just remove those workers from the payroll. It reduced labor supplied by domestic construction workers on both the extensive margin (fewer people working) and the intensive margin (fewer hours worked by those who stayed). When the immigrant framer leaves, the native-born electrician who depended on that framer's schedule to sequence his own work loses productive hours too.

This means the actual workforce impact of an immigration decline is roughly 1.3 to 1.5 times the direct headcount loss. An 88% drop in net migration doesn't cost you 88% of the immigrant-origin workers. It costs you that plus the productivity drag on the remaining domestic workforce that was organized around those people being there.

No commercial workforce planning tool incorporates this multiplier effect. Bridgit tracks attrition at the company level, and Procore forecasts crew needs by project, but neither system models macro labor supply shocks or the second-order productivity drag those shocks impose on the crews that remain. Howard, Wang, and Zhang quantified the phenomenon in the academic literature, yet nobody has built the commercial product that translates their finding into a planning input a builder can actually use.

A 50-person residential construction firm with 30% annual turnover loses 15 people a year. At an average replacement cost of $12,800, that is $192,000 annually just in direct replacement spending. Vantage Circle's analysis shows that investing 25% of that figure in structured retention programs yields a 4:1 return even before counting project continuity and safety improvements.

Replacement cost, though, understates the real problem. Bridgit's benchmark found the average team rookie ratio sits at 36.4% across all companies, and on teams with 51 or more people, it climbs to 56.2%, meaning more than half the crew on a large project started within the past year or two. Experience bleeds out. Superintendents carry median tenure of 3.7 years while seniors hang on for 7.0, and that gap between experienced leadership and green crews is where quality failures, safety incidents, and schedule slippage concentrate.

Procore's survey adds a dimension that makes the whole cycle harder to break. Forty-five percent of workers cite burnout, not wages, as their primary reason for leaving construction. Workers who stay absorb the workload of the ones who leave, burn out faster, and create a feedback loop that no amount of hiring can outrun.

What Would Actually Help

A residential workforce planning tool would need to do four things that nothing on the market currently does at that scale and price point.

First, it would need to model regional labor supply at a geographic resolution that matters to a builder who only works in one metro area. If you build homes in South Texas, the immigration enforcement impact on your available crew is categorically different from what a builder in Vermont faces, and a tool that ingested BLS metro-area employment data, CBO migration projections, and local permit volumes could give you a 6-to-12-month view of whether your labor market is tightening or loosening without requiring a six-figure enterprise contract.

Second, it would need to predict individual attrition risk, which Bridgit already does for ENR 400 firms with datasets of thousands of workers but which nobody has scaled down to a crew of nine. For small teams you don't need a neural network; you need something that tracks tenure, pay trajectory relative to local alternatives, commute distance, and seasonal patterns, and when your best framer hits 24 months while the going rate across town jumps 12%, even a $6/month scheduling app should flag that risk before the resignation comes in.

Third, it would need to quantify the continuity cost that vanishes from every replacement-cost spreadsheet. Replacing a foreman isn't $22,400 in isolation; it's $22,400 plus the quality cost of a 12-week ramp period during which a less experienced person is running a $400,000 framing package, and if that ramp-up period produces one rework event on a structural element, the total cost can easily double.

Fourth, it would need to connect sub-contractor availability to project scheduling in a way that reflects how residential construction actually works. Residential builders depend on subs far more than commercial GCs depend on their direct-hire labor, and when your HVAC sub tells you he's six weeks out instead of two because he lost three techs, every downstream trade shifts in a cascade that no residential scheduling tool currently models automatically.

The Strongest Counterargument

Small builders will say they don't need software for any of this because they know their people, they call around, they've been managing labor the same way for 30 years and it has always worked.

That argument held when the labor pool was stable or growing, when net migration ran at 2.7 million and retirees were replaced by the next generation entering the trades, because gut-feel planning works when the margin for error is wide and a builder who lost a crew member could replace him in two weeks because three candidates existed for every open spot in most metro areas.

At 321,000 net migration, with 41% of the workforce approaching retirement and 45% of workers citing burnout as their reason for quitting, that margin has collapsed. Your phone tree only works when someone answers.

What We Didn't Prove

This analysis relies on CBO migration projections for 2026, which are models, not census counts. Actual net migration could come in higher if enforcement policy shifts or lower if it intensifies. Bridgit's benchmark, while substantial at 233 companies and 114,000 workers, skews toward the ENR 400 and may not represent the attrition dynamics of a 12-person residential framing crew. Howard, Wang, and Zhang's complementarity finding was measured across a specific enforcement rollout period and may not scale linearly to the current environment. We could not find per-metro replacement cost data for residential-specific roles, and the Vantage Circle figures derive from broader construction industry composites.

What we can say with confidence: the labor supply inputs that residential construction has relied on for decades are contracting simultaneously, and the tools that would help small builders plan around that contraction were built for companies ten to a hundred times their size.

If You're Hiring a Builder This Year

Ask how many people on your project crew have been together for more than one previous build, because a crew that has framed three houses together doesn't need to be told where the point load falls, while a crew assembled last Tuesday absolutely does and your project absorbs the difference in both time and quality.

Ask whether your builder has lost any key crew members in the past six months and how they backfilled. If the answer involves a temp staffing agency, understand that temp workers in construction carry higher incident rates and lower first-pass quality than direct hires, and your project is absorbing that difference.

Ask what happens to your timeline if the framing sub can't start for eight weeks instead of three, and if your builder doesn't have a contingency answer that involves specific alternative crews and realistic schedule adjustments, then what he has isn't a workforce plan but a hope that the phone tree delivers.
