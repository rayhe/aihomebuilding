Your Floor Plan's L-Shape Costs $300 a Year in Heat. The Box Version Was Free.

By Elena Vasquez • October 5, 2026

Stand on the sidewalk at dusk. Look at two new houses on the same street. Same 2,400 square feet, same builder, same zip code, same year built, sold within months of each other for nearly the same price per square foot, which is exactly why the comparison is so revealing. On the left stands a box: two stories, four corners, nothing to catch the eye and nothing to catch the wind. The one on the right bends. It reaches one arm around a courtyard the way good houses do when they want to hold a small outdoor room in private, and light spills from its corner windows across the patio stones. It is, by any honest measure, the more beautiful house: the one with the courtyard that catches the last of the evening sun, the corner windows that make the whole plan feel considered, the outdoor room held in private the way good houses have done for centuries.

It is also the more expensive house, every year, forever.

Not in the way you are thinking: both houses sold for the same price per square foot, because that is how houses are priced: by conditioned area, the warm space inside, as if the walls themselves were free. They are not free. Roughly 1,826 more square feet of exterior wall wrap the bent house than the box, wall that exists for one reason only, which is the shape. Every winter, heat walks out through it, and every summer, heat walks in. Nobody itemized this for the buyer, and nobody itemized it for the builder either, because the industry's pricing vocabulary has no word for the difference between floor area and envelope area, and the appraisal, the listing, and the tax assessment all speak fluent square-footage while the walls stay silent.

I love the bent house, and that is the uncomfortable part, which I will not pretend otherwise. Architecture that refuses every bend in the name of efficiency gives you a street of shipping containers, and I have walked those streets, in the developments where value engineering won every argument, and they are not places anyone lingers. But love is not an excuse for innumeracy, and the building industry has been innumerate about shape for a very long time. Physics this old is settled, the tools to see it in real time are new, and the habit of looking is still missing.

The tax nobody itemizes

Here is the quiet scandal of residential pricing: the market charges by the square foot of floor and gives away the envelope for free, as if the walls were a rounding error rather than the single largest surface through which the house negotiates with the weather. Two floor plans with identical conditioned area can differ in exterior wall area by 70 percent or more, and the listing sheet will never mention it. A buyer compares $385 per square foot to $385 per square foot and concludes the houses cost the same to own, never suspecting that the number on the listing sheet measures the warm space inside while saying nothing at all about the coat wrapped around it. They do not; one of them is wearing a much larger coat, and coats cost money to heat.

This is not a metaphor: building physicists have a number for it. They call it the form factor or shape factor: the ratio of the building's exterior surface area to its volume, or in the Passivhaus world's friendlier variant, exterior surface area divided by treated floor area. Passivhaus Trust design guidance treats a heat-loss form factor of 3 or less as a useful benchmark for small buildings, and notes that buildings with identical insulation, identical airtightness, and identical orientation can have significantly different heating demands purely because of this ratio. Shape is not a footnote to the energy model. It is the model. In the words of one Irish design review, the envelope-to-volume ratio has the biggest single impact on a building's energy performance. Bigger than the window spec, and bigger, some days, than the insulation.

Research literature keeps confirming it from different directions, in different climates and at different scales, with a persistence that should make any honest designer stop sketching for a moment and do the division. A 2024 review in Energies found form factor to be a key determinant of heating and cooling loads across climates and building types, and cited work showing rectangular multi-family forms with the lowest lifecycle energy use while H-shaped forms carried the highest. A 2025 paper on the heat balance of buildings put it bluntly: every fragmented form, L, T, U, E, generates higher heat losses throughout the life of the building than a compact shape. A cold-climate study found EUI differences of up to 17 percent between shape and layout options for the same program. Seventeen percent is not a rounding error; it is the difference between a good envelope and a mediocre one, except it was decided with a pencil in week two.

The ledger

Let me show the arithmetic, because the industry's habit is to gesture at this and move on. Take a 2,400 square foot home, two stories, nine-foot ceilings. Draw it first as a box: a footprint of about 34.6 by 34.6 feet, perimeter 139 feet, wall area 2,494 square feet above grade. Now bend the identical floor area into an L: a 40-by-40-foot square with a 20-by-20-foot courtyard notch carved out, perimeter 240 feet, wall area 4,320 square feet, with the same roof, the same floors, and the same warm space inside.

The tally: 1,826 square feet of wall. Seventy-three percent more wall. Zero more house.

Now run the heat through it. A standard 2x4 wall with R-13 cavity insulation loses heat at a rate of about 0.077 BTU per hour per square foot per degree Fahrenheit. Multiply by 1,826 square feet of extra wall and you get roughly 141 BTU per hour escaping for every degree of difference between inside and outside. Chicago sees about 6,000 heating degree days in a typical year. Annualize it, 141 × 24 × 6,000, and you get just over 20 million BTU, about 202 therms of natural gas, which at $1.50 per therm, a conservative figure against recent EIA residential averages running from about $1.25 in early 2025 to about $1.75 by spring 2026, means the L-shape costs roughly $300 more to heat every year. Over 30 years, discounted at 4 percent, that is about $5,200 in present value. In the box version, this money was free. It was available on sketch day, in the first two weeks of design, when moving a wall on paper costs nothing and the client has not yet fallen in love with the courtyard.

There is also the wall itself: somebody framed it, sheathed it, wrapped it, insulated it, sided it, trimmed it, caulked it, and will someday re-side it. At a rough $20 per square foot of installed exterior wall assembly, the shape bought about $36,500 of wall that performs no structural or spatial job the box's walls were not already doing. That figure is soft, regional, and cladding-dependent, while the heating math is harder, but both point the same direction.

The market charges by the square foot of floor and gives away the envelope for free. The L and the box sell for the same price and cost different amounts to own, forever.

The machine that sees it in seconds

This is the part that should embarrass the profession a little, because the excuse expired recently, back when the analyses that used to require a specialist, a workstation, and a week started running in a browser tab in seconds. Autodesk Forma, built from the Norwegian startup Spacemaker that Autodesk acquired in 2020, is a browser-based environment for the first two weeks of a project, where you pull in the site, sketch massing volumes, and watch trained predictive models return sun, wind, noise, daylight, and operational energy results in seconds as you push and pull the form, while its Rapid Operational Energy Analysis reads window-to-wall ratio and envelope U-values live. Its embodied carbon analysis, built on a data model from the firm EHDD, scores material and form choices at massing stage. A 2026 review put the pitch exactly right: the analysis moves to the moment the decision is being made instead of arriving as a post-rationalization after it.

TestFit, the parametric configurator that now offers a free massing tool, tells an adjacent story: its customers run two to three times as many design iterations per project, and site planning runs four to ten times faster. Courtesy of the same coverage, the honest footnote is that TestFit's magic is mostly parametric solving rather than AI, which is fine, and the point stands either way. Computations that expose the shape tax can now run in seconds, at the life stage of the project when the shape is still negotiable.

And yet almost nobody runs it for a single-family home, where the design fee is a rounding error next to the construction loan and the energy model, if one exists at all, arrives long after the shape has been set into the foundation drawings. Custom residential design still moves from napkin sketch to developed plan on the strength of the architect's eye, and the eye is superb at proportion and terrible at heat transfer. The capability exists. The habit does not. Somewhere between the massing model and the permit set, the L gets drawn because the courtyard is lovely, which it is, and the $5,200 never enters the conversation, which is a failure of process rather than taste.

What the box cannot give you

Now the defense, at full strength, because the optimization crowd needs to hear it and I need to say it. Compactness is not free. Daylight deep in the plan, cross-ventilation on summer nights, a private outdoor room, and compliance with a narrow lot where a box physically cannot sit: that is what the courtyard buys. In cooling-dominated climates the physics genuinely gets murkier: researchers including Depecker and Albatici have found that compactness does not always reduce energy demand in warm climates, and elongated forms can win back their losses through daylighting that displaces electric light, which means the honest version of this article carries an asterisk the size of the Sun Belt, and any designer working south of the Mason-Dixon line should read the heating math as a cautionary tale rather than a verdict. A box can read as cheap, and if the market pays a $40,000 premium for the house with the courtyard, then the $5,200 lifetime energy tax is a rounding error the buyer happily absorbs. Markets price beauty. That is not irrational.

There is also an equity wrinkle that cuts against the efficiency sermon. Geometry punishes small buildings hardest: an 8-meter cube carries a surface-to-volume ratio around 1.1 to 1.3, while a 16-meter cube of identical proportions sits near 0.46. On ADUs, cottages, and small affordable homes the shape tax lands heaviest, exactly the buildings whose owners can least afford it, and no amount of clever massing fully repeals the square-cube law. An AI that flags every L as wasteful without weighing light, air, land, and resale is optimizing the wrong objective, the way a spellchecker optimizes prose into correctness and out of style, and I would rather read a beautiful wrong sentence than a correct dull one, just as I would rather live in the bent house. I am skeptical of tools that flatten design into a single variable, and a form-factor scold is still a scold.

So no. The answer is not "build boxes." The answer is that the trade should be priced, honestly and early, the way every other six-figure decision in a construction budget gets priced, instead of being smuggled past the client inside a pretty rendering. Price it: the courtyard is worth $5,200 in lifetime energy plus $36,500 in wall. Say it out loud at the sketch table, let the client decide with open eyes, and the L becomes a choice instead of an accident. Most clients, shown the number, will still choose the courtyard. Good. That is what numbers are for: not to kill beauty, but to stop it from happening by default.

What this calculation does not prove

The ledger above fixes the wall at R-13, Chicago at 6,000 heating degree days, and gas at $1.50 a therm, which makes it a single point in a large space of possible houses, climates, and fuel prices rather than anything like a universal constant. Change any of them and the number moves: a code-minimum R-20 wall trims the penalty to roughly $235 a year, a warmer climate shrinks it further, and gas prices are the largest uncertainty in the whole exercise. Windows, infiltration, and solar gains go uncounted in the calculation, and the cited studies show they can shift the balance, particularly where cooling dominates. That $36,500 capital figure is a rough assembly estimate, not a bid. Mostly modeled or multi-family rather than measured single-family stock, the peer-reviewed shape studies leave my ledger as engineering arithmetic, not a field trial. And Forma's energy feedback is a trained predictive model, not a meter reading. Treat the $300 as an order of magnitude with a clear sign, not a quote.

The questions worth asking

If you are designing a home, ask your architect for the heat-loss form factor of the massing before it leaves schematic design. Exterior surface area divided by floor area; if the number is north of 2.5 on a detached two-story, you are buying walls for looks, which is a fine thing to do deliberately. Ask whether the firm runs energy feedback at massing stage, in Forma or anything like it. If the answer is "we do that later," the shape is already locked and the analysis is decoration, arriving with the solemnity of a consultant and the usefulness of a weather report for yesterday.

If you are buying, do the perimeter walk in your head. Every notch, bump-out, jog, and courtyard is a line item on the utility bill that the price per square foot will never show you. In a heating climate it is real money. In a cooling climate, ask for the daylighting trade to be modeled rather than asserted.

And if you are building small, an ADU or a cottage, know that geometry is already taxing you the most. Keep the box as boxy as the lot allows, spend the savings on the things a small house actually needs: better windows, a tighter envelope, and one beautiful room instead of four mediocre ones. The box was never the enemy of beauty; the unpriced L was.
