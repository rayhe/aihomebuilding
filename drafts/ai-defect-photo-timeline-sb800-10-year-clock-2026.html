Your Defect Photos Are a Legal Timeline. California's 10-Year Clock Is Already Running.

By Catherine Chen • October 5, 2026

Take the photo today. Not tomorrow, not when the crack gets big enough to photograph well. The timestamp on that photo is worth more than your memory of when it appeared, and California construction-defect law cares about exactly one of those two things.

SB 800, the Right to Repair Act, is the state's 2002 answer to a very California problem: builders who built badly, lawyers who billed beautifully, and homeowners caught between them. It gives new-home buyers ten years to bring defect claims, which sounds generous until you read the fine print the way the code requires you to. That ten-year clock is really four clocks, and the shortest one may expire before the moving boxes are gone.

Civil Code section 896 sorts building components into warranty buckets, each with its own statute of repose. Untreated wood posts in soil contact and dryer ducts get one year; plumbing, sewer, electrical systems, and hardscape get four; paint and stains get five. Everything else, foundations and structure included, gets the full ten under section 941. These are statutes of repose, not statutes of limitation, and the distinction has teeth. A statute of limitation asks when you discovered the problem. A statute of repose asks what year it is. It runs from substantial completion whether or not you noticed anything, and when it expires it takes your claim with it, even if the defect stayed invisible the entire time. Construction-defect attorneys will tell you the repose clock is the one that kills cases, because discovery is a fact question and the calendar is not.

65% of new-construction buyers uncovered issues during their home inspections, and two-thirds say they regret buying new construction at all (Real Estate Witch survey, via RISMedia).

Those clocks create an information problem. An inspection is a snapshot and defects are a movie. A hairline stucco crack in month three is a cosmetic shrug; the same crack in year six is the first frame of water intrusion. Nobody remembers which month they first saw it, and the builder's lawyer will not take your word for it. Your phone, however, remembers everything. Every photo carries EXIF metadata with a timestamp and usually GPS coordinates, a contemporaneous record you created for free, and courts have spent the last decade learning to admit phone photos as evidence of condition and timing.

Meanwhile the technology for reading those photos has gotten uncomfortably good. A 2026 paper in Scientific Reports trained a YOLO26n model on 6,806 images of concrete deterioration and reported 95.8 percent precision with 97.7 percent recall across cracks, spalling, exposed rebar, rust stains, and efflorescence. A separate group built a smartphone app on an AlexNet backbone that averaged 99.09 percent accuracy on concrete-crack photos, though each image took about a minute to process, which tells you the honest state of the art: accurate, not instant. A Wiley survey of the field documents researchers deploying crack-detection CNNs as phone apps for general users, plus a 7-class severity classifier that hit 94.7 percent on 21,000 images. The research pipeline exists and the accuracy numbers are real. What does not exist is a product that walks your house once a month and files your evidence for you, and close is not the same thing.

Which is fine, because the product can be you plus ten minutes a month. Nobody has published a photo cadence mapped to SB 800's repose windows, so here is one:

Now the math, with every assumption visible. The habit costs nothing: a phone you own, ten minutes a month, free AI screening. Anchor the other side in builder data. From FY2025 10-K filings, large builders reserve between $384 per home (LGI Homes) and $3,808 per home (Toll Brothers) for future warranty costs, per HousingWire's analysis. D.R. Horton, the volume king, resolved 405 construction-defect claims last year, more than double its 2022 count. Independent inspectors put major system repairs at $1,000 to $5,000 and full replacements at $4,500 to $12,000 or more. Run the crude expected value: take the survey's 65 percent defect-discovery rate, assume one defect in twenty is a $5,000 repair that a photo timeline moves inside its repose window, and the habit is worth roughly $160 a year against a cost of zero and two hours of your time. That is not a quote; it is an illustration of how lopsided the bet is. The downside is an afternoon. The upside is a five-figure claim you can actually prove. And 85 percent of homeowners faced an unplanned repair in 2025 while 58 percent had nothing saved for one, per Clever's data, which is the other half of the lopsidedness: most people cannot afford to self-insure the thing they are legally required to notice.

Procedure matters as much as the photos. Under SB 800 you cannot sue first and talk later. You must send the builder a written claim describing the defect and let them inspect it; the builder then chooses among repairing it, mediating, or offering cash. Written notice under section 910 tolls, meaning pauses, the statute. Here is the part people miss: repairs made during the pre-litigation process do not restart the clock, with one narrow tolling exception in section 927. The date that matters is when the defect first existed and when you can prove you saw it, not the date someone patched it. Your photo log is the proof; their patch is not.

The strongest case for the other side

Now the honest objection, stated at full strength. SB 800's ten-year window is arguably the disease it claims to treat. California YIMBY has documented how decade-long exposure, vague defect standards, and fee-hungry litigation turned condo defect suits into a protracted industry, and condo construction in the state collapsed into luxury high-rises as builders priced in the legal risk. A free AI photo-log habit does not obviously improve that equilibrium. It could just as easily arm claim mills with perfectly timestamped demand letters, converting ordinary settling and cosmetic wear into ten years of legal exposure and raising the insurance premiums that get passed into home prices. Add two technical caveats: the headline AI accuracy numbers come from concrete and bridge datasets, not residential stucco, drywall, and flashing, where validation is thin; and EXIF timestamps prove when a shutter fired, not when damage occurred, and metadata is editable. A photo log is evidence, not a verdict, and a classifier trained on highway bridges will occasionally cry wolf about your stucco.

What this analysis did not prove: this is California law, and most other states run shorter repose windows with different procedures, so the protocol's legal bite changes at the state line. Statute citations are verified against current mirrors of the Civil Code; verify them at leginfo.legislature.ca.gov before relying on them, and nothing here is legal advice. The 65 percent defect figure is a 2022 industry survey. Builder warranty accruals are accounting reserves, not repair invoices; a low number can mean cheap homes rather than good ones. And no published study validates phone-based defect AI on the defect types that actually fill new-home warranty claims.

Your move: photograph on the cadence above, same angles, originals kept. Book an 11-month warranty inspection before the builder's express warranty expires; the inspection exists for exactly this reason. The day you find something real, send the written section 910 notice promptly, because that is what pauses the clock, and keep photographing through the repair, because the repair does not restart it. The code does not care about your feelings. It cares about dates, so give it some.
