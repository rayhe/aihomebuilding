Your Builder Lost $2,847 on the Last Job in Changes He Never Billed. The AI That Could Have Caught It Costs More Than His Truck.

Procore processes 150 project variables and predicts cost overruns with 92% accuracy. It also costs $50,000 a year and has never seen a residential job.

By Frank DeLuca

Chad Eslinger runs a three-person operation at Eslinger Homes in the Midwest. On a recent episode of the Beyond the Bid podcast, he put a number on something most builders feel but rarely quantify: his company was losing $2,847 per job in changes that never made it onto an invoice. Forty percent of legitimate scope additions went unbilled, not because the work was free but because nobody wrote it down fast enough.

That adds up. Fast.

That number, multiplied across a year of building custom homes, is not a rounding error but a slow-motion margin collapse that most builders never see clearly enough to stop. A mid-volume custom builder finishing 15 homes a year at $600,000 each runs $9 million in revenue, and at Eslinger's rate, the unbilled changes alone bleed $42,705 annually. Add the changes that do get billed but arrive late, trigger disputes, or eat margin in negotiation, and the real cost of change order mismanagement in residential construction sits somewhere between painful and existential.

Meanwhile, in commercial construction, the change order problem is being solved by machines that can see around corners the humans standing on the job site cannot.

What $50,000 a Year Buys in Commercial AI

Procore, the dominant project management platform for commercial general contractors, now processes more than 150 project variables through its AI engine to predict cost and schedule risks two to four weeks before they cascade. According to a 2025 vendor analysis by Business-Software.com, Procore's cost variance prediction hits 92% accuracy across its commercial project portfolio.

In November 2024, Procore announced it was adding agentic AI to the platform. A schedule agent identifies delays and automatically notifies every downstream trade that might be affected. An RFI agent analyzes conversation threads and recommends resolutions based on how similar issues played out on other projects. "If there's a schedule change that moves painting back a week, that might affect glazing, window dressing and other downstream trades," Procore VP Dennis Jenkins told SiliconANGLE. "The schedule agent will choose and notify all the parties involved."

By June 2026, Procore's Datagrid integration could surface answers from project records before a new RFI was even created, and identify discrepancies between approved designs and what was actually built in the field. Its training corpus spans millions of commercial projects.

None of those millions are custom homes, and nothing in the training data looks like a three-bedroom ranch in a subdivision where the GC is also the estimator, the project manager, the guy who picks up lunch, and the person who has to explain to the homeowner why the cabinet delivery is three weeks late and the tile they chose requires a substrate change that was not in the original bid.

So the residential builder is on his own, navigating a $600,000 project with the same tools a freelance graphic designer uses to track invoices.

What $300 a Month Buys in Residential Software

The residential builder's software shelf looks different. CoConstruct, Buildertrend, and JobTread cost $99 to $399 a month and they handle the basics competently: budgets, schedules, client portals, change order documentation with digital signatures. Some of them are genuinely good at what they do.

What none of them do is predict anything, which means every step in the change order lifecycle requires a human to initiate it at exactly the moment that human is least available to do so. They record change orders after someone thinks to create one, calculate the cost impact after someone enters the numbers, and notify the homeowner after someone drafts the message. In residential construction, that someone is usually the same person swinging a hammer, answering the phone, and trying to remember whether the tile upgrade the client mentioned on Tuesday was a formal request or an idle daydream that will evaporate by Thursday.

Numbers confirm what builders already suspect: New Avenue Homes analyzed their project data and found an average 8% increase from original bid to final cost, split evenly between discretionary changes (the homeowner wanted a bigger window) and non-discretionary ones (the framing was wrong, the soil was bad, the code changed mid-permit). The 2026 Houzz U.S. Home Study found that 37% of homeowners exceeded their budget in 2025, with 31% of those citing mid-project scope expansion as the reason their spreadsheet stopped matching reality.

And those are the changes that made it into the books, the ones someone remembered to write down and bill for. Eslinger's $2,847 represents the ones that didn't, repeated across every job, compounding into a margin leak that no end-of-year accounting review will ever fully reconstruct.

Why the Gap Exists

The obvious answer is money, and it is not wrong. Procore's enterprise pricing starts well above what any residential builder would consider spending on software, and a platform built for Turner Construction or Skanska has no business model reason to care about a three-person framing crew in Iowa running eight projects at a time.

But the deeper problem is data, and it runs all the way down to how the two industries operate at a structural level. Procore's AI works because it trains on structured, standardized project records across millions of commercial jobs: BIM models with tagged components, specification databases with version control, RFI logs with timestamps and assignees and resolution codes, change orders linked to specific cost codes and schedule activities and contract clauses. Commercial construction generates this data as a byproduct of its contractual complexity, the same way a hospital generates medical records as a byproduct of treating patients.

Residential construction generates text messages and a shoebox of receipts. Maybe.

A custom home builder's "project data" is a folder of PDFs from the architect, a spreadsheet with line-item costs that may or may not reflect reality, a thread in Buildertrend that the client checks sporadically, and a running group chat with the electrician where half the messages are photos taken at bad angles and the other half are one-word replies that could mean yes or could mean the guy is driving. All the information that would let an AI predict change orders, such as which design decisions correlate with scope creep, which homeowner behaviors signal incoming discretionary changes, and which subcontractor patterns precede non-discretionary ones, lives in the builder's head, not in a database.

The Math on What a Residential AI Could Be Worth

Run the numbers on a tool that doesn't exist yet.

Take the non-discretionary side first, since that is where prediction has the most leverage. If 4% of a $600,000 home's cost comes from non-discretionary change orders (half of New Avenue's 8% total), that is $24,000 per project in costs driven by things nobody planned for but somebody should have seen coming. Across 15 homes, $360,000 in annual change order costs driven by missed items, site surprises, and coordination failures.

An AI trained on even a modest dataset of completed residential projects could flag the patterns that experienced builders carry in their heads but never externalize into a system. Foundation specs that historically underestimate drainage costs in the builder's county. Framing packages from specific suppliers that consistently arrive with dimensional errors requiring field corrections. Tile selections in master bathrooms that trigger waterproofing upgrades the original estimate didn't account for because the estimator priced the tile, not the cascade of work the tile requires. If the system caught 20% of non-discretionary changes before they happened, that is $72,000 saved across the portfolio.

On the unbilled side, a system that flagged scope additions in real time, even something as simple as matching daily log entries against the original contract scope and asking "was this work in the contract?", could recover a meaningful fraction of Eslinger's $42,705 annual leak.

Now price it.

The break-even math is simple. Five hundred dollars a month. That sits squarely in the pricing range residential builders already pay for project management software.

The Strongest Case Against

Residential change orders are not commercial change orders, and pretending otherwise is how you build a product nobody uses. Fundamentally.

A homeowner standing in her half-framed kitchen, looking at the header above the sink, and deciding she wants a six-foot window instead of a four-foot window is not a predictable event. It is an emotional response to a physical space she has never occupied before. No model trained on historical project data will see it coming, because it is not a pattern. It is a person encountering the gap between what she imagined and what she is seeing for the first time.

The design-build industry argues, persuasively, that the best prevention is better upfront design. Spend more time in 3D renderings before breaking ground, walk the client through VR walkthroughs, and make every decision on paper, where changes cost nothing, instead of in the field, where they cost thousands. Hogan Design & Construction makes this case explicitly: most change orders are avoidable with better planning, not better software.

They are right, up to a point, but even the most thorough design process cannot predict what is behind the drywall of a 1960s ranch, or that the county will update its energy code between permit submission and inspection, or that the lumber delivery will include studs graded two classes below what was ordered. Non-discretionary changes are not design failures but information failures, and information failures are exactly what AI is built to catch.

What Would Have to Be True

For residential change order AI to work, three things would need to happen that have not happened yet. None are impossible. All are hard.

First, residential builders would need to generate structured data, not full BIM models but something lighter and more practical: standardized cost code taxonomies that map to residential work categories, digital daily logs with tagged activities, and photo documentation linked to schedule milestones. Infrastructure for this already exists in tools like Buildertrend and CoConstruct, platforms that already sit on thousands of builders' phones and already collect most of the raw information a prediction engine would need if anyone bothered to structure it properly, which nobody has, because the builder who needs predictive analytics the most is invariably the one running the leanest operation with the least administrative bandwidth to feed data into a system whose payoff is invisible until the end of the fiscal year. What does not exist is the discipline to use it consistently, because the builder who needs it most is the one with the least time to enter data.

Second, someone would need to aggregate that data across builders. Procore's AI works because it sees patterns across millions of projects from thousands of contractors. A single residential builder completing 15 homes a year does not generate enough data to train a useful model. Whoever solves this will need to pool anonymized project data across its user base, which requires trust, privacy engineering, and a user base large enough to matter.

Third, the predictions would need to be specific enough to act on. "This project has a 73% chance of exceeding budget" is useless to a builder standing on a job site. "The tile specification in the master bath historically triggers a waterproofing upgrade that adds $3,200 in your county" is actionable. One output produces a dashboard nobody checks; the other changes behavior on the job site.

Nobody has.

Limitations

The $2,847-per-job figure comes from a single builder's self-reported data on a podcast, illustrative rather than representative. Procore's 92% cost variance accuracy claim appears in a vendor comparison site, not a peer-reviewed study, and the methodology behind it is not public. New Avenue Homes' 8% average increase figure reflects one company's project portfolio in the San Francisco Bay Area, where construction costs skew high. None of these numbers should be treated as industry-wide baselines without independent verification.

Our break-even calculation assumes 20% prevention of non-discretionary changes, a figure chosen for illustration. No residential AI tool exists to validate that assumption. Actual capture rates would depend on data quality, model training, and the specific types of changes a given builder encounters.

What to Do Now

If you are a residential builder waiting for AI to solve change orders, stop waiting and start generating the data it will eventually need. Use your existing PM software to log every change order with a cause code: homeowner request, site condition, code change, estimating error, supplier issue, subcontractor deviation, and do it for a full year. At the end of that year, sort by cause code and total cost. You will know, without any AI, where your money is leaking and which leaks are preventable.

If you are a software company building for residential construction, the opportunity is sitting in plain sight. Buildertrend has 1.5 million users and CoConstruct manages billions in project volume, which means the raw ingredients are already in the building. The data exists. The aggregation infrastructure exists. The AI models exist. What does not exist is the product decision to connect them.

Procore's commercial AI predicts the future of a $50 million hospital project. Your builder's future is a text message he forgot to send.

That is the gap.
