# Research: SB 9 Lot Splits — AI Feasibility Checks vs. Actual Uptake

**Slug:** `sb9-lot-split-ai-feasibility-uptake-gap-2026`
**Journalist:** Catherine Chen (Policy & Regulation)
**Date:** September 10, 2026

## Thesis

California's SB 9 (2021) was supposed to end single-family-only zoning: any qualifying homeowner could split their lot and build up to four units. Five years later, uptake is a rounding error — and the new generation of AI parcel-screening tools (Acres.com's Feb 2026 AI zoning launch, Symbium Build in the Bay Area) can now tell a homeowner in 40 seconds whether their lot qualifies, while the actual approval and build process still takes a year and tens of thousands in soft costs. The feasibility question got automated. The feasibility answer barely matters, because the binding constraints were never information — they were money, occupancy rules, and construction economics.

## Kill test

Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes — directly. A California homeowner with a 6,000+ sq ft lot can learn: (1) whether their parcel passes SB 9's eligibility screens, (2) which free AI tools check that in seconds, (3) what the real timeline and soft costs are after the screen says "yes," and (4) why 99.5%+ of eligible owners still don't do it, so they can decide before spending $20k on a surveyor.

## Primary sources

1. **Terner Center for Housing Innovation, UC Berkeley — "Will Allowing Duplexes and Lot Splits on Parcels Zoned for Single-Family Create New Homes?" (July 2021), with MapCraft Labs.** Modeled financial feasibility parcel-by-parcel statewide. Key findings: SB 9 enables new development where none was feasible on ~110,000 single-family parcels (1.5% of statewide total); duplexes the dominant feasible form; nearly 97% of existing single-family homes retained (no demolition wave); owner-occupancy requirement cuts feasible units ~6% (~40,000 units). Prototype tables enumerate every allowed configuration (duplex conversion, lot split + two SFRs, ADU stacking, up to 4 units per original lot).

2. **California Government Code §§ 65852.21 (duplex provision) and 66411.7 (urban lot split provision); HCD SB 9 Fact Sheet (March 2022).** The statute itself: ministerial (no discretionary review, no CEQA) approval; split lots must each be ≥1,200 sq ft and roughly equal in size (no more than 60/40); owner-occupancy affidavit required (3 years); exclusions for prime farmland, wetlands, very high fire hazard severity zones, earthquake fault zones, flood zones, conservation land, protected-species habitat, historic districts, and parcels with tenant occupancy in the prior 3 years or Ellis Act evictions in the prior 15 years. Max 4 units per original lot.

3. **YIMBY Law 2025 report on pro-housing laws (covered by HSJ Chronicle, March 2025).** Statewide building permits for only **140 units** issued under SB 9 in 2023. Companion findings: AB 2011 produced 2 project approvals in 2023, 8 in 2024. A pro-development nonprofit's own verdict: "limited to no impact on the state's housing supply."

The duplex provision works modestly; the lot-split provision is nearly dormant.

## Original calculation: the feasibility-to-reality gap

- Terner/MapCraft modeled ceiling: up to ~700,000 new units theoretically enabled statewide (CNU summary of Terner analysis); ~110,000 parcels where development becomes newly financially feasible.
- Actual throughput: 140 units permitted statewide in 2023 (YIMBY Law); <500 applicants by May 2024 (WSJ/state data); ~200 total approvals implied by Riechers' one-third share (2026).
- **Realization rate: 140 / 700,000 ≈ 0.02% of modeled unit potential; ~500 / 110,000 ≈ 0.45% of newly-feasible parcels.** The law changed what is legal; it barely changed what is built.
- The AI screen collapses the *information* cost (weeks of code research → seconds) but the *transaction* cost remains: parcel map and survey ($15k–$40k typical for a lot split in the Bay Area), owner-occupancy affidavit locking the owner in for 3 years, separate utility connections, and construction costs that make the math fail on most lots — which is exactly what Terner predicted in 2021 (only 1.5% of parcels newly feasible).

## What AI tools can and cannot do here

- CAN: instant eligibility screen — lot size ≥2,400 sq ft (for two 1,200 sq ft halves), 60/40 balance, urban-area designation, exclusion overlays (fire severity zones, flood, fault, historic, wetlands) from GIS layers. Symbium does this visually for partner cities; Acres.com's 2026 AI does it in plain language for enterprise land teams.
- CANNOT: produce the parcel map (licensed surveyor), sign the owner-occupancy affidavit, finance the split, or make construction pencil out. Austin's Pre-Check tool is instructive as a contrast: explicitly rules-based, not generative AI — and the city is careful to say staff review still decides. The honest version of every AI zoning tool carries the same disclaimer.

## Skepticism / counterargument (full strength)

The strongest case against the "AI fixes SB 9" framing: information was never the binding constraint, so automating it changes little. Terner's 2021 model already showed only 1.5% of parcels were financially feasible — the AI screen mostly tells 98.5% of homeowners "no" faster. SB 450's 60-day review cap and HCD enforcement powers (AB 434, 2023) attacked the real bottleneck (city obstruction), not the information gap. And the duplex provision — no lot split required — is doing nearly all the actual work (569 duplexes vs. 4 lot splits in LA). A critic could fairly say this article's AI angle is a sideshow: the story of SB 9 is construction economics and owner-occupancy rules, and a faster "no" is still a no. The honest answer: the AI tools matter at the margin for the small set of genuinely feasible parcels, by cutting 4–8 weeks of feasibility research to an afternoon — but they cannot manufacture feasibility.

## Limitations

- Uptake figures mix years and sources (2023 permits, May 2024 applications, 2026 firm-level approvals); no single HCD-published statewide SB 9 time series was located. The ~200-approval inference from Riechers' "one-third" claim is the firm's marketing math, not an official count.
- Terner's 700,000-unit ceiling vs. 110,000-parcel feasibility figure come from different summaries of the same analysis; treat the ceiling as theoretical maximum, not a forecast.
- Soft-cost figures ($15k–$40k parcel map) are Bay Area practitioner ranges, not a surveyed dataset.
- SB 1123's 10-parcel path is too new for meaningful uptake data; its impact is speculative.
- AI tool capabilities described from vendor announcements and press coverage, not independent testing.

## Actionable takeaways (for the article)

1. **Screen your parcel before spending a dollar:** Symbium Build (free, in partner cities incl. San Mateo County) or your city's GIS + HCD's SB 9 fact sheet. You need: lot ≥2,400 sq ft splittable into two ≥1,200 sq ft pieces at no worse than 60/40; urban area; none of the exclusion overlays.
2. **If the screen says yes, budget the unsexy costs first:** parcel map/survey, separate utilities, and the 3-year owner-occupancy affidavit — the affidavit alone kills the investor play under SB 9 (SB 1123 is the investor path, with its own complexity).
3. **Consider the duplex provision instead:** LA's 569-to-4 ratio says the no-split path is where the actual approvals are. Converting to a duplex avoids the parcel map entirely.
4. **Timeline honesty:** SB 450 caps city review at 60 days, but survey + map + utilities + construction still means 12–18 months from "yes" to rentable. The AI gave you the answer in 40 seconds; the calendar does not care.
