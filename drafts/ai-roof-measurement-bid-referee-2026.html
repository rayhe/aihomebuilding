Your Roofers Measured 24, 26, and 28 Squares. The Satellite Photo Cost $18.

By Marcus Washington · September 5, 2026

Lynn Cummings remembers when every estimate started with a ladder. At Kasey Dalton Roofing in South Haven, Mississippi, the process was simple: send a salesperson up onto the roof, measure by hand, climb down, drive back to the office, and write the proposal, until the company started ordering aerial measurement reports and the whole ritual collapsed into a download. Now the salesperson builds the estimate before the truck leaves the lot, and prospects, Cummings told a trade reporter, are "kind of amazed."

I keep thinking about that salesperson, not the amazement but the ladder. Falls have been the leading killer in construction for as long as anyone has kept count, and roofers work exactly where the falls happen, which is why every estimate built without a climb is a small mercy, even if the mercy arrives with strings attached. Hold that thought. The rest of this story is not as simple as progress.

You need a new roof, so three roofers come out. Three bids read $19,400, $21,800, and $23,100, and you do what every homeowner does: you line them up and try to compare, except the numbers refuse to line up, because the three roofers did not measure the same roof. One measured 24.5 squares, one measured 26, one measured 28. A roofing square covers 100 square feet, so the spread is 350 square feet of roof that exists in two of the bids and not in the third, and at $400 to $600 a square installed, the going 2026 range for architectural shingles, that disagreement is worth about $1,750. Somebody's tape was wrong, or somebody's arithmetic was generous, or somebody padded the count and bet you would never check.

The referee already exists

EagleView will sell any contractor an aerial measurement of your roof for $18, with total squares and predominant pitch delivered in about an hour, while Hover takes a different route, building a measured 3D model from smartphone photos and promising a roof square estimate within 30 minutes. Roofr, RoofSnap, and half a dozen competitors sell their own versions. This is not experimental technology; it is a commodity with a price list. An independent check exists, and it is worth more than every vendor claim combined. Engineers at Haag, the forensic firm insurers call after hailstorms, had 13 Dallas-area roofs measured by hand and by drone photogrammetry, and against the hand measurements the automated numbers came in between 1.2 percent high and 2.7 percent low, averaging six-tenths of a percent off. That sits comfortably inside the industry's 2 percent accuracy goal. Tree cover was the enemy; the worst case was a roof shaded by overhanging branches, and everything else was nearly dead on.

Where the waste goes

Standard estimating guides start with a 10 percent waste factor for a simple gable roof, 15 percent for a hip, and 15 to 20 for a cut-up roof with valleys and dormers, and those are rules of thumb, which is exactly where margin goes to hide. Run a 24-square roof through a 12 percent rule-of-thumb order and you buy 26.9 squares; order from a measured 24 with a 5 percent contingency and you buy 25.2. That difference is 1.7 squares, roughly $300 in shingles at current material prices, that never get manufactured, never ride a truck to your driveway, and never get torn into a dumpster a generation from now. EPA estimates 11 to 13 million tons of asphalt shingles reach landfills every year. Precision is a climate strategy wearing a work belt. Back to the three bids: three and a half squares of spread at $500 a square installed, the midpoint of the published 2026 range, is $1,750 of pure measurement disagreement on the same house, and an $18 report collapses that uncertainty to half a square. I owe you honesty about the assumption, because the 24.5/26/28 spread is illustrative rather than measured: nobody publishes data on how much roofers' measurements disagree. That absence is its own finding. An industry that charges by the square has never audited the square.

The sales weapon problem

Here is what the vendors leave out of the brochure. Hover brags that roofers who show its 3D model during appointments close deals at 2.6 times the normal rate, so read that number again: the tool is sold to contractors as a closing weapon. The transaction did not get more honest; the presentation got prettier, and prettier presentations close deals. One contractor's testimony cuts the other way, and it is worth hearing, because Jason Alexander runs Sutherland Exteriors in North Carolina, where nearly every job runs through insurance claims and there is no slack for a bad estimate. His company orders EagleView reports on 80 to 85 percent of jobs, and he says they now use 98 to 99 percent of the materials they order. That is the technology behaving as advertised: less waste, fewer return trips, no roof left open over the weekend for want of a bundle. It happens to also be the technology behaving as a cost control for the contractor, not a discount for you. Notice whose pocket the precision lands in.

The man who walked a thousand roofs

My people come from the trades, so I will say the complicated part out loud: measuring a roof by walking it is a dying craft, and nobody's kid should fall off a roof to produce an estimate, but the estimator who walked a thousand roofs could read things no camera sees, the soft spot near the valley that means decking replacement, the hail bruising, the flashing some handyman butchered a decade ago, and for $18 the report knows your roof's area to within 2 percent and nothing about its condition. We are trading a dangerous, knowledgeable worker for a safe, ignorant measurement and calling it even, which it is not: it is a trade, and every trade has losers. One more thing about the referee: that "independent" number comes from an industry that is anything but, since Hover counts five of the ten largest U.S. insurance carriers as customers and partners with GAF and Beacon, the manufacturer and the distributor, while EagleView sells into the same ecosystem, which means both sides accept a measurement produced by the supply chain that profits from the measurement. That does not make the numbers wrong, exactly; it makes them interested. Interested numbers deserve verification, which is what the Haag engineers did and what you should do with your three bids.

What to do Monday morning

Make every bidder attach the measurement report to the bid. EagleView, Hover, Roofr, any of them. A square count with no report behind it is a rumor, so no report means no signature.

Compare the square counts across bids. Within a square of each other is method noise. Two or more squares apart means someone is padding or someone is guessing. Ask which one, out loud, while they are sitting at your kitchen table.

Get the waste factor in writing. Fifteen percent on a simple gable is margin wearing a hard hat. Make them justify it against the roof you can both see in the report.

Remember what the report cannot see. Decking, flashing, ventilation. Those line items decide whether the roof lasts 30 years or 12, and they are also where the real money hides. Judge the roofer on those, not on the 3D spin.

The case against all of this

Now the strongest argument against everything I just wrote. A perfect measurement of a bad job is still a bad job, and most roofing disasters are not measurement disasters but flashing disasters, underlayment disasters, ventilation disasters, which means a homeowner who audits the square count and waves through the flashing schedule has performed the easy verification and skipped the important one, and worse, the report can manufacture false confidence, because numbers this precise feel like diligence, and feeling diligent is the enemy of being diligent. If the $18 photo convinces you to stop asking hard questions, it cost you far more than $18.

Limitations of this analysis

Vendor accuracy claims are unaudited, since Hover's 95 percent figure comes from the company's own blog and I found no third-party verification of any vendor's pipeline, while the Haag study tested drone photogrammetry in general rather than EagleView's manned-aircraft imagery or Hover's phone-based modeling, so treat the 2 percent finding as evidence the methods work rather than proof any single product hits it. Tree cover, stale satellite imagery, and steep pitches degrade every method, and EagleView sells through contractor accounts, so most homeowners cannot order a report directly; the practical move is demanding bidders attach theirs. The 24.5/26/28 bid spread is an illustrative assumption, and the $300 waste figure assumes material savings reach the homeowner, which they often do not. Usually the spread is the contractor's margin, which is precisely why it is worth auditing.

The tape measure had one virtue: it belonged to a person standing on your roof. The satellite belongs to everyone and no one. Use its numbers. Then hire the roofer you would trust with the ladder anyway.
