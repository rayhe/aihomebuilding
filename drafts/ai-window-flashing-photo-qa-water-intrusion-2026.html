He Paid $43,000 for New Windows. They Leaked in Light Rain. The Flashing Was Buried Before Inspection.

By Jake Kowalski · September 21, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Dominic Lampos paid $43,000 for 22 windows and a sliding door. Aside from the house itself, it was the biggest check he had ever written, and Home Depot sent subcontractors to install them. When the first light rain came, Lampos told Tampa's WFLA: it barely rained, and the water poured in, leaving a puddle on the windowsill and a failed job after Pinellas County inspectors reviewed the work.

That story ran in November 2024, and it is not rare. It is the predictable outcome of a specific failure that happens thousands of times a day on American job sites, gets covered up by design, and costs roughly a hundred times more to fix than to prevent.

Two seconds of bad sequencing

Window flashing is supposed to work like shingles: every layer laps OVER the layer below it, so water sheds outward and down. Get one layer backwards and you have built a funnel. Construction defect experts have a name for the classic version: the reverse lap at the sill, where the flashing paper goes on first and the building paper gets lapped OVER it instead of under, so any water that gets past the exterior finish runs straight into the wall cavity.

An expert analysis of residential repair estimates ranks windows the #3 construction defect category overall, at 7.8% of total estimated repair dollars, behind only structural carpentry and roofing, and the repair is brutal: fixing a bad flash means removing the exterior finish, cutting out wet sheathing, reinstalling the paper, and re-cladding. That analysis puts it plainly: these mistakes "can be repaired only by removing the exterior finish and reinstalling the building paper to properly integrate with the window flashing paper." You are doing the wall twice.

Here is the part that should make you angry: the flashing goes in, the siding or stucco goes on, and only then does anything resembling a final inspection happen. Your wall's most consequential waterproofing detail was installed and buried before any inspector saw it. If nobody photographed it in that window, which usually lasts a day or two, the evidence of whether it was done right simply does not exist.

17x–100x Doing it right: $150–$300 per window. Fixing a failed one: $5,000–$15,000 per window once moisture has rotted the wall structure.

Do the math

A contractor in Ottawa prices proper window flashing at $150 to $300 per window, depending on size and complexity. Remediation, from the same contractor, when flashing fails and moisture rots the wall structure, at $5,000 to $15,000 per window. Take the endpoints: $150 to prevent versus $15,000 to repair is a 100-to-1 ratio. Take the middle: $300 versus $5,000 is still nearly 17-to-1. On a 15-window house, the whole flashing job costs $2,250 to $4,500. One failed window costs more than flashing the entire house.

This is not some exotic failure mode; data cited from the American Institute of Architects shows roofs are about 2% of construction cost but show up in more than 70% of construction litigation, with 60 to 70% of defect blame landing on construction execution rather than design or materials. Windows ride right alongside roofing in the defect rankings. Water damage is 18.2% of all homeowners insurance claims, with an average payout around $17,000, and insurers shell out roughly $13 billion a year on water claims while denying about 10% of them, the highest denial rate of any claim category, mostly because gradual deterioration is excluded, and mold starts within 24 to 48 hours. Every number in that paragraph points at the same conclusion: the cheapest dollar in residential construction is the one spent on waterproofing details nobody will ever see again.

Where AI actually earns its keep

So what does this have to do with AI? Not much, if your idea of AI in construction is a drone flying over the site. Everything, if your idea is cheaper eyes on the details that matter.

On the academic side, the foundation is stronger than most construction-tech marketing. A published paper in MDPI describes an image-enhanced U-Net model, built on a ResNet152 encoder, that performs semantic segmentation to detect defects inside window frames, the kind of pixel-level vision work that identifies exactly which parts of an installation are wrong. That research targets factory window frames, but the technique is the point: a phone photo of a rough opening, run through a trained model, can be checked against a spec list the way a tired superintendent never will. Sill pan present, head flashing lapped shingle-style, jamb tape continuous, corner junctions folded rather than cut: each one a yes or no, each one timestamped, each one pinned to the exact opening.

Capture infrastructure is already arriving. OpenSpace, the visual-intelligence platform that maps jobsite photos to floor plans, reported 86,000 projects across 129 countries and 60 billion square feet of imagery in February 2026, then announced 110,000 projects, 77 billion square feet, and 500 million expert-verified labels by September, with a new agent ecosystem that lets AI workflows query that visual record directly. One early user, T&H Investments, said its field inspection process went from 3 to 4 hours down to 30 minutes, with every issue documented by photo and exact location so subcontractors know exactly what to fix and supers can verify it was done.

Read that again, because the phone is already in every super's pocket and the photo is already being taken. What is still missing is a model that knows what a reverse lap looks like and a workflow that demands the photo before the cladding goes on.

What to do about it

The pre-cladding photo clause

  1. Put it in the contract. One sentence: "Contractor shall photograph all window and door flashing details, including sill pans and head flashing laps, before cladding installation; photos shall be dated and provided to the owner." Costs the builder nothing, and costs you everything if it is missing.
  2. Ask for sill pans by name, because a pan at the bottom of the rough opening is the last line of defense and water that gets in needs a way out. If your contractor cannot describe their sill pan detail without hesitation, that is a red flag.
  3. For existing homes, ask two questions: how old are the windows, and was pan flashing used at install? A 15-year-old window with builder-grade install and no sill pan is a wall-rot claim waiting for its first real storm.
  4. Do not accept "the inspector signed off." The municipal inspector almost certainly never saw your flashing: it was covered before final inspection, and their sign-off says nothing about it.
  5. On commercial or multifamily work, budget for water testing. A building-envelope consultant in Florida caught failing windows on a project before storm season through standardized water testing and estimated the avoided damage at over $1 million. Testing finds what photos cannot: manufacturing flaws and assembly defects invisible to the eye.

Skepticism

Now the honest counterweight, because the strongest case against this article deserves full strength.

First, the MDPI paper proves vision models can find defects in window frames in a lab and factory setting. It does not prove anyone has a deployed model that reliably grades field flashing installation across the chaos of real job sites, different cladding systems, different tape brands, different light. Photo-QA companies publish impressive scale numbers and customer testimonials, not third-party audits of catch rates for this specific defect. If you are a GC evaluating this, ask for the false-positive rate before you sign.

Second, and more important: photos catch installation sequencing, not manufacturing defects. That Florida project where testing saved $1 million? Those windows failed because of a rare factory flaw. No photo of perfect flashing would have caught it. Visual QA is a complement to water testing, not a replacement. Anybody selling you AI inspection as a substitute for a hose test is selling you something.

Third, most residential GCs building five houses a year are not going to buy a vision platform. For a small builder, the realistic version of this idea is embarrassingly low-tech: a superintendent, a phone, a checklist, and a shared folder. AI is a nice-to-have here, but the photo is the whole game.

What this analysis did not prove

The $150-to-$300 flashing cost and $5,000-to-$15,000 remediation cost come from a contractor serving the Ottawa EIFS and stucco market; pricing varies by region, cladding type, and how far the rot has spread, and stucco remediation runs hot relative to siding, so the 17x-to-100x ratio is a bounds calculation from those endpoints rather than a measured average across the industry. As for the defect ranking, windows at 7.8% (#3) come from one expert's review of repair estimates, a real dataset but not a national statistical sample, and the OpenSpace adoption figures are company-reported. And the claim that municipal inspectors rarely see flashing is an inference from standard inspection sequencing, flashing is covered by cladding before final inspection, not a surveyed fact. Treat the numbers as directional and strong, not precise to the decimal.

Still, and directionally, the math is not close: a few hundred dollars, photographed, checked, and buried under the siding on purpose, versus five figures to rip it all off and do it again. AI is optional here, but the photo is not.
