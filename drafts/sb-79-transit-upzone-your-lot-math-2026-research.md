## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes. If you own or are buying a home within a half-mile of a major transit stop in 8 California counties, the state rezoned your lot on July 1, 2026. The article gives the reader the actual math: what the tiers allow, what the worked example on a typical 5,000 sq ft lot produces, what could still block it (city exclusion maps, alternative plans), and what it does to land value. Actionable for owners, buyers, and small builders.

## Topic
California SB 79 (Abundant and Affordable Homes Near Transit Act) took effect July 1, 2026. It overrides local zoning near qualifying transit stops in 8 urban transit counties. AI-driven parcel feasibility analysis (the MapCraft/UrbanFootprint methodology the Terner Center used for SB 9) can compute per-lot capacity, but NO consumer tool tracks the moving target that matters most: city implementing ordinances, parcel exclusions, and TOD alternative plans. The gap: the state-law default math vs. what your city actually allows.

## Key facts / numbers
- 8 urban transit counties: Los Angeles, Orange, San Diego, Alameda, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, Sacramento.
- Tier 1 (heavy rail / 72+ trains/day commuter rail): up to 95 ft height, up to 160 du/acre near stop.
- LA model: <=200 ft: 160 du/acre + 4.5 FAR; <=1/4 mi: 120 du/acre; <=1/2 mi: 100 du/acre + 3.0 FAR.
- Minimum project: 5 units; density floor 30 du/acre; avg unit <= 1,750 sq ft.
- Inclusionary: 7% extremely-low, 10% very-low, OR 13% low income (AB 1893); projects 10+ units.
- Labor: no specific labor requirements unless building > 85 ft.
- CEQA: expanded SB 423 ministerial streamlining eligibility.
- Demolition protections: SB 330 / Housing Crisis Act anti-displacement rules apply.
- Cities may exclude parcels (industrial hubs, no walkable path <1 mi, very-high fire zones, historic, sea-level rise) and adopt TOD alternative plans (HCD-reviewed; keep >=50% density per parcel).
- SF: 763,000 units net zoned capacity under SB 79. San Diego: 861,000 required, ~57% in current zoning.

## Original contribution (worked parcel math — illustrative, assumptions stated)
Sample: 5,000 sq ft lot (0.1148 ac) within 200 ft of a Tier 1 stop (e.g., near BART in the East Bay):
- Density cap: 0.1148 ac x 160 du/ac = 18.4 -> ~18 units max.
- FAR cap: 5,000 x 4.5 = 22,500 buildable sq ft; at 1,000 sq ft avg unit -> 22 units. Density binds: ~18 units.
- Height: up to 95 ft (~7-9 stories depending on floor-to-floor).
- Inclusionary at 18 units: ~1-2 ELI or ~2 VLI or ~2-3 LI units.
- Residual land value (Bay Area multifamily land ~$60K-100K/unit, assumption stated): 18 x $80K = $1.44M vs. typical single-family lot value ~$400K-600K -> upzone creates roughly $0.8M-1M in latent land value.
- Feasibility check: mid-rise construction ~$450-550/sf in CA; 18,000 sq ft x $500 = $9M build cost; needs rents/sale prices to clear it. This is where the SB 9 lesson bites.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Upzoning is not building. SB 9 upzoned ~7.5M parcels; in year one LA saw 211 applications and 38 approvals; Terner found ~97% of single-family homes would never pencil. SB 79's bar is higher (mid-rise construction costs, inclusionary requirements, 85-ft labor threshold, SB 330 demolition protections) and cities are actively drawing exclusion maps and alternative plans right now. Most cities could not finish alternative plans by July 1 and are using temporary exclusions instead — the map is still being drawn, which means anyone computing state-law default capacity today may be computing a number their city will override. The land-value windfall accrues to whoever can assemble capital and navigate the process, not to the typical homeowner. The honest read: SB 79 creates option value, not housing, until financing, labor, and local implementation settle.

## Limitations
- Worked example is illustrative with Bay Area assumptions; not a substitute for a project pro forma.
- Exclusion maps and alternative plans are city-specific and changing; no single public database tracks them (this is the gap).
- Construction cost and land-value-per-unit figures are ranges, not quotes.
- AI feasibility tools (MapCraft/UrbanFootprint methodology, TestFit, Gridics) model physical capacity, not financial feasibility for a specific owner; I do not claim any consumer tool has an SB 79 module.
- Demolition/tenant protections (SB 330) can block redevelopment of rented properties entirely.
