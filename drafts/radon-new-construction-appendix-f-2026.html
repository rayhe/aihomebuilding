Your New Home Has a Radon Inlet Built Into the Slab. The $400 Fix Expires When It Cures.

By Priya Greenwood · September 6, 2026

The gravel goes down first. Four inches of clean crushed stone where your slab will be, spread and compacted, then heavy polyethylene sheeting over it, then the concrete. If you have never watched a pour, it is beautiful in a dumb, heavy way: the truck churning, the crew pulling screeds, your house becoming a fact in a single morning.

One piece is missing, and it will not be added later. A three-inch PVC pipe should run from that gravel bed up through the roof, giving soil gas a way out above your shingles instead of through your foundation. Your builder knows how to install it, and the materials cost less than a kitchen faucet. In most of the country, nobody requires it, and nobody mentions it.

Nobody.

$250–$750
What the EPA says radon-resistant features cost a builder during construction. Installed after the pour, the same system runs $1,000–$2,500, plus an electric bill for the fan for as long as you own the house.

The gas in the dirt

The math that makes this so maddening is simple: the same agency that attributes 21,000 American deaths a year to radon estimates the builder-cost fix at $250 to $750, and the fix sits in the building code as an optional appendix, which means the gap between what we know and what we require is exactly one jurisdiction-level decision that most places have never made.

Radon is the second leading cause of lung cancer in the United States, and the leading cause among people who never smoked. About 21,000 American deaths a year are attributed to it by the EPA. It is born when uranium breaks down in soil and rock, drifts up through foundation cracks and joints, and has no color, smell, or taste. Only a test can tell you it is in your house, and only construction can keep it out cheaply, because once the slab cures, the gravel bed you would have vented through is sealed under four inches of concrete.

What the $400 buys

Five ingredients make up the EPA's recipe, and none of them is exotic. A permeable gravel layer, which gives soil gas a channel to move in. Sheeting over it, so the concrete cannot clog the channel. A vent pipe, carrying the gas up and out. Caulk and sealant on every crack, joint, and penetration. And a junction box in the attic, wired and waiting, so an inline fan can be dropped in later without opening a single wall.

Wisconsin's health department has the honest numbers on the passive version: in properly built and sealed new homes, a passive stack cuts indoor radon roughly in half compared with the same house and a capped stack. Half is not a cure, which is why the fifth ingredient matters more than the other four: the attic wiring is an insurance policy. When your post-construction test comes back hot, a fan converts the stack into an active sub-slab system that can drive readings below 2 picocuries per liter. Retrofits were never expensive because of the fan; they were expensive because of the hole.

The map that decides, from 1993

Appendix AF of the International Residential Code would require all of this, and its first sentence is a shrug: the provisions are not mandatory unless the adopting ordinance references them. Oregon did reference them, by state law, in seven counties: Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill, where SB 1025 requires radon-mitigating construction outright. Washington folded Appendix F into its IRC adoption. Virginia lets its EPA Zone 1 jurisdictions adopt it locally, and seventeen have done so, which is a start and also a rounding error: every county that has not adopted the appendix is pouring slabs this week without the pipe. Most of the rest of the country has not adopted it, so most builders in most places are allowed to pour the slab without the pipe.

Across most of that map, what decides whether the requirement applies is itself a map. Built in 1993 from indoor measurements, geology, aerial radioactivity, soil data, and foundation types, the EPA Map of Radon Zones sorts all 3,141 U.S. counties into three zones. EPA officials warn it should not be used to decide whether an individual home needs testing, and recommend supplementing it with local data. Building departments use it anyway to decide whether new homes get the features at all. A county-level line drawn 33 years ago determines whether your 2026 slab gets a vent pipe.

The perversity is that the map's authors never designed it for this job: the EPA's own page says the zone map should not determine whether individual homes need testing and should be supplemented with local data, yet building departments across the country use it as the de facto legal trigger for whether new homes get radon-resistant features at all.

Last year the map got good: a January 2025 paper in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences (Li et al.) built high-resolution national radon maps from massive indoor measurement datasets, found that nearly a quarter of Americans live in the highest-risk areas, and said explicitly that the model could support community-specific building codes. We now have the data to target the requirement precisely. In most places, the requirement itself is still optional. That gap is the story of this industry in one paragraph: the sensing got cheap and precise, researchers can now map the risk at fine spatial resolution, and the rulemaking is still running on a 1993 fax machine.

What to ask for before the pour

This section is actually an instruction. If you are building or buying new construction, put the five radon-resistant features in your contract or specs now, and offer to pay for them. Builder cost runs $250 to $750 according to the EPA, and under $250 when the crew is already doing moisture-control work. Your contract price will land higher, but no negotiation on earth is cheaper than materials-plus at the pre-pour stage, in writing. This particular offer expires the day the vapor barrier goes under the gravel. After the pour, the same pipe requires drilling through cured concrete, and the price becomes $1,000 to $2,500 with a fan humming on an exterior wall for the rest of the house's life.

If your builder pushes back, the line that ends the conversation is arithmetic: the features cost less than the stone upgrade on the kitchen island, they go in before the slab in a single morning, and once the concrete cures the cheapest version of this decision is gone forever.

Start by checking your county, because buyers in Oregon's seven mandated counties or a Virginia locality that adopted Appendix F have the code doing the asking for them. Everyone else is the code. Either way, buy the test kit too, because a passive stack is not a guarantee: the EPA says fix at 4 picocuries per liter or above, consider fixing between 2 and 4, and retest every couple of years. The pipe buys you the cheap version of the fix later; it does not replace the measurement.

The economics stay forgiving even in the worst case: if your passive stack proves insufficient and you add the fan later, the fan costs a few hundred dollars to install and roughly twenty to a hundred dollars a year to run, which means the full worst-case total still lands far below the $1,000-to-$2,500 retrofit you would have faced without the rough-in.

Arguing against the pipe

Here is the case against all of this, stated at full strength. Radon is a parcel-level lottery. Two houses on the same street, same builder, same slab design, can test an order of magnitude apart, because the gas follows soil composition, foundation quirks, and ventilation habits that no map predicts perfectly. A passive stack halves the number; it does not promise it. So one could argue the honest policy is mandatory testing of every new home, with mitigation only where readings fail. Targeted money, not universal plumbing.

Consider also the perverse effect of the zone system. Calling Zone 1 the highest-priority zone implicitly tells the other two zones they are fine, and they are not: elevated readings show up in Zone 2 and 3 counties regularly, in houses whose owners were told, by the code's silence, that they had nothing to worry about.

And the version that should actually bother you: the people breathing the worst radon are mostly not buyers of new construction. They are renters in older housing stock they do not own and cannot retrofit, which makes the $400 pipe in a $600,000 new build a health intervention aimed at the people who need it least.

Uncomfortable.

Every word of that is true. The pipe is $400. Full stop. The targeting argument dies the moment universal costs less than a faucet: you cannot buy a cheaper reduction in a 21,000-death-a-year exposure than plastic and gravel installed while the gravel is exposed. Test-everything is the better policy on paper. Build-it-in is the policy you can get done on the Tuesday before the pour.

Limitations of this analysis

Cost figures are EPA builder-cost estimates and state-published ranges; your contract price for RRNC will exceed the EPA's $250 to $750, and regional contractor pricing varies. Passive stacks cut levels by about half in properly built and sealed homes, per Wisconsin DHS data; they do not guarantee readings below 4 pCi/L. Maps predict potential, not your house; the EPA explicitly warns against using the zone map for individual testing decisions. I checked code adoption directly in Oregon, Virginia, and Washington, and did not audit the other forty-seven states, so treat the adoption picture as a sampled snapshot rather than a census, and check your own jurisdiction's building department before assuming the appendix is or is not on the books. Those PNAS high-resolution maps are a research product, not referenced in any building-code ordinance I could find. The $600,000 new-build example is illustrative, not a market median.

The slab will cure whether or not the pipe is in it. Once it cures, the gravel becomes concrete's memory, the fan goes on the outside wall, and the fix costs five times as much, forever. Before the pour, it is a few hundred dollars of pipe and stone. That is the whole story: the cheapest fix in the entire house, with the shortest expiration date on the entire project. Ask for it before Tuesday.
