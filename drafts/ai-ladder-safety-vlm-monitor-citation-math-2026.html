Your Deadliest Tool Costs $200. Most Workers Who Die on It Fall Less Than 10 Feet.

By Marcus Washington · September 21, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Picture the last ladder on your last job. Fiberglass extension, maybe a 24-footer, leaning against the second-story wall plate. The base sits on backfilled soil because the trench closed yesterday, the ground still soft from the morning rain and the compactor sitting idle by the trailer where somebody left it Friday. The top is not tied off because tying off takes ten minutes and the siding guy needs the ladder for five, which is the entire economics of ladder safety on a residential site compressed into one decision made at quarter past seven in the morning. A kid six weeks into his first framing job, still learning which end of the speed square to trust, starts up it with a nail gun in one hand and a coil of air hose in the other. Nobody watches him climb.

Most of the time he comes back down, and that is the problem.

The Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 1,034 construction deaths in 2024. Falls, slips, and trips killed 389 of those workers, and 95.9 percent of the fall deaths were falls to a lower level. Construction now accounts for nearly half of every fatal fall in American private industry. And the ladder, the cheapest tool on the site, is implicated in 16 percent of fatal falls and roughly a quarter of fall injuries, according to CPWR, the construction safety research center. Most of those deaths happen at ten feet or less. Not off the roof, but off the ladder leaning against the garage. Ten feet.

Here is the part that should embarrass the industry: 38 percent of ladder incidents come down to the ladder not being correctly supported or set at the wrong angle. That is setup, and it is free to fix. Free. Nobody sells you a solution for setup, because there was nothing to sell until the cameras arrived.

A new generation of site cameras claims to watch ladders the way a good safety man does. Companies like viAct, Everguard, and Protex sell computer-vision monitoring that runs on ordinary jobsite cameras and flags unsafe acts as they happen, and ladders are the showcase problem. Hugo Lorenzo, viAct's CEO, told a trade interviewer that vision-language models changed the calculation: "With traditional machine learning, it's very difficult to answer the question of 'Is a person using a ladder unsafely?' You can find the ladders. You can find the people. But to logically reason and say 'Well, that person is fine' or 'Oh no, that person's standing on the top step.' Only the VLM can logically reason and then be like, 'All right, it's unsafe. And here's the OSHA reference that says you can't be on the top rung.'"

That is a real capability. A camera that can tell a worker passing a ladder from a worker standing on its top cap is doing something a motion sensor cannot, the kind of contextual judgment that carried the industry from bounding boxes to vision-language models and that, five years ago, nobody believed a jobsite camera would ever manage. The question is whether it is worth paying for, and the vendors, who price by the camera and sell by the demo, answer a different question than your spreadsheet should.

Run the injury math first, because the answer is not what the vendors tell you. BLS counts 13.9 nonfatal falls to a lower level per 10,000 full-time construction workers per year. For a ten-person residential crew, that is 0.0139 expected falls a year. CPWR's ladder share, about a quarter, brings it to 0.003475 expected ladder falls. The average construction injury claim runs $41,353 in medical and wage costs. Multiply the BLS rate by CPWR's ladder share and the average claim, and you get roughly $144 a year in expected ladder-injury cost for a ten-person crew, a number so small it fits inside a rounding error. That number is honest, and it is devastating to the sales pitch. No camera subscription on earth pays for itself against $144 a year. Read that again. That $144 is an approximation built from all-construction fall rates and average claim costs, not ladder-specific actuarial evidence, so treat it as a scale check rather than a budget line.

The Other Column

Now run the other column. Ladders are the fifth most-cited OSHA standard in construction, and violations arrive in clusters: an unsecured extension ladder, no three-foot extension above the landing, a cracked rail nobody took out of service, a stepladder climbed with the spreader bar unlocked, which means four violations is a routine ladder inspection. At $16,550 per serious violation under the 2026 penalty schedule, that is $66,200 in maximum exposure from one inspector's afternoon. A repeat within five years pushes each item toward $165,514. AI monitoring at one to three thousand dollars a month, if that unverified industry range holds, breaks even against a single avoided citation cycle. It never breaks even against the injury math. Never.

Say it plainly: the camera is a citation hedge, not a safety program. The injury argument is the moral one; the citation argument is the financial one. Vendors lead with the moral one because it photographs better. Follow the money. Your spreadsheet knows.

What the Camera Cannot See

The camera has blind spots the brochure will not name. The leading causes of ladder failures are support and angle, 38 percent per CPWR, and angle is genuinely hard to judge from a fixed camera across a muddy site. No camera can see whether the top is tied off behind the fascia. No camera sees the hairline crack in a fiberglass rail, the crushed rung from last month's drop, or the foot pads somebody meant to replace in March. No vendor publishes detection accuracy or false-positive rates for ladder misuse; the CEO quote above is a demo anecdote, not a validation study. Dust, occlusion, a camera mounted thirty feet from the action, and a February dusk that turns the whole site the color of wet cardboard will degrade everything the demo showed. The demo never shows February.

Then there is the workforce question, and this is the one that worries me. AmTrust's injury data shows first-year employees suffer 44 percent of construction injuries and 47 percent of the comp costs. Those are the workers most likely to misuse a ladder, and they are also the workers most likely to read a camera as surveillance instead of protection, especially on crews where English is the second language. A monitoring system that erodes the trust it takes to report a near-miss, the trust that makes a nineteen-year-old tell his foreman the ladder felt wrong instead of climbing it anyway, can cost you more injuries than it prevents. The camera does not replace the foreman walking the site, and it replaces nothing else. Walk the site. Every day.

What to Do This Week

If you run a crew, and especially if you run a small residential crew with no safety department, no compliance officer, and nobody whose job title includes the word safety, do the free things first. Download NIOSH's Ladder Safety app and make the angle check part of morning setup. Tie off the tops, extend three feet past the landing, and pull damaged ladders out of service instead of leaning them against the trailer for light duty. Call OSHA's free On-Site Consultation Program, which issues no citations, costs nothing, sends an actual safety professional to your actual site, and which almost nobody uses because nobody believes the word free. If after all that you still want the camera, buy it for the citation math and assign a human to review the alerts, because a flag nobody reads is just a more expensive way to ignore the problem.

What This Analysis Cannot Tell You

The $41,353 average claim is all-construction, not ladder-specific, so treat the $144 figure as an approximation, not an audit. OSHA maximums are rarely assessed in full against small employers; gravity, size, and good-faith reductions apply, so $66,200 is exposure, not the typical bill. No vendor publishes pricing or accuracy for ladder-misuse detection, so the one-to-three-thousand-dollar monitoring range is an industry figure, not a verified ladder-module price. CPWR's ladder shares come from 2018 to 2022 data; 2024 shares in that form are not yet published.

The ladder is the oldest tool on your site that nobody respects, the one everybody has climbed ten thousand times without incident right up until the morning it moves. It costs two hundred dollars, it kills at ten feet, it fails most often for reasons that are free to fix, and a camera can watch all of it happen. Whether the camera is worth it depends on which column of the spreadsheet you read, so read both. Both columns.
