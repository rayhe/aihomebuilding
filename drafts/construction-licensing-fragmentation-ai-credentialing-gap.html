Your Electrician Is Licensed in Two States. She'd Need Forty Hours and $4,200 to Add a Third.

The US has 70,000 licensing authorities for construction trades. An AI could map them all in seconds. Nobody's built it.

By Catherine "Code" Chen

A master electrician in Portland, Oregon, who wants to wire a house twenty minutes across the Columbia River in Vancouver, Washington, can't just drive over and start pulling permits. She needs a reciprocal license, and Oregon and Washington have one, but only for general journeyman electricians who completed an 8,000-hour apprenticeship and passed their home state's exam. Master electricians aren't covered, and neither are plumbers, HVAC techs, or general contractors. That reciprocity agreement, announced in August 2020 with the enthusiasm of a trade summit, covers exactly one trade classification in exactly two states.

Multiply that friction across the country and you begin to see the shape of the problem. The United States has more than 70,000 construction licensing authorities, according to Avalara, which tracks business compliance across industries and has identified construction as one of the most jurisdictionally fragmented sectors in the American economy. Fifty states, 3,000-plus counties, 35,000-plus municipalities, and a scattering of special districts each maintain their own applications, their own exams, their own bond requirements, and their own definitions of what "licensed" means in the first place.

Texas has no statewide general contractor license, which means you can frame a house, pour a foundation, and hang drywall without any state credential whatsoever, because the state only licenses specialty trades like electrical, plumbing, HVAC, and fire sprinkler through the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation. A one-week sting operation in Austin uncovered 102 violations, primarily from unlicensed electricians and HVAC contractors working residential jobs.

California sits at the opposite extreme, requiring a state license for any project exceeding $1,000 (a threshold raised from $500 in January 2025) plus a $25,000 surety bond, and the CSLB filed 229 legal actions against unlicensed operators in 2024. Florida demands four years of experience and three separate state exams covering Business and Finance, Contract Administration, and Project Management, with unlicensed contracting treated as a criminal misdemeanor on first offense and escalating to a third-degree felony for repeat violations, carrying up to five years in prison.

New York doesn't have state-level contractor licensing either, so the city's Department of Buildings issued 651,000 violations in 2024, with penalties for unlicensed work ranging from $2,500 to $25,000 per offense, and whether your contractor needs a license, which license, and from whom depends entirely on which side of a county line your lot sits on.

This matters because the residential construction industry is missing 19,000 homes a year.

The Home Builders Institute, working with NAHB and the University of Denver, published a landmark study in 2025 quantifying the skilled labor shortage's economic impact at $10.806 billion annually, a figure that includes $2.663 billion in carrying costs from longer build times and $8.143 billion in homes that simply don't get built because the workers aren't available, because the average labor-related construction delay now runs 1.98 months, because the national housing deficit has widened to 4.03 million units according to Realtor.com, and because one in four of the country's 3.3 million payroll construction workers is an immigrant, many carrying skills and experience from countries with entirely different credentialing systems, and the immigrant share of the construction workforce hit a historic high of 25.5% in the HBI's Fall 2025 report, with one in three tradespeople born outside the United States.

The reciprocity landscape is a patchwork so irregular it borders on parody. Mississippi recognizes licenses from Louisiana but only waives certain trade exams, covering mechanical and HVAC but not plumbing. Georgia accepts commercial general contractor licenses from four states and residential licenses from three, each requiring proof of a state exam, an active license, and a clean disciplinary record for three years. Idaho grants journeyman electrician reciprocity from twelve states but master electrician reciprocity from exactly one: Wyoming.

Arizona passed the broadest reform in 2019, a universal licensing recognition law that doesn't automatically recognize out-of-state licenses but eliminates duplicate training and education requirements for applicants who already hold a license elsewhere. Applicants still file an application, pay fees, and meet state-specific requirements. Duplicate eight-hundred-hour coursework disappears.

Here is where the technology gap opens, and it is wide enough to drive a work truck through.

Every piece of data required to build a licensing navigation system already exists in the public record: every state licensing board publishes its requirements, exam content frameworks are documented, reciprocity agreements are filed, continuing education rules and bond amounts and insurance minimums and disciplinary databases sit on .gov websites and regulatory filings, formatted inconsistently and updated on no common schedule, but present, accessible, and waiting to be aggregated into something useful by a system capable of processing seventy thousand jurisdictions simultaneously.

An AI credentialing platform could take a worker's existing credentials as input and output a map showing where they can work today, which states offer reciprocity, what additional steps each non-reciprocal state requires, and what it costs. That delta between "licensed in Oregon" and "licensed in California" becomes a checklist with a price tag and a timeline rather than a six-month bureaucratic maze.

Nobody has built it.

Cobalt Intelligence offers AI-powered contractor license verification for construction lenders, checking whether a contractor holds a valid license before a bank funds a draw, addressing a compliance problem with more than $500 million in lending exposure. But it verifies existing credentials without mapping pathways between them. Procore, the $12 billion construction management platform, published a state-by-state reciprocity guide on its website that serves as a useful static reference document. Not intelligent. Not dynamic.

A master electrician maintaining licenses in five states pays $10,000 to $25,000 annually in licensing overhead, covering application fees, bond premiums, exam preparation, continuing education, and renewal cycles that never align, and that overhead doesn't make the work safer or the wiring better, only more expensive, and the homeowner absorbs the cost without knowing it exists.

The strongest argument against universal portability is genuine: building conditions vary, and they vary in ways that matter. Florida's hurricane-resistant construction standards are not California's seismic requirements, and neither resembles Oklahoma's wind load calculations. A plumber trained exclusively in one climate zone may genuinely lack knowledge relevant to another, and licensing, at its best, ensures that the person wiring your home understands the specific code amendments your jurisdiction adopted on top of the National Electrical Code.

But that argument supports targeted supplemental training, not redundant full relicensing. If the delta between two states' electrical codes amounts to forty hours of study and a single supplemental exam, the answer is forty hours and an exam, not a complete restart of a four-year apprenticeship verification process that treats a fifteen-year veteran like a first-day apprentice. An AI system mapping code differences between jurisdictions could calculate that delta automatically, recommend specific training modules, and verify completion in days rather than months.

The labor shortage is structural, and the HBI report says so explicitly, and the numbers back it up: Gen Z participation in construction has doubled from 6.4% in 2019 to 14.1% in 2023, which still isn't enough to replace the retiring workforce, and non-supervisory wages rose 9.2% in a single year, significantly outpacing inflation, while employers still can't fill positions because the industry is competing with data center construction that pays electricians $280,000 annually for work residential builders cannot match.

Against that backdrop, a licensing system that traps qualified workers inside state borders protects a bureaucratic structure, not the public. A journeyman plumber with fifteen years of experience and a clean record in Ohio cannot work in Kentucky without near-zero credentialing restart, because Kentucky recognizes electrical reciprocity from only four states and has no explicit plumbing reciprocity at all.

The technology to fix this is trivial compared to the politics, because every state licensing board is a regulatory body with enforcement authority, fee revenue, and institutional inertia, and universal licensing recognition threatens all three simultaneously, which is why Arizona's 2019 law passed only because the state framed it as economic development rather than deregulation, a distinction that mattered enormously to the building trades unions that might otherwise have opposed it.

But four million homes aren't getting built, and somewhere in Portland, a master electrician drives past a job site in Vancouver that needs wiring, because the piece of paper that says she knows how to do the work was issued on the wrong side of a river.

What This Means If You're Building

If your project sits near a state or county line, ask your builder whether their subcontractors hold licenses in your specific jurisdiction, not just "a license." A plumber licensed in the next county may not be licensed in yours. If your contractor says they're "working on getting licensed," that means they are currently unlicensed for your work, and you should verify independently through your state licensing board website. If you're a builder hiring across state lines, the cheapest investment you'll make this year is a compliance audit of every subcontractor's credentials in every jurisdiction you operate in, because the fines for getting it wrong range from $2,500 to $25,000 per violation in most states, and enforcement actions have increased dramatically since 2022.

Limitations

No published study isolates licensing fragmentation as the primary barrier to construction labor mobility, because housing costs, family ties, and wage differentials all play significant roles that no researcher has cleanly separated from the licensing variable. The $10.8 billion labor shortage figure includes many factors beyond licensing. The claim that 70,000 licensing authorities exist comes from a compliance software company with a commercial interest in the problem's perceived severity, though the underlying methodology of counting states, counties, municipalities, and special districts is sound and independently verifiable. This article does not assess whether licensing barriers correlate with construction defect rates or consumer protection outcomes, which remains a critical open question that the available data cannot answer.
