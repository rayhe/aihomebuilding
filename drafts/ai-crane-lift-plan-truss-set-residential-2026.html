Your Builder Rented a $300-an-Hour Crane. The Lift Plan Was Free. He Skipped the Free Part.

By Marcus Washington · September 20, 2026 · 9 min read

At 10:45 on the morning of December 7, 2018, a 36-year-old construction laborer stood on a residential jobsite receiving wooden roof trusses as a crane swung them into place, when the boom collapsed, struck the trusses, and the trusses struck him. He fell with them and was killed; a second worker was injured. OSHA's investigation file lists the keywords the way a coroner lists causes: collapse, crane, crane boom, fall, struck by, truss.

My father ran a union crew for twenty-three years. He had a rule about crane days: everybody eats breakfast, nobody hurries, and the new guy stays off the roof. He understood something the industry's data keeps confirming. Cranes kill between 42 and 44 American workers a year, according to the Bureau of Labor Statistics' 2011-2017 count, while OSHA's own analysis of crane accidents puts the annual toll closer to 71, with twelve crane operators dying in 2022 alone. These are not exotic failures. They are rigging that was never calculated, ground that was never assessed, and lifts that were never planned.

But the planning tool exists, and it has existed for years. It is mature, physics-based, and boring in the way that reliable things are boring.

What a lift plan actually is

Forget the word "AI" for a minute. A proper lift plan answers five questions before the crane leaves the yard. How much does the load weigh, rigging included? What is the crane's rated capacity at this exact boom angle, boom length, and radius, per the manufacturer's load chart? What is the ground bearing pressure under each outrigger, and can the soil take it? What is the sling angle on the rigging, because a 30-degree sling angle doubles the tension on each leg? And what does the swing path clear, including the power lines nobody measured?

3D Lift Plan, the industry-standard software from A1A Software, answers all five in a 3D simulation. You enter the load's weight and dimensions and the jobsite obstructions. It searches the load charts of its 1,400-crane database, ranks the most economical configurations that can handle the lift, calculates outrigger loads and ground bearing pressure under the mats, designs the rigging with sling angles and tension, and lets you simulate the entire lift from any angle, then goes one step further: its 3D Lift Vision simulator puts the operator, the riggers, the signal person, and the tagline holders inside a virtual version of the jobsite with VR headsets, so the whole crew rehearses the lift before the real crane ever arrives. Employers use it to qualify operators for specific lifts. Think of the simulation as the meeting where everyone discovers the power line at the same time, instead of discovering it one at a time with a load in the air.

ALL Erection & Crane Rental, the 14th-largest rental fleet in the country with 30 locations, put 3D Lift Plan on the onboard computers of its entire fleet and offers lift planning to its customers for free. No question in residential construction safety has a more lopsided price tag: the answer is free, and it is sitting on a crane computer in Cleveland.

Truss day on a residential job

Now picture truss day the way it actually happens on most residential jobs, which is not the ALL Erection fleet. A boom truck, maybe a 23-ton Terex at $265 an hour with a four-hour minimum, rolled by the lumberyard or a local "crane for hire" outfit. The driver sets the outriggers where the ground looks firm, the foreman points at the roof, and somebody hooks the first bundle. Nobody has calculated the sling tension or checked the ground bearing pressure against anything except optimism. From the cab, the operator can't see the peak; the signal person waves; two tons of engineered lumber swing forty feet up over men standing on two-by-fours.

Oregon's Fatality Assessment and Control Evaluation program investigated what happens when this goes wrong slowly enough to watch. On that job, the crane operator and the worker on the ground both told the foreman, several times, that the temporary bracing on the trusses was inadequate. He added some lateral restraint boards and kept setting trusses until thirteen were up. When the foreman disconnected the thirteenth truss from the rigging, the whole run dominoed. It knocked the foreman and two crew members off their elevated positions to the concrete below. One worker cutting bracing boards on the ground was struck on the head by a falling truss and killed. Read the report and the detail that stays with you is not the physics. It is that two men said stop, out loud, more than once, and the lift continued anyway.

Software would not have saved that crew, and neither would a plan, because the failure was not a lack of information. It was a foreman who heard the warning and kept going. I want to be honest about that, because the temptation with technology stories is to imply that the tool fixes the culture, and it does not. What the tool fixes is the large category of failures where nobody knew the ground was too soft, the radius was too far, or the sling angle was turning a routine pick into a rigging failure. Those are the failures a free simulation eliminates before breakfast.

Run the arithmetic

Let me show the math, because builders respect math more than warnings. A residential truss set runs on a boom truck at roughly $300 an hour. Four-hour minimums are standard, so call it $1,200 base. Add mobilization, fuel, and the 30 to 50 percent in fees the rate sheets bury in the fine print, and the real crane cost lands around $1,600 to $2,400 for the day. Your framing crew is six people at roughly $35 an hour fully loaded. That is $210 an hour, $1,680 for the day, and every one of those dollars burns whether the crane is picking trusses or sitting idle because the outrigger started sinking into last night's rain.

One bad crane day, one wrong-sized truck, one soft setup spot, one pick that cannot clear the neighbor's oak, and you have burned $3,300 to $4,100 before lunch, plus remobilization, plus the schedule slip that pushes every trade behind framing. Against that, the planning step costs the price of asking the rental company to run the simulation, which ALL does for free, or a software subscription that pays for itself the first time it catches an undersized crane on a bid. This is not a technology adoption problem but an arithmetic problem that the industry keeps getting wrong, because the cost of planning is visible on Tuesday and the cost of the collapse is hypothetical until it is not.

What the software cannot do

Here is the strongest case against everything I just wrote, stated at full strength. A lift plan models the lift, not the jobsite. It assumes the soil bearing capacity the engineer typed in, not the saturated clay after Tuesday's rain. It assumes the operator follows the plan instead of the foreman shouting to hurry because the concrete truck is idling at the curb. Most residential crane work is not performed by crane companies at all. It is a boom truck with a driver who shows up, eyeballs the setup, and starts picking, with no lift director, no rigging calculation, and no documented ground assessment. OSHA's Cranes and Derricks in Construction standard applies to that job whether the GC has heard of it or not. A VR rehearsal is a genuine advance for the fleets that use it. It does nothing for the jobs where the planning step never happens because nobody on site knew it was a step.

There is also a labor question nobody in the software business wants to ask. When the plan lives on the crane company's computer, the knowledge stays with the crane company. None of them learn anything except that the lift worked: not the framers on the roof, not the rigger on the ground, not the kid holding the tagline. My father's generation learned crane safety by standing next to men who had seen failures. As a teacher, the VR simulator is arguably better than war stories. But only if the crew is in the room, and on residential jobs the crew is never in the room.

Your truss-day playbook

  • Demand the lift plan in writing before the crane arrives. Load weight with rigging, crane configuration, boom angle and radius, ground bearing pressure under each outrigger. If your builder cannot produce it, the lift was eyeballed.
  • Verify the operator's certification. OSHA requires crane operators in construction to be certified by an accredited body such as NCCCO, so ask for the card, because a driver with a CDL is not automatically a certified crane operator.
  • Walk the setup spot after rain. Outrigger sink is the failure the plan cannot see: soft ground under one outrigger is how booms end up sideways, so ask whether mats are on the truck to spread the load.
  • Confirm the swing path clears power lines. Electrocution through the load line is one of the oldest crane fatalities on record and it still happens, so measure the distance and do not estimate it.
  • Price the bad day, not the good one. Get the crane quote with mob, demob, minimums, and fees in writing, then ask what happens, in dollars and schedule, if the truck cannot make the picks, because that number is the budget for planning.

What this piece did not prove

Boundaries, stated plainly. BLS crane-fatality figures come from the detailed 2011-2017 dataset; recent years are grouped under broader construction categories, so the residential share of crane deaths cannot be isolated from public data. OSHA's figure of roughly 71 annual crane-accident fatalities comes from the agency's own analysis, cited in secondary reporting, and the two counts use different definitions. No independent study was available measuring safety outcomes from 3D lift-planning software specifically; the evidence is fleet adoption and the face validity of the physics, not a controlled trial. Crane rental rates are drawn from published 2025-2026 rate sheets and vary widely by market; treat the lost-day arithmetic as representative inputs, not a quote. That $35-an-hour crew figure is a national approximation for residential framers. Subscription pricing for 3D Lift Plan could not be verified from a primary source, so the article relies on the documented fact that ALL Erection offers lift planning free to rental customers.

My father's rule was simple: nobody hurries on crane day. Software now does the hurrying for you, the night before, in three dimensions, for free. And the men on the roof are still waiting for the rest of the industry to use it.
