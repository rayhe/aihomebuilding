Your Water Utility Knows Which Homes Have Lead Pipes. Your Real Estate Agent Doesn't.

In 2017, workers in Flint, Michigan dug up 8,833 residential service lines. A machine learning model built by two University of Michigan researchers told them where to dig. They found lead or galvanized pipe at 6,228 locations. Seventy percent.

One year later, an outside engineering firm took over and ignored the model. Workers dug at 10,531 properties. Lead turned up at 1,567 of them. Fifteen percent. Same city, same pipes, same ground. Only the decision about where to dig had changed.

A court order in 2019 forced Flint back to the algorithm. By July 2025, every lead service line covered by the settlement agreement had been replaced.

BlueConduit, the Ann Arbor company behind that model, now covers more than five million service lines across 300 communities. Its predictions routinely identify 80 to 90 percent of lead lines, and those positive predictions are 80 to 90 percent accurate. EPA guidance for developing service line inventories, published under the 2024 Lead and Copper Rule Improvements, endorses predictive modeling in Section 5.5 and cites the Flint research by name.

None of this appears in a home inspection report. None of it shows up in a standard real estate disclosure. When you close on a house built before 1986, the year Congress banned new lead pipes, nobody hands you a probability score.

Predictions built for utilities, invisible to buyers

Somewhere between four and nine million American homes still receive their drinking water through lead service lines. EPA published the higher number in its 2024 Drinking Water Infrastructure Needs Survey. A November 2025 agency press release slashed that estimate by more than half without detailed explanation. NRDC, which obtained the original data through a Freedom of Information Act request, called the revised number unreliable. Published estimates over three decades, from the agency and from industry groups, have ranged from 6.1 million to 12 million. Nobody agrees on how many pipes are in the ground. Everyone agrees lead is a neurotoxin with no safe level of exposure.

Children absorb more lead per pound of body weight than adults. Water can account for 20 percent or more of a person's total lead intake, according to EPA estimates. Both facts have been established for decades. What has changed is that finding the pipes no longer requires digging up every yard in America.

BlueConduit's models use three primary variables: age of home, assessed value, and location. Additional inputs include neighborhood records, prior inspection data, and material of nearby service lines. Feeding excavation results back into the model improves predictions over time, a pattern that held consistently across the Flint deployment. VODA.ai, another predictive analytics firm, ran its LeadZero software for Rock Hill, South Carolina, where individually verifying 30,000 service lines would have cost an estimated $7.5 million at roughly $250 per field visit. Machine learning compressed the problem into a probability map.

Massachusetts, Maryland, and New Jersey have each published formal frameworks for how water systems should build, validate, and submit ML-based predictions. Predictive modeling is no longer experimental. It is becoming regulatory infrastructure.

What the LCRI requires, and what it leaves out

Finalized in October 2024, the Lead and Copper Rule Improvements represent the most aggressive federal action on lead pipes since Congress banned them. Approximately 67,000 public water systems must fully replace all lead and galvanized-requiring-replacement service lines within 10 years. Baseline inventories are due by November 1, 2027. Congress appropriated $15 billion specifically for lead service line replacement through the Bipartisan Infrastructure Law, with 49 percent directed to disadvantaged communities as grants or principal forgiveness loans.

Water utilities challenged the rule in court. Oral argument is expected in fall 2026. In an unusual political twist, the Trump administration upheld the LCRI rather than rolling it back. But guidance released by EPA in April 2026 offered what the agency called "flexibilities" for compliance. Yanna Lambrinidou, co-executive director of the Campaign for Lead Free Water, was blunter: "They provide language that essentially gives water utilities ways to justify not replacing lead service lines," she told E&E News.

Missing from the rule is any mechanism for reaching homebuyers. Systems serving more than 50,000 people must publish inventories online. Progress. But no provision requires that information to appear in real estate transactions. No federal rule requires sellers to disclose service line material. No standard requires home inspectors to check it. In most states, nobody requires any of this from anyone.

Underground, and out of scope

A standard home inspection covers roof, foundation, electrical panel, HVAC, and interior plumbing. It does not cover the pipe running between the water main in the street and your meter. That pipe is the service line. It runs underground, possibly under a sidewalk or driveway. Inspecting it typically requires excavation or a specialized camera insertion, neither of which falls within a general home inspection's scope.

So a buyer can commission a $500 inspection, receive a 40-page report documenting every visible fixture, and learn nothing about whether their drinking water passed through lead on its way inside.

BlueConduit launched an interactive tool called LeadOut Map at leadoutmap.org, built with the Rockefeller Foundation and Google.org. It uses the same machine learning technology deployed in Flint to generate address-level probability estimates. Free. Publicly accessible. But almost nobody buying a house knows it exists. An agent who has never heard of it will not recommend it. A buyer who does not know to ask will not ask.

What a homebuyer can do today

For anyone purchasing a home built before 1986, four steps cover the gap. None is expensive relative to what you are buying.

First, check your utility's service line inventory. Initial inventories were due by October 2024 under the LCRI. Many have been published online. Search your local water system's name plus "service line inventory" or "lead line map." If the line at your prospective address is classified as lead, that is definitive. If it is classified as unknown, that is not clearance. Unknown means nobody checked.

Second, use BlueConduit's LeadOut Map. Enter the address. A property flagged at high probability deserves further investigation before you sign.

Third, request a physical inspection of the service line. A plumber or utility contractor can often identify material at the meter connection point without excavation. Budget $250 to $400 if digging is needed. Compare that to discovering the problem after closing.

Fourth, ask whether the home qualifies for a free replacement. Under the LCRI, utilities must replace lead lines within 10 years. Some are ahead of schedule, using federal funds to replace lines at no cost to homeowners. A home with a lead service line and an active replacement program is a very different financial proposition than one without.

Better data creates winners and losers

Lead service lines are not randomly distributed. They cluster in older housing stock, which means older neighborhoods, which in many American cities means communities of color and lower-income areas. EPA's environmental justice framework acknowledges this. IIJA's funding formula, directing 49 percent of grants to disadvantaged communities, acknowledges this.

Here is the tension nobody wants to name: if every listing in an older neighborhood carries a lead pipe flag, property values could decline. Sellers in those neighborhoods, who are disproportionately Black and Latino homeowners, would bear that cost. Transparency that protects buyers could punish sellers.

It is the same friction that surfaces in flood zone mapping, radon disclosure, and contamination reporting. Better information creates losers, and the losers are often people who can least afford it.

But silence is worse. A buyer who discovers a lead service line after closing, especially one with young children, faces a replacement cost between $1,200 and $12,000 depending on line length, soil conditions, and local labor rates. EPA's regulatory impact analysis puts the national average around $4,700, assuming the utility covers its side. If the homeowner is responsible for the full run, that number roughly doubles.

Limitations

Predictive models are not certifications. Eighty to 90 percent precision means 10 to 20 percent of flagged homes will not have lead. Some homes the model clears will. No algorithm eliminates the need for physical verification.

LCRI's 10-year replacement timeline is under legal challenge. If the D.C. Circuit rules against EPA, the regulatory landscape shifts significantly. Compliance guidance from the Trump administration, while not formally weakening the rule, has introduced ambiguity that could slow implementation.

Coverage remains uneven. Large systems must publish inventories online. Smaller systems face weaker requirements. Rural water systems, serving millions, may lack data infrastructure to run predictive models at all.

And none of this addresses lead paint, lead dust, or lead in interior plumbing fixtures, governed by different regulations with their own gaps. A home with a copper service line can still have lead solder at every joint inside the walls. Service lines are one vector among several, though often the largest single source.

A disclosure failure dressed up as a data problem

Mature technology to identify lead pipes before they poison anyone exists and runs at scale across hundreds of communities. A regulatory framework to replace those pipes is funded, in place, and surviving legal challenge.

What does not exist is a bridge between the utility's database and the buyer's due diligence. An algorithm knows. A water system knows. A seller may know. But the real estate agent, the home inspector, the mortgage underwriter, and the buyer often do not. A machine learning model running at 80 percent accuracy on a public health neurotoxin, producing results nobody is required to share at the point of sale, is not a technology gap. It is a disclosure gap. And closing it requires one line on a form.
