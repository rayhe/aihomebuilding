# Research: In-Drum Concrete Intelligence — The Water Your Foundation Drank in Transit

**Article #959 — Jake Kowalski (Construction Technology) — 2026-09-24**

## Topic
Sensors mounted inside ready-mix truck drums now measure slump, temperature, water additions, and water-cement ratio continuously during transit, and transmit the data in real time. AI/curing platforms then predict strength gain in place. The angle for homeowners and builders: the strength of your foundation was largely decided between the batch plant and your jobsite, by water additions nobody on your crew can see.

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? **Yes.** A residential foundation pour is 30–60 cubic yards at roughly $150–$250/yard delivered. One under-strength or over-watered load weakens the foundation for its entire life, and the driver adding water to make the mix flow easier is the oldest open secret in concrete. Knowing that in-drum sensors exist, that ASTM C94 requires water additions to be recorded on the delivery ticket, and that superplasticizer is the no-strength-penalty alternative gives a buyer/builder three concrete actions before the next pour.

- Source: Command Alkon press release via GlobeNewswire, May 16, 2024 (updated Sept 18, 2024).

Stated benefits: tighter water control → reduced cement per yard, lower standard deviation of compressive strength, temperature regulation for placement.

### 2. MoDOT / Iowa State research on VERIFI in-transit system
- Source: Iowa State Institute for Transportation, MoDOT research summary (intrans.iastate.edu).
- Facts: VERIFI (the predecessor system, now Command Alkon Load Assurance) accurately measures slump and temperature in the truck; can adjust slump automatically in transit by adding water and admixture; adding water in transit instead of at the jobsite or plant does not negatively affect performance. Recommends VERIFI data be allowed for acceptance purposes.
- URL: https://www.intrans.iastate.edu/wp-content/uploads/2018/08/SP14-3-Koehler-Verify-sec.pdf

### 3. NRMCA CIP 26 — water added at the jobsite
- Source: NRMCA Concrete in Practice CIP 26 (via Smith Ready Mix PDF).
- Facts: Rule of thumb: 1 gallon (~10 lb) of water per cubic yard buys ~1 inch of slump. Adding ~2 gal/yard over the design amount can erase the strength overdesign for the specified strength. All jobsite water must be measured and recorded on the delivery ticket; a designated representative should sign/initial. ASTM C94 recognizes automated systems that estimate slump in the mixer and add water/admixtures to a target, provided they record additions. After discharge begins in earnest, no water addition permitted; minimum 30 drum revolutions at mixing speed after any addition.
- URL: https://Smithreadymix.com/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/26p.pdf

### 4. NRMCA FAQ — specifications details (2024)
- Source: NRMCA, FAQ_Concrete_Materials2024.pdf (nrmca.org).
- Facts: ASTM C94 permits jobsite water addition only if slump is below required AND design mixing water content is not exceeded; all sources must be determined, documented, and preferably the limit stated on the delivery ticket; purchaser-requested additions must be signed off. Automated slump-estimation systems are recognized and must maintain records.
- URL: https://www.nrmca.org/wp-content/uploads/FAQ_Concrete_Materials2024.pdf

## Supporting data points
- Per-gallon strength cost: ~1 gallon (3.8 L) of extra water per cubic yard raises slump ~1 inch and cuts compressive strength roughly 1–1.38 MPa (145–200 psi), wastes about a quarter bag of cement, and increases drying shrinkage ~10% (theconstructor.org / Big Apple Group summary of industry data).
- ASTM C94 time/revolution limits: discharge must complete within 1.5 hours or 300 revolutions; no water after 300 revolutions.
- Residential scale: a typical single-family foundation pour runs 30–60 cubic yards; at ~$150–250/yard delivered, the pour is a $5,000–$15,000 line item whose quality is invisible after the forms strip.
- Giatec SmartRock (maturity sensors) and Kryton-type in-place sensors are the "after the pour" complement; the article's focus stays on the in-transit gap.

## Skepticism / counterpoints to include
- Sensor adoption is producer-side; a residential GC cannot retrofit a supplier's fleet. The leverage is in asking for the data and specifying it, not buying hardware.
- In-drum slump inference is a hydraulic-pressure correlation, not a direct slump cone; calibration matters.
- Water addition within design limits is legitimate and ASTM-sanctioned; the villain is undocumented over-addition, not the driver.
- Converge's 30%-faster and 95%-confidence figures are company claims; independent verification is thin.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
1. Before your pour, ask the ready-mix supplier: do your trucks carry in-drum sensors (Load Assurance/VERIFI-class), and will you share the water-addition log per load? If they look confused, that's information.
2. Require on the delivery ticket: max water to be added at site, and initials for every addition (ASTM C94 already requires recording; enforce it).
3. If the crew wants more flow, ask for superplasticizer (mid/high-range water reducer) instead of water — same slump gain, no strength penalty, costs a few dollars per yard.
4. For slabs: specify max w/cm 0.45–0.50 and hold the line; each undocumented gallon per yard is ~150–200 psi you'll never get back.
