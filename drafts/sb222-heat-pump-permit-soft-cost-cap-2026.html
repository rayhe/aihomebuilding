Your Heat Pump Permit Just Got a $200 Price Cap. Your HOA's Veto Is Now Illegal.

By Priya Greenwood, September 16, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

A California retiree waited seven months for her homeowners association to approve a heat pump installation. When the approval finally arrived, nearly $6,500 in incentives she had qualified for had expired. The equipment was fine, the contractor was ready, and the paperwork ate the project. That story, reported by Canary Media this month, is the entire argument for SB 222 in one anecdote. Sacramento figured out how to make the machines cheap; the cities figured out how to make the permission expensive.

Consider the machine first, because the economics are genuinely good and they survive contact with an actual utility bill. Data from TECH Clean California shows households saving an average of $304 a year by switching to a heat pump for space heating and cooling, and $97 a year trading a gas water heater for a heat pump water heater. Heat pumps run about three times more efficiently than a gas furnace, and more than 90 percent of owners say they are satisfied with theirs. A quarter of Californians still have no air conditioning at all. For them, the same data shows adding cooling raises the monthly bill by a median of three dollars. Three dollars.

Then consider the permission. California has more than 600 jurisdictions that can each write their own heat pump permitting rules. A residential permit fee of $300 to $600 is routine, and the plan-check surcharges stacked on top are where the real money hides. Swapping a gas furnace for a heat pump often triggers a second electrical permit for the panel or feeder circuit, another $150 to $400, with its own inspection visit. Plan-check review takes two to six weeks, during which the dead furnace stays dead and the contractor's quote accumulates an "expedite" line item. HOA architectural review adds thirty to ninety days and, in one documented case, $6,500 in evaporated incentives. None of this shows up in the equipment quote; all of it shows up in the decision to keep the gas furnace for another decade.

SB 222, the Heat Pump Access Act, is Sacramento's answer. Senator Scott Wiener's bill passed the legislature in August, 26 to 5 in the Senate and 60 to 8 in the Assembly, and the governor has until September 30 to act. If signed, it does five concrete things. Five rights. Two deadlines. One signature still missing. First, it caps permit fees at $150 for a heat pump water heater and $200 for a heat pump HVAC system, unless a jurisdiction demonstrates higher costs to recoup. Second, it limits cities and counties to one permit per heat pump project, though panel replacement or demolition can still be permitted separately. Third, it bars local governments and HOAs from imposing requirements beyond state code, which means the HOA veto is dead. Fourth, it bans specific busywork for like-for-like swapouts: no site plans, no noise regulations for inverter-based units, no information demands that duplicate what the CF1R energy form already contains. Fifth, it phases in the machinery of speed itself: asynchronous inspection options beginning in 2027, and automated or instant permitting by July 1, 2028, for cities over 5,000 and counties over 150,000.

This is not a new playbook. California ran it for rooftop solar in 2022 with SB 379, which forced most cities onto automated online solar permitting, while AB 1132 capped residential solar permit fees and was extended to 2034, giving heat pumps a tested template rather than a theory to argue about at the counter. Solar soft costs fell as the paperwork standardized, and Sacramento is betting the same trick works twice. There is also new fine print worth reading: contractors using the automated lane must certify that a proper load calculation was performed, so the right-sizing discipline stays in the job, and cities have to self-certify compliance with the bill whenever they apply for California Energy Commission funding. Paperwork incentives, for once pointed at the counter staff instead of the homeowner, which is where the months actually disappear.

Now the skepticism, because a fee cap is not a heat pump, and this bill has escape hatches worth naming plainly. A jurisdiction that demonstrates higher costs can exceed the caps, and cities under 5,000 plus small counties sit outside the instant-permitting mandate entirely, which means the state's most rural installers get paperwork reform last, if they get it at all. Crucially, the instant lane and the asynchronous inspections are the provisions that matter most, and they do not take effect until 2027 and 2028, when a future legislature could still amend them. The federal 25C credit expired at the end of 2025, so the $304 a year in savings is now running without the federal rebate stack that made early-adopter math sing. A heat pump quote in the Bay Area still starts around $20,000, and no permit reform conjures an available electrician out of thin air. Soft costs were never the biggest line on the invoice, and the bill's loudest fans sometimes talk as if they were, which sells homeowners a promise the legislation cannot keep. What SB 222 actually fixes is the insult added to the injury: the months of delay and the arbitrary fees that turned an already expensive decision into a humiliating one.

None of this helps if you do not know how to use it at the counter, so here is the playbook, sequenced by when each right becomes real.

If you are pulling a permit this fall, ask the clerk for the fee basis in writing. Once signed, the caps are $150 for a water heater and $200 for an HVAC heat pump, and the city cannot stack a second permit onto the same project for the heat pump itself. If the counter demands a site plan for a straight equipment swap, or asks for information already on your CF1R form, you are allowed to say no. That is not rudeness; that is the statute, and clerks respond to paper trails.

If you live under an HOA, the architectural review committee no longer gets a veto. It can still review, but it cannot impose requirements beyond state code, and it cannot ban the installation outright. The retiree's seven months should be the last of that species, so keep the Canary Media anecdote and the bill number in your pocket anyway. Committees respond to paper trails, and now the paper trail is on your side.

If your furnace dies this winter, do not wait for the instant-permitting lane, because it arrives in 2028 and a dead furnace is not a strategic asset, but the fee caps and the one-permit rule are the parts that help you now. The load-calculation certification, required for the automated lane, is worth demanding from your contractor regardless: an oversized heat pump short-cycles its way to an early grave, and the sizing is the part of the job that actually determines your $304 a year.

Step back and the shape of the strategy is clear: the 2025 Energy Code, effective this January, already standardized efficient electric heat pumps for space and water heating in new California homes, projecting $4.8 billion in statewide energy savings over thirty years at a seven-to-one benefit-to-cost ratio, and SB 222 standardizes the paperwork for the existing homes those mandates cannot reach. One law for the equipment, one law for the permission. With the next El Nino forecast to bring record heat while a quarter of Californians still cool their homes with open windows, that $304 a year was always real. Sacramento finally decided the invoice should not have a surprise line.
