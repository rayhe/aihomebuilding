## Original contributions (novel analysis, not in prior coverage)

1. **The five-degree gap.** The Harvard/GWU study says injury risk climbs at ~85°F heat index. The proposed OSHA rule required mandatory rests, buddy monitoring, and lone-worker check-ins only at 90°F. The 85-90°F band is a zone with documented rising injury risk and **zero mandatory-rest requirement** under the rule as written. No prior site article connects these two numbers.

2. **True-toll estimate (derived, labeled as estimate).** Apply OSHA's own underreporting factors (14× fatalities, from the NPRM record) to official counts:
   - Construction fatal heat injuries, 2023 (CPWR/BLS): 18 reported → **~250 estimated actual**.
   - OSHA's economic analysis priced compliance; this prices the toll the compliance was meant to prevent.

3. **Claim-cost anchor.** NM data: $7.4M / 1,150 heat-related comp claims (2022-24) = **~$6,435 per claim average**. One average heat claim costs about **13× a SlateSafety armband** ($500); outfitting a 12-person crew ($6,000) costs less than one average claim. (NM average includes minor and severe claims; a hospitalization costs far more.)

4. **The 80°F trigger vs 85°F inflection.** The rule's *initial* trigger (80°F: water, shade, breaks-if-needed) actually sat below the injury inflection; the gap is specifically in *mandatory, structured* protections, which is what changes crew behavior on a production schedule.

## Strongest counterargument (stated at full strength)
The repeal side has a real case. OSHA's own analysis puts compliance at **$7.8B/year nationally** and the rest-break provisions are the most expensive part. A fixed 80/90°F threshold is climate-blind: a Phoenix framing crew works in 100°F+ heat for months and acclimatizes; a 90°F day in Seattle can hospitalize a roofer who never sees that weather. Small residential GCs (the 2-5M shops this site writes for) would have faced written HIIPPs, six-month temperature logs, and paid training time for every new hire, with the US Chamber arguing performance-based flexibility beats rigid thresholds. Messmer's repeal bill says the quiet part: many builders see this as paperwork, not protection.

The injury data answers it without needing the federal rule: states with heat regulations show **lower injury risk on hot days** (the study itself). And none of the politics change the 85°F inflection. A GC who sets a mandatory-rest trigger at 85°F heat index gets the protection the stalled rule forgot, at zero federal paperwork.

## Limitations (honest accounting)
- The Harvard/GWU study used OSHA ITA data from establishments with **100+ employees in high-hazard industries**. That excludes the small residential GCs and subs that do most residential framing, roofing, and stucco. If anything the study **undercounts** residential construction injuries.
- Geocoded weather matching shows association between heat index and injury counts on specific dates, not proven causation for individual injuries.
- OSHA's 7.5×/14× underreporting factors are **rulemaking-record estimates**, not measured counts; the ~250-construction-deaths figure is an extrapolation, not a count.
- The 28,000 figure is injuries *linked* to hot weather in the dataset; senior author David Michaels says the true number is likely higher because most heat injuries are not recorded as such.
- This article does not compute metro-level counts of 85-90°F heat-index days (that requires NWS climatology per market); the gap is described structurally, not as a day-count per city.
- OSHA's $7.8B compliance cost and the repeal bill's claims are taken from the rulemaking record and sponsor statements; the final rule, had it survived, might have changed provisions (the high trigger was one of the most commented-on items).

## Actionable takeaways (the gate)
- **Move your mandatory-rest trigger to 85°F heat index, not 90.** The dead federal rule set the alarm at 90; the injury data says the fire starts at 85. This costs nothing and requires no regulation.
- **Watch heat index, not the thermometer.** The study matched injuries to heat index (humidity is the multiplier). A 92°F day at 60% humidity hits ~100°F heat index; a 95°F day at 20% humidity sits around 96. Your crew feels the index, not the air temp.
- **Keep the temperature logs anyway.** The stalled rule required six months of heat-condition logs. The General Duty Clause is still alive and the NEP's 60 citations show OSHA will cite heat under it. Logs are your best defense, and they cost a thermometer and a clipboard.
- **One average heat claim (~$6,400 in NM's data) costs more than outfitting a whole crew with core-temp armbands.** A hospitalization costs multiples of that. The ROI on physiological monitoring is one avoided claim.
- **Acclimatization week is still the danger week** (see our earlier coverage: "heat-illness-first-week-acclimatization-wearable-2026"). New hires and returnees get graduated workloads regardless of what any rule says.
