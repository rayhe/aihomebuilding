Your Framer Costs $35 an Hour. His Insurance Costs $22. One Fall Bills You for Three Years.

By Marcus Washington · October 4, 2026

The call comes at 10:14 on a Tuesday. A framer's boot misses a rung on the way down from the top plate, and he rides the ladder sideways into the deck. Ankle folded wrong, everybody stops working, somebody calls 911, somebody calls the office, and the foreman fills out the incident report with a hand that is not entirely steady.

Six weeks later the ankle is healed and the framer is back on the tools, but his company's workers' comp policy is not healed, and it will not be for three years. That is how long every claim rides the experience modification factor, the multiplier that prices a contractor's loss history into every future premium, which means the injury is over and the billing has just begun.

A rating bureau, NCCI in most states, compares your actual claims against the expected claims for a shop your size doing your kind of work, and the resulting number lands like a punch, with no appeal to effort and no credit for good intentions. Score 1.00 and you pay the book rate; score 1.25 and you pay 25 percent more on every payroll dollar; score 0.85 and you pay 15 percent less. The number follows the company from carrier to carrier like a credit score it cannot refinance.

Now run the numbers, because nobody does this in front of a homeowner and everybody should, starting with the standard NCCI worksheet walkthrough, which uses a roofing company as its example. Roofing sits at the expensive end of the scale, so treat this as the shape of the penalty rather than your framer's exact rate. Two hundred thousand dollars of roofer payroll at $63.17 per hundred dollars of payroll comes to $126,340 in base premium, and with $525 of clerical the book premium is $126,865; apply a 1.25 mod and the premium becomes $158,581. That is $31,716 a year in pure penalty, money that buys no coverage and fixes no roof, and because the claim that caused it rides the mod for three full years, the three-year cost is $95,148; push the mod to 1.40 and the penalty runs $50,746 a year, $152,238 over three years.

$95,148 Three-year penalty premium from a 1.25 experience mod on the NCCI worksheet's example payroll, before the claim itself cost a dollar (CEIWC).

Translate it to the crew standing in front of you: sixty-three dollars and seventeen cents per hundred dollars of payroll means 63 cents of insurance on every wage dollar. A carpenter earning $35 an hour carries $22.11 an hour of workers' comp at a 1.00 mod; at 1.25 that same carpenter costs $27.64 an hour to insure; at 0.80, the number a clean shop earns, he costs $17.69.

$22.11 Workers' comp cost per hour for a $35-an-hour carpenter at a 1.00 mod, using the NCCI worksheet rate. At a 1.25 mod it is $27.64. Between a careful shop and a careless one: nearly $11 an hour per worker.

That gap is $11,497 a year per carpenter, and all of it is in your bid whether the line item says so or not, because contractors do not eat an eleven-dollar-an-hour penalty; they price it.

Built into the formula is a cruelty, and it lands hardest on the smallest shops. NCCI splits every claim at the split point, $16,500 as of 2026: the first $16,500 counts as primary loss at full weight, everything above counts as excess at partial weight. Construction claims average $40,000 to $70,000, which means on a typical $55,000 ladder-fall claim, $16,500 hits the mod at full weight and $38,500 hits it discounted, which means frequency is punished more than severity. For a ten-person shop, one bad Tuesday moves the number; for a five-hundred-person firm, it is a rounding error. And the market enforces its own ceiling on top of the math: brokers report that some general contractors simply will not hire a sub carrying a mod above 1.75, and owners write prequalification cutoffs into bid documents. A bad mod does not just cost money; it costs work.

Which brings us to your kitchen table. Two framing bids, one $14,000 cheaper. Maybe the cheap contractor runs a clean 0.85 mod and prices like it. Or maybe the cheap contractor carries no workers' comp at all, and the $14,000 is not efficiency. It is missing insurance.

This is where the math turns into liability, and the law draws a bright line. When a licensed, insured contractor's employee gets hurt on your property, the owner is generally shielded; the California Supreme Court said so plainly in Gonzalez v. Mathis in 2021, holding that an owner who does not control the work is not liable for the contractor's injuries. But hire an unlicensed contractor and the shield inverts: under Rosas v. Dishong, the injured worker is not limited to workers' comp and may sue the homeowner directly as the de facto employer, even if the homeowner had no idea the contractor was unlicensed.

Your vetting checklist is four lines long. Verify the license on the CSLB website, active and in the right classification. Demand a certificate of insurance naming you as the certificate holder, with a workers' comp line item and dates that cover your project. Ask for the experience mod and watch the reaction, because a contractor who answers without flinching is telling you the number is fine, while the one who changes the subject has just answered a different question. And pull the preliminary-notice and lien-release paperwork that the CSLB's own sample contract warns you about, because the same cheap bid that skips insurance often skips paying its subs.

Now the technology, because this is a solvable problem and the solution is a camera: Intenseye sells round-the-clock computer-vision monitoring at roughly $500 per camera feed per month, flagging dozens of violation types in real time; Smartvid's Vinnie, now inside Oracle, analyzes jobsite imagery for missing PPE and slip hazards at roughly $600 per project per month. Those are comparison-list figures, not vendor quotes, but the order of magnitude is the point: six or seven thousand dollars a year to watch one site. Set that against a $95,148 three-year penalty for a single claim. The payback math is not close. One prevented ladder fall pays for a decade of cameras.

But a camera is also a foreman who never blinks, and the crew knows it. A union hall will have opinions about who owns that footage and what else it gets used for, and those opinions are not wrong, because the technology prevents falls but it also normalizes being watched. Buy the cameras, but bargain the data rules with the crew before the first lens goes up, or the safety program will cost you the trust it was supposed to protect.

A few honest limits before the takeaways. My per-hour figures use the roofing-class rate from the NCCI worksheet example; framing classifications run lower than roofing but far above California's $1.65 all-industry average, so treat the dollars as the shape of the penalty. Those mod scenarios are illustrative: the real formula runs through expected loss rates, D-ratios, and ballast values that vary by state and class. My arithmetic applies a mod to a sourced base premium; it does not derive one from a claim. California cases do not generalize to every state, and neither do its rate filings: WCIRB's 10.4 percent was its May 2026 proposal, while the commissioner's approved average came in at $1.65 per hundred dollars of payroll, 6.6 percent above the prior year. And the AI camera prices come from a comparison list, not from the vendors' mouths.

If you are hiring a builder, the checklist above is the whole playbook, plus one more line: never release the final payment before the lien releases are in your hand. If you are a small contractor, the moves that matter are four. Pull your unit statistical report and audit it, because consultants who do this for a living find misclassified codes, wrong payroll figures, and claims logged against the wrong policy year with depressing regularity, and every error rides your mod for years. Run a real return-to-work program, because a claim that stays medical-only instead of becoming lost-time is a smaller primary loss. Price one camera feed against one month of a 1.25 penalty before you call the technology expensive. And shop the coverage itself, because carriers apply their own loss-cost multipliers on top of the bureau rate, so the same payroll and the same mod price differently across the market.

Somewhere there is a ledger with your framer's name in it, and every fall is an entry, but no bill ever arrives at the insurance office. It arrives at your kitchen table, folded into a bid, three years running. Ask what the number is before you sign.
