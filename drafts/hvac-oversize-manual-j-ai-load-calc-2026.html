Your AC Is a Ton Too Big. Your Contractor Did It on Purpose.

By Priya Greenwood, January 3, 2027, Sustainability & Green Building

Four out of ten HVAC contractors will tell you, on a federal survey, that they size your air conditioner bigger than the math says it should be, and not by accident.

Surveyors for the Department of Energy asked, and nearly 40 percent of contractors said they oversize equipment deliberately. Their reasons were honest, at least: to reduce callbacks, to allow for future expansion, or because customers demanded it, and two of those three are about the contractor's convenience while the third is about selling you what you asked for instead of what your house needs.

This is the quietest energy waste in your home. Not a drafty window or an old fridge. A machine bolted to a concrete pad outside, humming along at a size your house never asked for, charging you for the privilege every month for fifteen years.

A 40 percent cushion

Patrick Pease runs mechanical engineering at cove.tool, a company that builds load-modeling software, and he describes the old industry tradition without flinching: "You took whatever you calculated and added 30 percent or maybe even 40 percent." The goal, he says, was making sure there was always enough capacity. Enough became extra, and extra became standard practice.

Then the nominal sizes do the rest, because equipment comes in half-ton steps. A load calc says 2.5 tons, the cushion says 3.4, and the truck stocks 3.5 and 4, so guess which one goes on the pad.

Nobody involved thinks they are doing something wrong. Your contractor is buying insurance against the one brutal August week when an undersized unit cannot keep up and the phone rings off the hook. Insurance has a premium, though, and this one is itemized on your utility bill.

What the extra ton actually costs

Then the meter starts turning, and it starts with the purchase: installed cost data puts a 3-ton central AC at about $6,200 on average and a 4-ton at about $7,100, which is roughly $900 for a ton of capacity you did not need, paid before the unit runs a single cycle.

Researchers at the National Renewable Energy Laboratory simulated oversized residential air conditioners across a range of scenarios and found energy penalties of 10 to 21 percent for units oversized by roughly a factor of two, when off-cycle parasitic power scales with capacity. Their fact sheet is blunt about the mechanism: it is not the cycling efficiency that hurts you. It is the controls and crankcase heaters drawing power while the oversized unit sits idle, which it does more often precisely because it is oversized.

Run the household math on a home spending $600 a year on cooling, keeping in mind that NREL's 10 to 21 percent band covers roughly doubled capacity, so a 60-percent oversize lands toward the lower end of it: roughly $60 to $90 a year in penalty, compounding to $900 to $1,350 over a 15-year equipment life, undiscounted, stacked on top of the $900 day-one premium. It costs you twice, once at purchase and once every summer.

Money is only the start of the penalty. An oversized unit satisfies the thermostat fast and shuts off, which means short runtimes and weak dehumidification. Rooms feel clammy, so occupants push the thermostat below comfort to feel dry, and that overcooling creates more opportunity for mold around the air handler. You paid extra for the unit, extra for the energy, and the reward is a damper house.

Zoom out and the grid feels it too. A Florida study cited by DOE tied oversized units to 13 percent higher summer peak electrical demand. Your ton too big is a rounding error; a few million of them shape the peak that utilities build power plants around.

What the code already demands

Here is the part that should embarrass the industry. A load calculation exists with a name, a standard number, and the force of law.

ACCA Manual J, 8th edition, is the ANSI-recognized national standard for residential HVAC load calculations, and ACCA notes it is required by national building codes and most state and local jurisdictions. Manual S then governs equipment selection from those loads. Under the International Residential Code's mechanical section, M1401.3, heating and cooling equipment shall be sized in accordance with ACCA Manual S based on loads calculated per ACCA Manual J. ENERGY STAR's new-homes program goes further, demanding a full HVAC Design Report with room-by-room Manual J loads before a home can be certified.

Counties like Larimer County, Colorado enforce it at the permit counter, requiring Manual J and S with the plan submittal down to specifying approved software and county design temperatures. None of that paperwork is exotic; it is just paperwork, and in too many replacement jobs nobody asks for it.

That is the enforcement gap: new construction with a permit and an ENERGY STAR rater gets a documented load calc, while a 20-year-old condenser dying in July gets a sales call, a tape measure waved at the old nameplate, and whatever tonnage the truck has. And the most common HVAC transaction in America is the least calculated one.

Software ate the excuse

The old defense of the cushion was labor: a proper Manual J took hours of measuring, looking up heat-transfer multipliers, and grinding through the CLTD method by hand, and that defense is expiring.

ACCA-approved applications from Wrightsoft, Elite, and Kwik Model 3D have digitized the procedure for years. cove.tool's load modeling, tested against ASHRAE Standard 140, lets designers iterate on right-sizing instead of padding. And the newest entrants are putting AI agents directly on top of the workflow: HVAKR, a California startup out of the Techstars 2024 cohort, unifies load calculations and duct layouts in one cloud platform with an AI agent assisting the engineer, and is now onboarding MEP consultancies.

Where this goes is obvious, because measuring a house, pulling its orientation and envelope data, and producing a documented load is becoming a minutes-long task instead of an afternoon. When the calc is cheap, the only remaining reason to skip it is that the answer might shrink the sale.

A $900 question, answered with a table

Take a 2,000-square-foot home with a true Manual J cooling load of 30,000 Btu/h, or 2.5 tons. Apply the traditional 30 to 40 percent cushion, round up to the next nominal size, and a 4-ton unit lands on the pad. That is 60 percent oversize, 45 points past the Manual S limit of 115 percent of cooling load for straight air conditioners. Now the arbitrage. A mechanical engineering firm will produce an independent Manual J, D, S, and T package for under $1,000, according to Fine Homebuilding's reporting, and that report costs less than the single unneeded ton it prevents, before counting a dollar of the energy penalty. It is the cheapest line item in the entire project and the one most often skipped.

An exception that proves the rule

Intellectual honesty requires the counterargument at full strength, and it comes from NREL itself.

In a separate study on heat-pump sizing, NREL researchers found that oversizing variable-capacity heat pumps should not be discouraged from a heating-season perspective, because those oversized units cut the hours spent in electric resistance backup heat, which is ruinously inefficient, and posted a 7.4 percent seasonal efficiency gain plus a 10.1 percent heating peak-demand reduction. In heating-dominated climates, a moderately oversized variable-speed heat pump is good engineering, not sloppiness.

Manual S agrees in principle: it allows heat pumps 115 percent of cooling load in cooling climates and 125 percent in heating climates, and furnaces 100 to 140 percent of heating load, so a 3-ton load taking a 3.5-ton unit sits just over the line and margin is not the scandal.

Contractors also have one honest defense: Manual J inputs are estimated. Infiltration rates, duct leakage, and occupant behavior are guesses unless someone runs a blower door and a duct blaster, so a calculation built on guessed inputs has error bars and margin against error bars is rational. But a 40 percent cushion plus round-up-to-stock is not margin against error bars; it is a business model.

What this analysis does not prove

NREL's 10 to 21 percent penalty comes from simulation, published in 2014, modeling fixed-capacity equipment, and that penalty hinges on off-cycle parasitic power existing and scaling with capacity; in systems where it is minimal, the same study says oversizing has negligible energy effect. Modern variable-speed equipment changes the cycling picture substantially, and the study does not model it.

Those cost figures are national averages from contractor cost databases, and they swing wildly by market, brand tier, and efficiency rating: your 3-to-4-ton step might be $400 or $2,000. That $600 annual cooling spend in the worked example is illustrative; a mild-climate home with a $200 cooling bill faces a smaller absolute penalty, though the percentage holds.

AI tooling is the least proven link: HVAKR serves MEP consultancies, not the residential replacement market, and none of the current AI-assisted platforms is yet the thing that files an ACCA-compliant Manual J with your permit office, though the trajectory is clear and the product is not here yet.

What to do before anyone orders equipment

Demand the documented Manual J, room by room, and the Manual S equipment selection. In most jurisdictions the code already requires it, so you are asking for the paper the inspector is supposed to see, not a favor.

Check the nameplate math yourself: multiply the unit's tons by 12,000 to get Btu/h, then divide by the Manual J total cooling load. Over 115 percent for straight AC, or 125 percent for a heat pump in a heating climate, and you are buying capacity the standard says you do not need.

If the contractor will not produce a load calc, hire the calc separately. Under $1,000 for an independent Manual J package is less than the $900 average premium on one unneeded ton. That is not a close call.

Watch the failure moment, because when a condenser dies in August, the pressure is to replace like-for-like by tomorrow. That old unit's tonnage was probably already oversized, which means the nameplate on the rusted box is the last number you should trust. Size to the calculation, not to the corpse.

And if you are shopping heat pumps for a cold climate, use the exception wisely. A modestly oversized variable-capacity unit earns its keep in February. Do not let a contractor stretch that exception into two extra tons of single-stage air conditioning. Physics rewards the heat pump and punishes the AC, and your utility bill knows the difference.
