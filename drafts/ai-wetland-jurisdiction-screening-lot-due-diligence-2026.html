The Supreme Court Deregulated Your Wetland in 2023. Your State Claimed It in 2025. Comments on the Rewrite Close Friday.

By Catherine Chen · October 7, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

In 2004, Chantell and Michael Sackett bought a small lot near Priest Lake, Idaho, for about $23,000, and started backfilling it to build a home. A compliance order arrived instead, from the EPA. According to the agency, the lot contained federally protected wetlands. It ordered the Sacketts to restore the site immediately and threatened penalties of more than $40,000 a day if they did not comply. They sued. On May 25, 2023, the Supreme Court ruled for them 9-0, adopting a "continuous surface connection" test for federal wetland jurisdiction and throwing out the broader "significant nexus" test the agencies had relied on.

It was a clean win that changed almost nothing about your risk as a buyer, which is the part worth sitting with, because the ruling answered a question about federal power while leaving every state program, every adjacent-area rule, and every buyer's due-diligence problem exactly where it found them. Nine justices agreed on the law. None of them could say what it meant for your lot, because that was never the question before them.

Nine to Zero, and Then the Patchwork

Under Sackett, only wetlands with a continuous surface connection to relatively permanent, standing, or continuously flowing waters fall under the Clean Water Act. In September 2023 the agencies issued a conforming rule stripping the significant nexus test from the regulations, and as of this month that 2023 rule is still the legal definition of "waters of the United States." The homebuilding industry's response was not gratitude. NAHB's chairman called the amended rule "a blow to housing affordability" that "assures continued uncertainty regarding federal jurisdiction," which tells you something about how much the narrowing actually narrowed.

Federal withdrawal created a vacuum, and states rushed to fill it. Nobody mentions this part at the celebration. A federal "no" is not a state "yes." Your lot can be free of federal jurisdiction and still be unbuildable, because the state program never left, never paused, and never needed Washington's permission to keep regulating the mud at the back of your parcel.

The Map Is Now a Suggestion

New York is the clearest example, and the numbers are startling. Until recently, the state Department of Environmental Conservation only regulated freshwater wetlands of 12.4 acres or more that appeared on DEC's official maps. In April 2022, the legislature passed a rewrite that took effect January 1, 2025, and it dismantled that framework almost entirely. Legally, the old maps are now "informational," which is Albany's polite way of saying they do not determine anything. Instead there is a rebuttable presumption that any area meeting the definition of a freshwater wetland is regulated, mapped or not. Wetlands of any size can be regulated if they meet one of eleven "unusual importance" criteria, which include sitting in an urban area or in a place that has flooded before or is expected to flood. And on January 1, 2028, the size threshold drops from 12.4 acres to 7.4 acres, a 40 percent cut that newly captures every wetland in the 7.4-to-12.4-acre band. DEC's own regulatory analysis acknowledges the changes will "indirectly result in increases in wetland permitting." No kidding. Albany expanded its reach while its maps went decorative.

Pay attention to the mechanism, because it is the future everywhere: the state now leans on remote sensing to find wetlands the old maps missed. DEC's guidance says the department will rely primarily on aerial imagery and remote data to determine whether regulated wetlands sit on a parcel. From orbit, the state is reading your lot. You might as well read it yourself first.

The Ring Around the Wetland

Here is the arithmetic that ruins lot purchases, and it is the piece most buyers never do. New York regulates not just the wetland but a 100-foot "adjacent area" around it. Take a roughly circular 2-acre wetland: its radius is about 167 feet, so the regulated footprint extends to a radius of 267 feet, covering roughly 5.1 acres. Only about 39 percent of the regulated land is the wetland you can see. On a one-acre lot, a modest wet corner can consume the entire buildable envelope, and you will discover this after the survey, after the offer, after the earnest money is nonrefundable, which is to say the whole transaction was priced on the assumption that the wet corner was decoration, and nobody in the chain, not the agent, not the seller, not the title company, was paid to check whether the state agreed.

Run the same math in reverse and the market starts to look rational, because the price ladder from a $450 desktop review to a $20,000 full delineation is really a ladder of timing, and every rung you climb after closing costs roughly ten times the rung before it. Environmental consultants will review the maps and databases for a lot for about $450, or send a scientist out for a field assessment for $600 to $1,200. A full delineation for a parcel under five acres runs $4,000 to $5,000, and larger sites reach $15,000 to $20,000. A Corps jurisdictional determination takes 45 to 60 days on the agency's own goal, then the county permit adds another two to four weeks. That $450 option exists precisely for people who have not bought the land yet. Buying first and delineating later is the most expensive possible order of operations, the equivalent of skipping the home inspection and hiring the structural engineer after the foundation cracks, and it is the one most buyers choose, because nobody told them the $450 option existed.

A Machine That Reads Swamps

The screening technology has quietly caught up with the regulatory need. Researchers in Alberta trained machine-learning and deep-learning models on airborne LiDAR, orthophotography, and Sentinel-1, Sentinel-2, and PlanetScope satellite data, and the U-Net convolutional neural network classified wetland versus upland at better than 90 percent accuracy, with LiDAR integration adding up to 13 percentage points. A Florida study combining drone-derived multispectral imagery with satellite data hit 77.4 percent accuracy across five wetland community types. These are research-grade numbers, and the commercial screening tools that borrow from them are good enough to answer the only question a pre-offer buyer has: is there something here that will trigger a $5,000 delineation?

Be clear about what the machine cannot do: no model issues a jurisdictional determination, at no confidence score, under no reading of the statute, because the law does not run on probabilities. Only a field delineation confirmed by the agency does that, and the AI is a triage tool, not a permit. But triage is exactly what the buying process needs. For a few hundred dollars, that same class of remote sensing can regulate your own risk before the state regulates your lot. There is something satisfying about the symmetry.

The Other Side of the Argument

Now the strongest case against everything above, stated fairly. On one point the homebuilding industry is not wrong: jurisdictional whiplash is a tax on housing. Federal rules have swung through at least four definitions of "waters of the United States" since 2015, and every swing reprices land, restarts permitting, and lands on the buyer's closing statement. Those 220,000 comments show genuine appetite among regulated parties for bright, narrow, predictable federal lines, and predictable lines have real economic value even when they are drawn stingily. A 50-state patchwork may well be worse than one federal rule. A builder working across five states now tracks five wetland programs, five adjacent-area rules, five exemption lists, five appeal processes, five definitions of the same mud. Instead of one. New York's rebuttable presumption flips the burden onto the landowner to prove a negative, which is an expensive posture for a small builder to occupy.

And the AI screening pitch deserves its own skepticism. Recommending that every buyer spend $450 on remote sensing to discover what the state already regulates is a private tax on a public information failure. If DEC can see the wetland from aerial imagery, and DEC can, since its own guidance says so, then DEC could publish a current, honest map, and the fact that it does not, choosing instead to regulate by rebuttable presumption while the landowner pays for the mapping, tells you everything about who bears the cost of the state's convenience. Albany let the old maps go informational because it could not keep them accurate; the buyer is now paying to do the mapping work the agency declined to maintain. None of this is the technology's fault. But do not confuse a clever workaround with a solved problem.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The adjacent-area arithmetic uses New York's 100-foot buffer and an idealized circular wetland; irregular shapes, streams, and tidal wetlands change the geometry, and other states use different buffers or none. Those AI accuracy figures come from research studies in Alberta and central Florida, not from the commercial screening product a buyer would actually purchase, and vendor claims about those products were not independently verified, which means the honest statement of the evidence is that the technology works in the lab on real landscapes, at high accuracy, under researcher supervision, and that the commercial version you would actually buy inherits the method but not the proof. New York's free jurisdictional-determination program is documented in DEC's regulatory filings, but current turnaround times were not confirmed. That 45-to-60-day Corps figure is the agency's stated goal and consultant experience, not a guarantee. As of this writing, the September 2026 supplemental WOTUS proposal is not final, and its final form, effective date, and litigation prospects are unknown. Penalty figures reflect the statutory maximums and the EPA's threat in the Sackett record, not typical enforcement outcomes against individual homebuilders.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you are buying a lot anywhere near water, a low spot, or a floodplain, spend the $450 on a desktop wetland screen before the offer, not the $5,000 delineation after closing. Order it during the inspection window and make the purchase contingent on the result. In New York, request a DEC jurisdictional determination, which the state will perform free of charge and which is valid for five years; that single document is worth more than any map you can buy. Wherever you are building, check the state program, not just the federal one, because post-Sackett the federal map shrinking is exactly when state enforcement grows teeth. And if federal jurisdiction affects your projects, the comment period on the September supplemental proposal closes October 9, which is Friday. By law, the agencies are required to read what you send. Whether they listen is a separate question, but the docket is open and the deadline is real. And silence is also a comment.
