Remodeling

A $4,672 Garage Door Returns $12,526. A $161,511 Kitchen Returns $57,380. Your AI Estimator's Error Bar Decides the Rest.

By Frank DeLuca • October 5, 2026

Twenty years of watching projects go sideways teaches you to read a bid the way a doctor reads a chart, looking for the number that will kill the patient. Last spring a homeowner showed me two numbers for the same kitchen: an AI estimate of $31,000 generated in four minutes from a phone photo, and a contractor bid of $47,000 generated after three site visits and one very long conversation about the subfloor. Both numbers were sincere. Only one of them was going to be right, and the difference between them was larger than the entire profit the project could ever return at resale.

That gap is the whole story of AI in remodeling right now. Seventy percent of American homeowners plan to renovate in the next year, and nearly three-quarters of them say they wish they understood the return on investment before they start, according to a Realtor.com and CensusWide survey. Into that confusion has walked a generation of AI estimators that promise to price your project from your address and a few photos. What none of them will tell you is that their own stated accuracy is wider than the margin on the only interior remodel worth doing.

A table that ends arguments

Every year, Zonda publishes the Cost vs. Value Report, now in its 38th edition, and it remains the most rigorous dataset remodeling has. Costs come from Verisk's XactRemodel estimating engine, the same pricing database contractors and insurance adjusters use. Resale values come from surveys of real estate professionals in 119 markets. Twenty-eight projects, one question: what does this add to the sale price?

Read the 2025 table from the top and a pattern hits you like a two-by-four. Eight of the ten highest-returning projects are exterior replacements. A garage door ($4,672) returns $12,526, or 268 percent. A steel entry door returns 216 percent. Manufactured stone veneer returns 208 percent. Fiber-cement siding returns 114 percent. Only one interior project clears 100 percent: the minor kitchen remodel at $28,458 in, $32,141 out, 113 percent. Everything else inside the house loses money at resale, some of it spectacularly. A midrange major kitchen ($81,076) returns 50 cents on the dollar. An upscale kitchen ($161,511) returns $57,380, a 35.5 percent recovery that vaporizes $104,131. An upscale primary suite addition costs $338,323 and returns $63,767. The homeowner pays $274,556 for the privilege of a nicer bedroom. Zonda's Todd Tomalak put it plainly: exterior improvements drive value for sellers, while interior projects make sense for people planning to stay.

Buyers decide an enormous amount in the first thirty seconds from the curb, and the resale data keeps capturing that reality year after year. Minor scope beats major scope on nearly every interior line item. A $28,000 kitchen refresh returns 113 percent. A $164,000 gut returns 36 percent. Nobody ever lost money underestimating how little buyers care about your custom cabinetry.

What the machines promise

Kukun calls its estimator the Blue Book for renovations. CEO Raf Howery says it uses the same data construction companies use to set prices each year, and the company targets 80 percent accuracy. Snap a photo of a room in the Kukun Homes app and the AI reads its condition, then prices the renovation at three finish levels. Realtor.com liked the pitch enough to build a Renovation Calculator on Kukun's data directly into its My Home dashboard. Plunk goes further, combining AI, machine learning, and computer vision to produce real-time valuations and a "Plunk Value" score for your home's upside, project by project.

Eighty percent accuracy sounds reassuring until you run it against the table above. Here is the arithmetic nobody in the estimator business volunteers.

When the error bar eats the margin

Take Kukun at its word: estimates land within roughly 20 percent of the true cost, in either direction. Apply that band to the Cost vs. Value numbers.

A minor kitchen costs $28,458 and returns $32,141, a profit of $3,683. If the AI estimate runs 20 percent high, the implied cost becomes $34,150 and the return drops to 94 percent, below break-even. If it runs 20 percent low, the implied cost is $22,766 and the return jumps to 141 percent. That uncertainty band, about $5,700 in either direction, is wider than the project's entire $3,683 profit. On the single interior project that pays for itself, the AI's stated accuracy is not good enough to tell you whether it pays for itself.

Now run the same band on the garage door. Cost $4,672, value $12,526, profit $7,854. An estimate 20 percent high still leaves a 224 percent return. That conclusion survives the error bar with room to spare. And the upscale kitchen? Cost $161,511, value $57,380. Even an estimate 20 percent low, the rosiest possible read, produces a 44 percent return. This one survives too: it destroys money under every plausible estimate.

That is the decision rule, and it is the original finding worth carrying out of this article. AI cost estimates are decision-grade precisely where the decision is already obvious, and decorative where the decision is close. Tighter margins mean less room for estimator error, and the only profitable interior project has the tightest margin of all. For anything near the break-even line, you do not need an estimate. You need three bids and a contractor who has opened up enough walls to know what the subfloor looks like.

What the table does not see

Two honest complications before anyone wires money based on a table. First, the Cost vs. Value resale figures are real estate professionals' estimates of value added, not measured transaction premiums, and regional variance is violent. Zillow's read of the report shows a minor kitchen returning 129 percent in the Pacific region ($29,728 in, $38,384 out, a profit of $8,656) and 94.5 percent in the West North Central ($27,880 in, $26,333 out, a loss of $1,547). Same scope, a $10,203 swing in net outcome, determined by zip code. A national-average AI estimate cannot see your market, and your market is the only one that prices your house.

Second, a competing dataset disagrees with Zonda, and the disagreement deserves daylight. Researchers at Today's Homeowner scoured 70,000 Redfin listings to estimate value added from actual sale prices across 34 project types. Their finding: only one project broke even, the garage door, and the average project returned 69 percent. Upscale kitchens and baths recovered about half. A finished basement returned 23 percent, dead last. Carlos Martín of Harvard's Joint Center for Housing Studies, who watches this industry for a living, called resale-value estimation "much more of an art than a science." When the two best datasets in the business disagree by forty points on the average project, treat every ROI figure as a compass reading, not a survey stake.

Arguing against myself, at full strength

Here is the uncomfortable truth the table buries: return on investment is the wrong metric for most remodelers, because most remodelers are not selling. According to the National Association of Realtors' 2025 Remodeling Impact Report, only 18 percent of homeowners remodeled to prepare a sale. Everyone else remodeled for themselves: worn finishes, better energy efficiency, a kitchen that finally works. NAR's "Joy Scores" rank the primary bedroom suite, the kitchen, and a new roof at a perfect 10 out of 10 for homeowner happiness. Bathroom renovations score 9.8. Those two projects are the ones the ROI table calls the worst investments, and they are the two projects owners love most. Deputy chief economist Jessica Lautz called it "the disparity between the joy experienced post-remodel and the actual cost recovery," which is the polite way of saying the spreadsheet misses the point.

A family that cooks dinner together for a decade in an upscale kitchen got its money's worth in a currency no report measures. A garage door returning 268 percent is still a door you will never once admire. If you are staying, optimize for the joy score and let the next owner worry about the table. If you are selling within two years, work the table from the top down and do not let a designer talk you into custom anything. Honest answers depend on your timeline, and anyone selling you a single number for both situations is selling you something.

One more skepticism, aimed at the estimators themselves. Kukun's business ends in contractor matching, complete with a "Kukun score" for pros. That estimate is the top of a lead-generation funnel, not a fiduciary opinion. An 80 percent accuracy target is a company goal stated to a tech blog, not an audited result, and I found no independent verification of any vendor's estimator accuracy. Treat every AI number the way you would treat a number from someone who profits when you hire the contractor.

Five moves, in project order

First, read the table before the catalog: if a sale is within two years, price the garage door, the steel entry door, and the stone veneer before you price anything inside. Treat the top of the Cost vs. Value table as a shopping list, and the total for all three runs under $20,000.

Second, apply the error-band rule: take any AI estimate and run it 20 percent high and 20 percent low. If the project still clears 100 percent at the high end, proceed with confidence. If it only clears at the low end, the estimate has told you nothing and you need real bids.

Third, check your region before your budget: a minor kitchen nets +$8,656 in the Pacific and −$1,547 in the West North Central. Look up your market's Cost vs. Value figures at costvsvalue.com rather than trusting a national average baked into an app.

Fourth, choose minor scope on every interior: the $28,458 kitchen nets +$3,683 nationally while the $161,511 version nets −$104,131. Buyers pay for finishes that are clean, functional, and current. Custom is a gift you give yourself, priced accordingly.

Fifth, match the metric to the timeline: staying five-plus years, chase the Joy Score and ignore the ROI table. Selling soon, chase the table and ignore the design magazines. Zonda's own tariff analysis notes that 20 percent of midrange remodels are already being downgraded to small repairs as costs rise; in a market like this, the cheapest project with the highest return is not a compromise. It is the plan.

Limitations of This Analysis

The error-band calculation takes Kukun's 80 percent accuracy target at face value; no independent audit of that figure exists, and the true band could be wider. Cost vs. Value resale figures are survey-based professional estimates, not observed transaction premiums, and the Today's Homeowner/Redfin study's lower numbers are a standing warning about method sensitivity. Regional figures cited (Pacific, West North Central) come from Zillow's summary of the report, not the primary tables. Vendor pricing and product availability change without notice, and Zonda's cost baselines predate current tariff effects, which the firm's own analysis says are already shrinking project scopes. Data-licensing terms of the Cost vs. Value Report limit reproduction; figures here are cited narratively with attribution to the 2025 report. Nothing here is financial advice; confirm scope pricing with licensed contractors and valuation with a local appraiser before committing funds.

Twenty years in, the lesson repeats itself on every job: the cheapest line on the bid is usually the one that makes the money, and the line everyone photographs is usually the one that loses it. AI will get faster at pricing both. It will not get better at telling you which one to build. That part is still yours.
