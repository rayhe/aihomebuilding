Your Electrician Lost Five Workers to a Data Center. He’s Hiring Teenagers. Your House Takes Two More Months.

By Marcus Washington · July 30, 2026

Scotty Wristen has owned WE Electric in Abilene, Texas, for years. He wires houses: duplexes, single-family, the occasional small commercial job. This spring, five of his workers left. Not for a competitor. Not for retirement. For a data center on the outskirts of town that pays $35 an hour to do substantially less strenuous work than pulling residential wire in a Texas attic. Wristen pays $20 and cannot match the offer. "Some of them were guys that I've had for eight years, five years," he told the Texas Tribune. "They came, they got trained, and they left to go out there." He does not blame them. So he is doing what a residential electrical contractor does when the labor market has been turned inside out by a $600 billion investment wave that has nothing to do with housing: he is hiring teenagers straight out of high school and training them from zero.

"They're new. They don't even have a set of tools," Wristen said. "It's usually about four or five months of hell where we have little mistakes that cost us time and money."

The data center that emptied his crew sheet is not some modest server farm. It is Stargate, a four-million-square-foot AI facility backed by OpenAI, Crusoe, and Oracle. It joins more than 300 data centers already operating in Texas, with roughly 100 more in the planning pipeline. And it is one node in a national buildout that Fastmarkets estimates will absorb $600 to $700 billion in capital expenditure in 2026 alone.

of a data center’s entire construction budget goes to electrical subcontractors, according to the International Brotherhood of Electrical Workers

That ratio explains why every data center project lands like a depth charge in the local residential labor pool. A new home requires electrical work, but it is one trade among a dozen. A data center is, at its core, an electrical project with a building around it. When a single facility consumes between 45 and 70 cents of every construction dollar on electrical subcontracting, it does not merely compete with homebuilders for electricians. It redefines the local market for them entirely.

Two Months Per House

Gene Lantrip is 69 years old and has been building homes in Abilene for decades. His company typically finishes 60 to 70 houses a year. This year, demand from data center workers flooding the area has pushed him to 86 houses under construction, with a path to 150 by year-end. He has never had more work. He has also never been slower.

"It's taken us two months longer to build the houses than what it did before the data centers were coming in," Lantrip told the Texas Tribune. "My subcontractors don't have the people. My electrician, he lost two of his lead men and several of his helpers to the data center."

The math is straightforward. A residential electrician in Abilene earns $15 to $20 an hour. Stargate pays double, plus overtime and per diem. An experienced lead electrician choosing between those two numbers is not making a career decision. He is doing arithmetic.

"Of course, the guys got to do good for their families," Lantrip said.

Two extra months per house is not just an inconvenience. It is money. Carrying costs on a residential lot under construction, including land payments, builder's risk insurance, and interest on the construction loan, typically run $1,200 to $2,000 a month. Across 86 houses, two months of delay costs Lantrip's operation somewhere between $206,000 and $344,000 in carrying costs alone, before accounting for the revenue he cannot collect because closings are sliding into next quarter.

The Pipeline That Cannot Backfill

Nationally, roughly 20,000 electricians leave the workforce each year. One in three working electricians is between 50 and 70. Becoming a licensed journeyman requires years of supervised apprenticeship, not a weekend certification course, not a YouTube playlist. "You can't just snap your finger and say, in six months, we're going to have all these other electricians," said Scott Norman, CEO of the Texas Association of Builders.

The ABC's 2026 workforce analysis identified electricians specifically as a bottleneck: "Demand for electricians capable of precision wiring has surged due to the rapid increase in data center construction." One-fifth of all electricians nationally are over 55. The pipeline was already losing people faster than it was adding them. Data centers accelerated the drain and redirected what remains toward higher-paying commercial work that does not build a single house.

The Home Builders Institute puts a number on the damage: $10.8 billion a year in costs attributable to the skilled labor shortage, preventing the construction of approximately 19,000 homes annually. Edward Brady, HBI's CEO, described the dynamic plainly: "If they don't have the [residential work], they're going to leave the residential industry and go to the commercial. Then, residential construction suffers. Those commercial jobs are more stable and, in many cases, better paying."

The Crowding-Out Effect

Data centers account for less than 2% of the construction industry by volume. But the American Institute of Architects and Brookings Institution research found they create an 11% increase in local construction employment over five to six years through knock-on demand, a "halo effect" that sounds benign until you realize that the halo lands on the same electricians, concrete crews, and HVAC technicians that residential projects need.

The macro picture is worse than any single market suggests. Fastmarkets' analysis found that in 2025, between one-third and one-half of underlying U.S. economic spending growth, once trade and inventory swings are stripped out, came from data center construction. Not manufacturing, not housing, not consumer spending, but server farms. That concentration of capital is keeping inflation and interest rates elevated, which flows directly into mortgage rates, which flows directly into the affordability equation for every buyer considering a new home.

The result is a kind of double squeeze. Data centers take your electrician directly, by paying him more. And they take your mortgage rate indirectly, by keeping the economy running hot enough that the Fed cannot cut as fast as housing needs it to.

The Irony Nobody Mentions

The technology being built inside these data centers is artificial intelligence. Among its advertised capabilities: making construction faster, cheaper, and more efficient. AI scheduling tools, AI cost estimation, AI project management, AI design optimization. The industry that promises to solve the housing crisis with software is consuming the labor, land, and capital that housing needs to exist.

This is not hypocrisy; it is economics. The companies building data centers are not in the housing business and are not pretending to be. But it is worth naming the dynamic clearly: in markets where data centers and housing compete for the same workers, the AI that is supposed to make homebuilding cheaper is, right now, making it more expensive. Not through its outputs but through its inputs. The concrete and the copper and the electricians and the capital all go to the same places they always went. There is just a new, extremely well-funded buyer at the table.

What Happens Next

Texas is trying to loosen the bottleneck with reciprocity agreements allowing electricians from Iowa, Alabama, and Arkansas to transfer their licenses without retesting. Cameron Dodd, political director of the Austin IBEW chapter, called it a start but acknowledged the lag: "All the baby boomers and older electricians are going to retire." Programs at Texas State Technical College are expanding electrical training, marketing the higher wages now available in the trade.

None of these measures produce a licensed electrician in under two years. In the meantime, builders like Wristen are training their own, absorbing the cost of mistakes, and hoping the teenagers they hire stay long enough to become competent before a data center offers them $35 an hour and a per diem that makes $20 look like an insult. In Abilene, home prices have risen 10.45% year over year. Duplexes that rented for $1,500 now rent for $2,500. The data center brought 14,000 temporary workers who need places to live, and the houses that would house them are stuck in the mud, waiting for an electrician who is somewhere else, doing something that pays better, for a machine that does not need a bedroom.

What This Means If You Are Building

If your project is in a data center corridor, including central Texas, northern Virginia, central Ohio, Phoenix, or the Atlanta exurbs, expect electrical subcontractor timelines to run 30 to 60 days longer than historical norms. Get your electrical sub under contract early, with a locked schedule and penalty clauses for delays, or accept that your timeline is a suggestion. Ask whether your sub has lost workers to commercial or data center projects recently. If the answer is yes, build the delay into your schedule now rather than discovering it at rough-in.

If you are buying a new home in one of these markets, understand that the price increase you see is not just lumber and land. Part of it is a labor premium driven by a construction sector that has nothing to do with your house but everything to do with the electricians your house needs. That premium is unlikely to ease until either data center construction slows or the electrician pipeline catches up, and neither of those is happening soon.

What This Does Not Prove

The data center labor drain is concentrated in specific metro and exurban markets, not evenly distributed. A builder in coastal New England or the Pacific Northwest may not feel it at all. The 2-month delay figure comes from a single builder in a single market (Abilene, TX) that is disproportionately affected by the Stargate project. National averages will be smaller, but the effect is real where it lands. Wage differentials between residential and data center electrical work vary by market, and the $20-versus-$35 spread in Abilene may be wider than the gap in a union-heavy market like Chicago or New York where residential electricians already earn $40 or more. The Fastmarkets estimate of $600-700 billion in 2026 AI capex is a consensus forecast, not a realized figure, and actual spending may come in lower if the AI investment cycle decelerates. Finally, housing starts are affected by many forces beyond labor availability, including interest rates, zoning, material costs, and consumer confidence, and isolating the data center effect precisely is not possible with current data.
