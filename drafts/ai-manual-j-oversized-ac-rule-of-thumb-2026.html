Your Contractor Quoted a 4-Ton AC. Manual J Says 2.5 Tons. The Extra Tonnage Cost You $2,200.

By Jake Kowalski · October 4, 2026

A quote is sitting on your kitchen table: four tons, 16 SEER2, $9,400 installed, from a contractor who spent eleven minutes in your house and measured exactly one thing, the square footage. Two thousand square feet divided by 500 is four tons, and that was the entire engineering analysis behind the most expensive appliance you will ever bolt to the side of your house.

That math has a name, the 500-square-foot-per-ton rule of thumb, and it was a reasonable guess in 1978, when houses leaked like screen doors and the cooling load was enormous. Your house is not a 1978 house. It has insulation, double-pane windows, and an attic that no longer doubles as a solar oven, and the rule never got the memo.

Allison Bailes is a building scientist in Georgia who got tired of arguing about this, so he ran the numbers: forty real homes in hot and mixed climates, full Manual J load calculations on each one, and the average came out to 1,431 square feet per ton, not 500. Results ranged from 624 all the way to 3,325, which tells you everything about why one number was never going to work, while newer homes kept landing around 800 square feet per ton or higher and high-performance builds reached 2,500. A contractor sizing your 2,000-square-foot house at 500 per ton is installing somewhere between two and five times the air conditioning you actually need.

1,431 Square feet per ton: the real average across 40 Manual J load calculations on actual homes, versus the 500 the rule of thumb assumes (Energy Vanguard).

Manual J is the ACCA protocol for doing this right: room by room, window by window, it totals what the house gains and loses at design conditions. Since the 2009 edition of the International Residential Code, Section M1401.3 has said the quiet part out loud, because equipment gets sized per ACCA Manual S from loads computed per Manual J, and ENERGY STAR's new-homes program caps cooling oversize at 15 percent. That math is not a suggestion; it is code.

Almost nobody enforces it on replacements, though. A code official on the Building Code Forum described requiring Manual J on changeouts and finding that most of the old units were wildly oversized, including one job where a contractor wanted to hang a 95K furnace on a 58K heat loss. Contractors, in his words, were screaming, and most of his neighboring jurisdictions do not ask for calculations at all, which means the rule that has been code for seventeen years is, in practice, a suggestion your contractor has never read.

Oversizing is not a victimless rounding error. An oversized AC blasts cold air, satisfies the thermostat fast, and shuts off before it has wrung any real moisture out of the air, then does it again, and again, in the short-cycling rhythm the trade knows too well. Rutgers' green building manual puts HVAC at 48 percent of a typical home's energy use and lists the damage plainly: poor humidity control, moisture and mold problems, extra wear on the equipment. NYSERDA draws the lines with numbers, because comfort and efficiency impacts begin at 120 percent of building load and severe impacts begin at 150, which means a 4-ton unit on a 2.5-ton load runs at 160 percent, past the severe line on day one.

Then there are the ducts, which make everything worse, because the Department of Energy's 2018 review of residential installation practices found that fewer than one in three systems moves enough airflow. National Comfort Institute testing puts the average system at 0.82 inches of water column total external static pressure against fans rated for 0.50, and since static pressure climbs with the square of airflow, bolting a bigger box onto ductwork that is already strangling just tightens the noose. Oversized and under-aired at the same time. Your compressor did not sign up for that.

Now the money, because this is where the rule of thumb invoices you: PickHVAC's installed-cost tables put a 2.5-ton central AC at $4,250 to $8,600 and a 4-ton at $5,950 to $11,300, so taking the midpoints gives roughly $6,425 against roughly $8,625. Those extra 1.5 tons cost about $2,200 on installation day, before the system has burned a single kilowatt, and then it burns those kilowatts inefficiently for fifteen years, short-cycling through humid summers, because you paid extra for the privilege. A 2026 cost guide puts the typical replacement at $3,500 to $7,500 and names tonnage as the biggest lever on the price, which is another way of saying the sizing decision is the pricing decision.

Doing it right used to be expensive in a different way: a real Manual J took a trained tech the better part of a day with a tape measure, which is exactly why the rule of thumb survived, because it was free, and that excuse just died. Conduit Tech, founded in 2022, put LiDAR on an iPad, so the tech scans the house in ten minutes and hands you an ACCA-certified Manual J, room by room, with permit-ready reports, in under fifteen, and ServiceTitan liked the company enough to buy it outright. A fifteen-minute scan does what the eleven-minute quote never bothered to.

Give the oversizers their hearing, because their reasons are not all dumb. Nobody ever got a callback because a house cooled too fast. Manual J designs to the 1 percent summer condition, and heat waves keep rewriting what that condition is. Additions happen, ADUs happen, and infiltration rates are educated guesses, so engineers pad. Modern inverter equipment ramps down to meet the load, which genuinely softens the cycling penalty. All fair, but the inverter defense dies in the ductwork: static pressure still squares with airflow, and most ducts are already running at 0.82 against a 0.50 rating. Padding for an addition you might build taxes every summer until you build it. And a callback-free install that grows mold in the master bedroom is not actually callback-free. It is just deferred.

A few honest limits before the takeaways. My $2,200 figure uses national midpoint ranges that swing by thousands with your market and equipment tier, so treat it as the shape of the penalty, not your bid. Bailes' 40 homes are hot and mixed climates, not a national sample. Conduit's accuracy claims are vendor claims; nobody has independently audited them. Manual J enforcement genuinely varies by jurisdiction, and the 160-percent figure assumes the 2.5-ton result for the example house. Your house has its own number, which is the entire point.

If your condenser is dying or you are building, here is what to do. Demand the room-by-room Manual J report before you sign anything. Not a tonnage, not a verbal assurance, the report, with inputs you recognize: your windows, your insulation, your orientation. A contractor who quotes tonnage from square footage alone has told you everything about his process, so get a second quote. If anyone suggests matching the old unit's size, remember the envelope has likely improved since that unit went in, and the old unit was probably oversized when it was new. Run the ratio yourself, quoted tons divided by Manual J tons: past 1.2, ask hard questions, and past 1.5, walk away.

Seventeen years of code say to do the math, and a fifteen-minute scan now does it for you, which leaves exactly one thing in America still sizing air conditioners by vibes: the guy with the eleven-minute quote. Do not pay him $2,200 for the privilege.
