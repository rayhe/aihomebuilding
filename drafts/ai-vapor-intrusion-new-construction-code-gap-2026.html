The Dry Cleaner Closed in 1998. Its Solvents May Be Under Your Slab.

By Jake Kowalski · September 21, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

For thirty years the dry cleaner sat at the end of the strip mall, humming through its last decade on a busted solvent trap that dripped perchloroethylene into the soil one invisible drop at a time. It closed in 1998. A developer scraped the lot in 2024, the dirt tested clean enough to build on, and four new houses went up with nice granite and nine-foot ceilings. Soil tests came back clean. The air above it is a different question.

Perchloroethylene moves, and warm soil gas rises through the unsaturated zone, finds the shrinkage cracks in a slab, the gap around a sewer lateral, the cold joint where the footing meets the wall, and walks into the living room uninvited, which is exactly why a clean Phase I soil test tells you almost nothing about the air the family will breathe. Soil lies. Air doesn't. EPA has a name for it: vapor intrusion. And nobody at the permit counter asked about it.

The Code Checks Your Radon Pipe and Ignores Everything Else

In a Zone 1 county, the IRC makes you build radon-resistant: a gas-permeable layer under the slab, a loop of perforated pipe, a sealed riser stubbed through the roof. Appendix F, right there in the model code. That loop, that riser, that aggregate bed, is the exact hardware vapor intrusion mitigation needs. Same pipe. Same physics. Same trades.

But Appendix F only knows radon, not the PCE from the cleaner, the TCE from the degreaser shop two blocks over, or the benzene from the gas station that pulled its tanks in 2009. No model code provision requires screening or mitigation for any of them in new residential construction. EPA published a technical guide for assessing and mitigating the vapor intrusion pathway in 2015. Guidance, not code. ASTM E2600-15 screens property transactions, not building permits. The inspector checks your radon riser, signs the card, and never asks what is riding the soil gas up through the same slab, because the codebook on the clipboard has no line item for it.

None of this hardware is exotic, which is the point: sub-slab depressurization, SSD, is the technology of choice for vapor intrusion, per contractors who install it at former dry cleaner sites in Massachusetts. A fan keeps the soil under the slab at lower pressure than the house, so soil gas physically cannot move against the gradient. Installed systems cut volatile contamination in indoor air by up to 99.9 percent, dropping PCE below laboratory detection limits, and the fan motors run about fifteen years before needing replacement. A U-tube manometer on the pipe tells the homeowner it is working. No app. No subscription.

The Math Nobody Runs at the Slab Pour

Here is what a passive loop costs when the concrete truck has not arrived yet: sixty to eighty feet of four-inch perforated pipe at $120 to $160, a gas-permeable aggregate layer at $150 to $250 marginal since most slabs already get a drainage bed, a roof riser with a junction box roughed in for a future fan at $120 to $200, four to five hours of one plumber's time during slab prep at $340 to $475, and two sub-slab sampling ports stubbed for later testing at $80 to $120. Total: roughly $900 to $1,400. Cheap insurance.

Now price it after occupancy, when the job means coring through the slab and finished flooring ($300 to $600), routing PVC and wiring a fan ($600 to $1,200), and patching drywall and paint around a family that never signed up for construction dust ($400 to $800). Diagnostic testing to size the suction field: $300 to $500. That lands at $1,600 to $3,100, and Nevada DEP documents residential PCE mitigations from $2,000 up to $20,000 at hot sites. New Jersey's radon guidance puts a typical sub-slab depressurization install at $1,300, ranging $500 to $2,500 by house and soil.

Cut through the ranges and the ratio is stubbornly stable across every bid table I have seen: a preemptive install costs about half of a retrofit, with zero interior disruption, and where Appendix F already demands radon hardware the same loop does double duty for both threats at once. No jackhammer required.

How the Screening Actually Works

EPA regions screen in buildings within about 100 feet of the plume, occupied structures first. EPA's generic attenuation factor is 0.03: indoor air concentration runs about three percent of the soil gas reading. EPA's vapor intrusion screening levels target a one-in-a-million cancer risk and a hazard quotient of 0.1 for non-cancer effects, which means the machine is calibrated to flag first and ask questions later, and that calibration is simultaneously the reason it protects families and the reason developers call it a racket. Chemicals of concern read like a commercial corridor directory: PCE, TCE, vinyl chloride, benzene.

One rule of thumb from the ITRC, cited in EPA's brownfields primer: if sub-slab concentrations run 1,000 to 10,000 times the target indoor level, mitigate proactively instead of characterizing further. Another round of sampling will not teach you anything the numbers have not already said. Sample in heating season. Concentrations spike when the windows stay shut.

One caveat that matters more than the rest: preferential pathways. Sewer laterals, utility conduits, sumps, elevator pits. These are vapor highways that ignore the 100-foot screen entirely. A clean soil gas reading next to a compromised lateral means less than people think.

The Counterargument, at Full Strength

This screening machine is designed to over-call. One in a million is an ultra-conservative target, and chasing it has burned six figures on risks invisible at any realistic exposure. A recorded mitigation system can stigmatize a property: the next buyer sees "vapor intrusion mitigation system" on a disclosure and walks, even when the system is a passive loop that was never activated. On a borderline Tier 1 screen, where a vapor encroachment condition merely cannot be ruled out, more sampling is sometimes the correct move, not a pipe. ASTM E2600 itself says mitigation design is beyond its scope; it is a transaction screen, not engineering. And a passive loop does nothing for preferential-pathway infiltration entering above the slab, which is a real limitation the hardware cannot fix.

Fair, all of it; proportionality, not paranoia, is the right response.

What to Actually Do

Builders, on infill lots or anywhere near a commercial corridor, should add an ASTM E2600-15 Tier 1 vapor encroachment screen to the Phase I, because it flags petroleum releases within a tenth of a mile and hazardous-material releases within a third of a mile for roughly $500 to $1,000 as a Phase I add-on, before anyone pours concrete. If a vapor encroachment condition cannot be ruled out, pour the passive loop. Twelve hundred dollars at construction beats three thousand after the furniture arrives.

Buyers, within a few blocks of a cleaner, station, or auto shop: demand sub-slab and indoor air sampling before close, in heating season if the calendar allows. A Phase I without a vapor intrusion screen next to a dry cleaner, a gas station, or an auto shop is an incomplete Phase I, and no clean soil sample on closing day protects the family breathing in the living room six months later.

The trigger rule: sub-slab readings at a thousand times the target indoor level mean mitigate, not more sampling. When sub-slab readings hit that multiple, believe them.

Limitations

Cost figures come from contractor marketing ranges and state guidance documents, not a bid sample; labor is assumed at $75 to $95 per hour for residential work. That 100-foot screening distance is a regulatory heuristic, not a safety radius, since plumes migrate with groundwater flow, sewer laterals carry vapors hundreds of feet past the mapped edge, and a house outside the line can breathe a plume the map says does not reach it. No third-party dataset counts how many new homes get preemptive vapor intrusion systems, so adoption rates in this article are unverified. Field verification data for passive loops is radon-centric; the physics are identical for PCE and TCE, but the verified numbers mostly belong to radon. And the screening levels themselves target one in a million, conservative by design, which is exactly what the counterargument above is about.
