Your House Burns 164 Watts While You Sleep. An Algorithm Can Name Every One.

By Priya Greenwood · September 20, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

At 2 a.m., when the dishwasher has finished and the thermostat has settled and every light in the house is dark, your home is still working. Not hard. Just steadily, pointlessly, expensively, the way a faucet drips when nobody is thirsty. Researchers put a number on the drip, and it came from a Stanford-backed analysis of 70,000 California homes, published by the Natural Resources Defense Council, which found that the average house draws 164 watts around the clock doing absolutely nothing its occupants asked for: not heating, not cooling, not lighting a single room, just humming along in the dark. Your house has a second electric bill hiding inside the first one, and it never sleeps so you can.

Call it always-on load, the polite term for the least polite fact in residential energy: nearly a quarter of the electricity in those homes was consumed by devices nobody was using.

164 Watts, Around the Clock

NRDC's Home Idle Load study remains the most rigorous measurement of the phenomenon, because it paired smart-meter data from tens of thousands of homes with in-person audits of ten residences where researchers catalogued every plugged-in device, finding sixty-five electrical devices per home on average, two-thirds of them drawing more than a watt each in always-on mode.

Always-on electricity came to 23 percent of total residential consumption in the Northern California sample. And the composition should embarrass anyone who thinks of energy waste as a heating-and-cooling problem, because the traditional big uses, heating, cooling, lighting, refrigeration, accounted for just 15 percent of the always-on total, while consumer electronics took 51 percent and miscellaneous electric loads, recirculation pumps, aquariums, fishponds, bathroom GFCI outlets, claimed the remaining 34 percent.

NRDC priced the worst offenders from its onsite audits: a hot-water recirculation pump left running at up to $93 a year, a desktop computer idling at up to $49, a television at up to $38, a cable set-top box at up to $30, and a printer ranging, absurdly, from 2 to 26 watts depending on the model. Even the coffee maker with its little clock display charges up to $6 a year for the privilege of knowing the time in the kitchen.

Here is the part that converts a curiosity into a budget line. NRDC found that 40 to 60 percent of idle load could be eliminated through simple no-cost or low-cost measures: timers on networking gear, killing the second refrigerator in the garage, unplugging the drill charger that draws 10 watts continuously for a tool nobody has touched in a month. Nearly half of the waste dies to measures that cost nothing, which means the barrier was never price but visibility, because nobody can see 164 watts.

Repricing the Dollar-a-Watt Rule

When NRDC published its findings, the national average residential rate sat near 12.5 cents per kilowatt-hour, and the study distilled its economics into a rule of thumb: every watt of always-on load costs about a dollar a year. One watt, running 8,760 hours, burns 8.76 kilowatt-hours, and at 12.5 cents that is $1.10, so round to a dollar and remember it forever. Rates, unfortunately, did not stay at 12.5 cents.

At today's average of roughly 17 cents per kilowatt-hour, the same arithmetic gives $1.49 per always-on watt per year. Multiply by 164 watts and the average always-on home is now spending about $244 a year on electricity nobody used, up from the $165 NRDC reported. That phantom tax grew nearly 50 percent while nobody was watching, driven by rates rather than behavior, which is the quiet cruelty of it: households did nothing differently and the bill for nothing got bigger.

Scale that nationally and the older federal figures start to look quaint. For scale, the Department of Energy has long cited standby power at 5 to 10 percent of residential electricity, costing Americans around $4 billion a year. A more recent estimate, built on the same always-on research line, puts inactive plugged-in devices at $19 billion a year. That gap is methodology, not contradiction: always-on idle includes devices doing low-grade work like pumping recirculated water and keeping networks alive, not just pure standby. Either way, the country is spending billions annually to power the state of being plugged in.

Apply NRDC's 40-to-60-percent reduction potential to the repriced $244, and the recoverable money in an average always-on home lands between $98 and $146 a year. That is the number every product in the next section is chasing.

An Algorithm That Names Watts

Seeing 164 watts requires identifying which device is responsible for each sliver of it, and that is a machine-learning problem with a thirty-year pedigree. In 1992, a researcher named Hart showed that individual appliances could be picked out of whole-house power measurements by detecting step changes in real and reactive power, the electrical equivalent of recognizing a voice in a crowded room. He founded a field called non-intrusive load monitoring, which has been waiting three decades for cheap sensors and serious compute to catch up with the idea.

Sense, a company out of Cambridge, Massachusetts, built the flagship consumer version. Its founders came from speech recognition, their technology had shipped in Samsung's S-Voice and Apple's Siri, and they aimed the same pattern-matching instincts at the electrical panel. Its monitor samples household power a million times per second, enough to tease out the telltale startup signature of a microwave or the subtly increasing motor draw of a washing machine filling with water, and its models distinguish around 30 appliance types at once. The company claims it can accurately disaggregate 80 percent of a home's energy use.

Does it pay? Sense's CEO told TechBrew that active users save 9 to 15 percent on utility bills, though he added the honest footnote that only about half of users reach those savings. Company materials cite roughly 9 percent on average. Treat both numbers as vendor-reported, because no independent randomized trial of consumer disaggregation savings exists, but the mechanism is at least legible: the app shows you the hot tub pump cycling every 30 minutes all day, and suddenly the abstract 164 watts has a name, a schedule, and an off switch.

There is a cheaper, dumber, arguably more honest approach. Emporia's Vue 3 skips the fingerprinting entirely and clamps current sensors onto individual circuits inside the panel, measuring each one directly. A mains-only version costs $99; eight circuit sensors bring it to $149, and sixteen sensors, enough to instrument nearly every circuit in a typical home, cost $199.99. Emporia claims plus-or-minus 2 percent accuracy, utility-grade metering, with no subscription. No machine learning required: when the water-heater circuit draws power at 3 a.m., you know exactly which breaker to blame.

Academic work keeps pushing the cost floor down. A 2025 paper in the South African Journal of Industrial Engineering demonstrated appliance disaggregation on a Raspberry Pi with clip-on sensors and a lightweight neural network, classifying devices every two seconds. Naming watts is getting cheaper faster than watts are getting cheaper, and Sense is already embedding its models into next-generation smart meters, which would put disaggregation inside the meter itself rather than in a box you buy.

Where the Algorithm Gets It Wrong

Before buying anything, hear the case against the story, because it is stronger than the marketing admits. That 23 percent figure flatters the narrative somewhat, because NRDC's dataset is Northern California, mild coastal and Central Valley climates where heating and cooling loads run small, so the always-on share looks outsized next to a mild winter. In Minneapolis in January, the same 164 watts is a rounding error beside the furnace, which is to say geography edits the headline.

Not all always-on load is waste, either, since some of it is the price of a functioning house: refrigerators, smoke and carbon-monoxide detectors, medical devices, sump pumps, the security system, and the networking gear that keeps all of it reachable. A smart strip on the wrong outlet trades $40 a year for a flooded basement or a dead security camera, which is the kind of savings that costs money.

The fingerprinting has real limits, too: variable-speed devices, inverter-driven heat pumps, EV chargers, modern washing machines, produce smeared power signatures that look like noise rather than fingerprints. Two identical devices, two televisions, two refrigerators, are nearly indistinguishable to the models. A circuit monitor tells you the water-heater circuit is hot at 3 a.m., but it cannot tell you whether that is a failing thermostat or a teenager's shower. The algorithm names the watt but not the motive, and the motive is where the savings decision lives.

None of this erases the finding; it bounds it. Somewhere between the $4 billion standby figure and the $19 billion always-on estimate sits a pile of money that is genuinely recoverable, genuinely measurable, and genuinely ignored, because the devices responsible are designed to be forgotten the moment you stop looking at them.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The 164-watt and 23-percent figures come from a 2015 NRDC-Stanford study of 70,000 PG&E homes in two Northern California climate zones; the national extrapolation and the $19 billion estimate are reported figures, not federal statistics, and the climate-zone bias described above applies. Rate repricing uses the EIA's roughly 17-cent national average residential rate for 2024-2025, so actual payback varies enormously by utility, from around 12 cents in Louisiana to roughly 40 cents in Hawaii. Savings claims from Sense (9 to 15 percent) and Emporia (10 percent or more) are vendor-reported with no independent randomized trial found, and Sense's own CEO notes only about half of users reach the claimed band. The 85-to-95-percent disaggregation accuracy cited for the field comes from research summaries rather than a single peer-reviewed benchmark, and real-world accuracy degrades when appliances run simultaneously. Emporia pricing was verified from the company's store and a February 2026 review roundup; Sense pricing was not independently verified and is deliberately omitted. The 35-watt entertainment-center example used in the payback math is illustrative, within NRDC's measured range for TV centers, not a measured home.

Your Checklist

If you are buying a home, read the 2 a.m. baseload during inspection. Most smart meters and utility apps show hourly usage; a 300-watt sleeping baseload means the house is full of 24/7 electronics the seller never audited, and it is a data point worth having before you inherit their plug loads.

If you already own the house, start free. NRDC found nearly half of idle load dies to no-cost measures, so walk the house at midnight with fresh eyes: the second fridge in the garage, the networking gear that could run on a timer, the drill charger feeding a tool nobody has touched in a month, the recirculation pump on a timer instead of continuous run. Do the free half before buying any hardware.

What not to kill: the refrigerator, smoke and CO detectors, medical devices, sump pumps, and security and networking gear you actually rely on. Phantom-hunting is surgery, not demolition, and the devices worth keeping on are exactly the ones whose silence you would notice at the worst possible moment.

Your utility bill has two authors. One is the life you live, the lights, the laundry, the long showers. The other is the house itself, humming along at 164 watts while you sleep, billing you for the privilege of being plugged in. You cannot negotiate with the second author until you can see it. Now you can.
