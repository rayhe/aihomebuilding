Your Last 10 Jobs Lost Money the Same Way. Your Next Bid Has Amnesia.

By Frank DeLuca · October 6, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

March. A kitchen remodel in the suburbs, the fourth one this year from the same plan set, the same cabinet line, the same tile the designer always picks. On Tuesday the electrician's final invoice lands, and Thursday brings the tile setter's bill. By Friday the job cost report is closed, and the number at the bottom reads 4.1 percent margin against a bid that promised 10.

Three lines are red: electrical 18 percent over estimate, tile labor 22 percent over, drywall 9 percent over.

Then the project manager opens the January kitchen report. Same three lines, same red, same percentages within a rounding error as the November kitchen and the September kitchen before it. He has now paid full tuition to learn the same lesson four times. Monday morning, the bid for kitchen number five goes out the door containing none of it.

I have watched this exact scene play out for twenty years, in shops running two jobs a year and shops running forty. It is the most expensive amnesia in residential construction, and it has nothing to do with how good your estimator is.

Three Kinds of Builders

Gregg Schoppman, a principal at the construction consultancy FMI, sorts small contractors into three buckets when it comes to job costing, and bucket one does not job cost at all. Bucket two job costs, but measures so few items that Schoppman calls it "codes in infancy," and he reaches for the ocean metaphor: it is like taking a teaspoon out of the ocean. Bucket three measures everything, which sounds rigorous until the field crews drown in cost codes and start dumping hours and receipts into whichever bucket is closest, at which point, in Schoppman's words, "you can't trust the numbers to tell you the truth about what's happening in your business."

His summary of bucket one deserves to be quoted in full, because I have never heard the situation stated more precisely: "The ones that don't capture anything at all have no predictability. They don't really know how their jobs are doing until the final nail is in the building, or the coffin, as I like to say in their case."

Read that again. The final nail or the coffin. In twenty years I have not found a sentence that explains more residential builder failures in fewer words.

Here is the part nobody in the estimating-software business wants to dwell on: buckets two and three have the same disease as bucket one, just better documented. A builder can produce beautiful job cost reports every Friday, with color-coded variances and executive summaries, and still send out Monday bids that learned nothing from them. The reports exist. The feedback loop does not. Nobody closes it. Ever.

Nine in Ten, and the Errors Are Not Random

A meta-analysis from Propeller Aero and Oxford found that 9 in 10 construction projects overrun their budgets by an average of 28 percent, a number that gets quoted as proof that estimating is hard when it is actually proof of something more useful: the errors are not random, which means they can be measured, modeled, and corrected, provided someone bothers to keep the records that make the pattern visible.

Random errors cancel out, because if your misses were truly random, some jobs would come in 28 percent under and the portfolio would balance. It does not balance, because the same cost codes bleed on job after job. Electrical runs light because the estimator prices it like the last decade's labor market. Tile labor runs heavy because the setter you actually use is slower and better than the one in the price book. Drywall drifts because nobody reprices the finish level the designer keeps specifying. These are not surprises; they are habits with invoices.

Psychologists have a name for the underlying defect. Estimators work from the inside view: this job, these plans, these conditions, this crew. What they need is the outside view: what your last twenty jobs of this type actually cost, line by line, with no optimism and no memory loss. Daniel Kahneman spent a career showing that the inside view loses to the outside view on every forecasting task ever tested. Construction estimating is just the forecasting task with the most expensive consequences.

28% Average budget overrun across construction projects, per the Propeller Aero and Oxford meta-analysis. Nine in ten projects overrun. Misses repeat in the same cost codes, which means they are calibratable.

What Closing the Loop Actually Looks Like

The machinery for this exists now, and it is priced for the five-person shop, not the ENR Top 400. Buildxact, which an October 2026 industry roundup rated best for estimate-to-actual costing among residential builders, runs $199 to $599 a month. Its costing model tracks three numbers per line item instead of two: estimated, committed, and actual.

That middle number is the one most builders have never seen, and it changes how you read a budget mid-job. Committed costs are the purchase orders and subcontracts you have signed but not yet been invoiced for: money that is spent but invisible in an actuals-only report. A kitchen that shows $180,000 estimated against $120,000 actual looks healthy until you add $55,000 in committed electrical, tile, and cabinet subcontracts, at which point the remaining exposure is $5,000 and the job is already underwater. Spreadsheet builders discover this at final invoice; the three-way model discovers it on a Tuesday in March, when there is still time to do something.

Adoption is further along than the industry's reputation suggests. Buildertrend's 2026 industry research reports that 91 percent of surveyed builders track job costs in real time on at least some projects, and 86 percent integrate estimating directly into job costing. Treat those as vendor-survey numbers, which they are, not independent benchmarks. Still, the direction is unmistakable: the estimate and the job cost report are becoming one continuous document instead of two documents that never meet.

Machine learning enters here, not as a robot estimator but as a pattern reader, and the distinction matters because nobody needs software that guesses at quantities: what small builders actually lack is a tireless reviewer that reads every coded job at month-end, compares each cost code against its own history, and flags the drift a human reviewer rationalizes away. Current models reach 75 to 83 percent accuracy against traditional estimates, improving as historical data accumulates. The practical use is narrower and more valuable than the marketing suggests: variance analysis across coded jobs, flagging the systematic biases a human reviewer glosses over. As one estimating guide puts it, repeated margin losses of 3 to 5 percent signal systemic mispricing rather than bad luck, and "patterns emerge before problems hit the site."

The Calibration Math

Enough theory. Here is what the loop is worth, with the assumptions on the table.

Take the median residential remodeler from the NAHB census: five payroll employees, eight jobs a year at $10,000-plus. Scale the job to a $400,000 kitchen-heavy remodel at a 7 percent target net margin, which is $28,000 per job if everything goes right.

Now assume the pattern from the cold open: electrical actuals run 15 percent over estimate on every job, and electrical is estimated at $28,000. The leakage is $4,200 per job, which means one cost code quietly removes $33,600 a year across eight jobs. That is more than the entire target margin on one job, evaporating inside a line item nobody reprices because nobody aggregated the evidence.

Eighty-one thousand dollars a year, in three cost codes, on the median remodeler's volume. Per year. Every year. Against that, the software that surfaces the pattern costs $4,788 a year at the mid-tier price, which means a single corrected bias pays for roughly seven years of the subscription, and even catching a modest 3 percent systematic mispricing covers the tool two and a half times over.

Detection speed is the second half of the math. With per-job noise around 10 percent on a cost code, a 15 percent systematic bias separates from the noise after roughly five or six jobs of clean coded data, which the median remodeler generates in about eight months. A project manager reviewing reports by instinct typically needs eighteen months to two years to act on the same pattern, and most never do, because each individual overrun gets explained away: tough client, bad weather, that one sub. The machine does not accept anecdotes; it counts.

Objections, at Full Strength

The strongest case against all of this comes from FMI's own expert, and it is devastating in its simplicity: the model is only as honest as the cost coding, and most cost coding is fiction. When the field finds the system tedious, hours land in the wrong buckets, and then the machine learns confidently wrong lessons and calibrates your next estimate toward a fantasy. Garbage in, calibrated garbage out. Schoppman again: you cannot trust the numbers to tell you the truth, and software does not fix a discipline problem; it amplifies whatever discipline already exists, in whichever direction it points.

Second, small samples learn slowly: eight jobs a year means a systematic bias takes most of a year to confirm, while labor rates move, material prices swing, and your best super quits in June. By the time the model is confident about your 2024 tile-labor factor, it is 2026 and the crew that generated the data is gone. Calibration has a half-life, and nobody publishes what it is.

Third, the estimator's judgment is not in the database, because the veteran who walks the lot, looks at the access, and quietly adds two days for the soil nobody tested is carrying information no cost code captures, no variance report flags, and no model can learn from history it was never given.

Over-calibrate to history and you start punishing the exact judgment that wins work: the model "learns" you always beat your concrete estimate, never knowing your best foreman runs every concrete job, and then he retires and the calibration becomes a trap.

Fourth, treat the vendor numbers as vendor numbers. The 75-to-83-percent ML accuracy figure comes from a company selling estimating services, measured against traditional estimates that themselves overrun 28 percent of the time. There is no independent audit of ML estimate calibration specifically for residential builders; that adjacent evidence is real, but it stays adjacent.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The calibration table is my model, not a field study, and I set every input by hand: eight jobs a year from the NAHB census, a $400,000 average job, a 7 percent target margin, and 10 percent per-job noise on a cost code, which means real shops will vary wildly on every one of those assumptions while the $81,120 figure scales linearly with volume and bias, cutting both directions.

The "five to six jobs to detect a bias" figure is a signal-versus-noise sketch, not a statistical result; real detection depends on how the noise is structured, which I did not model.

Buildertrend's adoption figures are vendor-published survey results describing software users, not the industry. I did not independently verify Buildxact's current pricing beyond the October 2026 roundup; treat it as directional. And nothing in this article proves that builders who adopt these tools actually improve margins. The mechanism is sound, the arithmetic is honest, and the outcome data does not exist yet. Yet.

The Discipline

None of this requires artificial intelligence. It requires a discipline that AI finally makes cheap enough for a five-person shop to sustain, and the whole program fits in five steps:

First, code every job the same way, with few enough cost codes that the field will actually use them. Twenty codes used honestly beat two hundred codes used randomly. This is the entire game; everything below is commentary.

Second, track committed costs, not just actuals. The purchase order is the moment the money is spent. If your budget report cannot show estimated versus committed versus actual on a Tuesday in March, you are flying with two instruments taped over.

Third, review variance monthly, by cost code, across jobs, not within them. The question is never "why did this kitchen go over on electrical." The question is "why do all of them."

Fourth, recalibrate the estimate factors once a year from the variance data, and write down what changed and why. The written record is what stops next year's estimator, who might be you, from drifting back.

Fifth, if you are the homeowner rather than the builder, add one question to the interview: show me your estimate-versus-actual variance on your last ten jobs like mine. A builder who can answer is running a business. A builder who cannot is running an experiment, and your kitchen is the lab.

Twenty years of watching projects go sideways has taught me that margins are not lost in the big dramatic disasters that get retold at the bar, the nightmare client or the blown schedule everyone saw coming. They leak out through the same three cost codes, job after job, year after year, while the bid for the next one gets typed up with a clean conscience and a blank memory. The reports remember everything, and somebody finally has to read them.
