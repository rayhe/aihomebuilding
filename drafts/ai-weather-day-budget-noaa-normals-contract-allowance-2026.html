Your Contract Allows 5 Weather Days. The Climate Record Says 36.

By Frank DeLuca · October 7, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Some lawyer in a dry office wrote the weather clause in your construction contract. It has never stood in mud. I have stood in mud, most recently on a Tuesday in November, watching a framing crew stack lumber under a tarp while the schedule bled out another day that the contract insisted was free. Every custom-home contract I have read in thirty years carries a weather-day allowance, a tidy little number of days the builder is permitted to lose to the sky. Almost none of those numbers were derived from the sky. They were derived from the last contract.

Here is the part that bothers me, twenty years into watching this particular ritual. Actual climatology is free: the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration publishes thirty years of rain-day data for thousands of stations, month by month, at no charge, in a format a spreadsheet can read. Your builder's allowance came from a template while the rain comes from the sky, and nobody in the chain ever checked one against the other, which is why I checked it myself.

What the Template Grants

A published sample weather-day clause computes the allowance as contract days divided by 365, times seven. On an 18-month, 540-day contract, that works out to about ten days. Scale it to a typical nine-month custom build and you get roughly five. Five days. Underneath sits the practical rule the Construction Financial Management Association describes: projects build weather days into the schedule, and the builder only gets additional time when adverse days exceed what was built in. Below the allowance, the rain is the builder's problem. Above it, the clock stops. That number does real structural work in the contract, yet it was chosen by arithmetic on a calendar, not by meteorology.

Some owners do this properly: the town of Clayton, North Carolina, publishes a weather-delay specification that defines adverse weather as precipitation above one-tenth of an inch, sets a month-by-month baseline (six days in January, three in July, fifty-four across the year), and counts a delay day only when work stops for half the scheduled day. That is a contract that has met the climate, which is more than most residential contracts can claim.

What the Climate Grants

NOAA's 1991-2020 climate normals publish, for every station, the mean number of days per month with precipitation at or above 0.10 inches. That threshold is not a coincidence of my choosing. It is the same tenth-of-an-inch line Clayton uses to define a weather day, which means the federal dataset and the industry specification are already speaking the same language. Take the Seattle-Tacoma station (USW00024233), the reference point for the metro where a huge share of Pacific Northwest custom homes get built, and lay a real schedule over it.

Census Bureau construction data, analyzed by NAHB, puts the 2025 average custom build (homes by hired contractors) at about 11.7 months from authorization to completion, and the Pacific division at 10.3 months (builder.media summary of the Survey of Construction). Strip out roughly six weeks of authorization and you have about nine months of actual construction. Assume a fall ground-breaking, October 1, which is when plenty of Northwest projects start, and assume the envelope is closed by the end of month five. That leaves excavation, foundation, framing, roofing, and exterior work exposed to the weather from October through February, the wettest five months of the Seattle year. This is the window that decides the schedule.

NOAA's normals for SeaTac over those five months: 55.4 days at or above a tenth of an inch. Not all of those stop work, so apply a foreman's discount. Days above half an inch, 16.4 of them, are near-certain lost days on exposed work; nobody frames productively in that. In the light band, the remaining 39 days lose about half a day each to mud, sequencing friction, and crews working at three-quarter speed. Expected weather-lost days: 16.4 plus half of 39, which is 35.9. Call it 36. Five is what the contract allows. I will let you do the subtraction, since you already did.

36 expected vs. 5 allowed A 9-month Seattle custom build starting in October can expect about 36 weather-lost days during its exposed phases, per NOAA 1991-2020 normals, against a standard template allowance of about 5: a shortfall of roughly 31 days.

The Same Clause in Phoenix

Run the identical arithmetic at Phoenix Deer Valley (USW00003184). October through February brings 9.1 days above a tenth of an inch, of which 2.2 clear half an inch, for about six expected lost days. That same five-day allowance covers fourteen percent of Seattle's expected weather while covering Phoenix's with room to spare, which means identical contract language is simultaneously a rounding error in one metro and a comfortable cushion in another, and neither the buyer nor the builder can tell which one they signed without the normals table in front of them. Your weather-day allowance should be a function of your zip code. In practice it is a function of your lawyer's last template, and the template was probably drafted somewhere dry.

The Price of the Gap

Thirty-one uncovered days have a price, and it compounds the way all schedule costs compound. NAHB's construction lending survey put the average Q1 2026 contract rate on pre-sold single-family construction loans at 7.19 percent. On a $750,000 Seattle-metro custom build, each day of delay at full draw costs about $148 in interest carry, which prices thirty-one days at $4,580. Then add the builder's extended overhead: supervision, trailer, insurance, equipment rental, the superintendent's truck payment. Three hundred dollars a day is a conservative figure for a small residential GC, and thirty-one days of it is $9,300. Combined exposure beyond the contract's weather budget: roughly $14,000. On the average outcome. Not the bad one. Half of wet-season builds will do worse than this, which is what an expected value means.

The Clause That Eats Itself

There is a legal kicker buried in the standard forms, and it favors whoever did the homework. AIA A201, the general conditions behind most custom residential contracts, grants time extensions for adverse weather, but Section 15.1.6.2 requires the claim to document that conditions were abnormal for the period, could not have been reasonably anticipated, and hurt the scheduled work, a three-part test that sounds balanced until you notice the middle prong quietly imports the entire federal climate record as the standard for what a reasonable contractor should have seen coming. Read that requirement again against the NOAA table: a rainy November in Seattle is not abnormal. It is the normal, published by the federal government, averaged over thirty years, available to anyone with a browser. A builder claiming a wet Seattle November was unforeseeable is claiming the climate record does not apply to them. No fight is won in the claim; it is won or lost in the baseline table you write into the contract before the first shovel, the way Clayton, North Carolina did.

Where the Machines Fit

The industrial version of this article already exists, and it is called generative scheduling. ALICE Technologies runs construction schedules through AI optioneering: on a Denver healthcare project with Mortenson, its analysis shaved 84 days off a 540-day schedule (Building Design+Construction); Takenaka generated more than 300 schedule options for a Bangkok tower and trimmed over 30 days (AEC Magazine); a $600 million highway job saved 69 days against a conventional planning team. ALICE's own analysis shows why early matters: the productivity cliff between a 10 percent and 20 percent loss jumps schedule impact from days to nearly 200 calendar days, which is the mathematical case for catching slippage before it compounds.

All of that is commercial scale, priced for commercial GCs, and I will not pretend your custom-home builder is buying it. For residential work, the honest version is the manual one this article just demonstrated: a normals-driven weather budget at bid time, the seven-day National Weather Service forecast coupled to the critical path every Monday morning, and interior tasks like rough-in and drywall held deliberately as weather float, ready to swap into any rained-out week. Photo-based progress tracking tools do the catching-up part, flagging the week weather-driven slippage starts instead of the month it becomes undeniable, which is the difference between re-sequencing two crews on a Monday and re-baselining the entire back half of the job in a panic. Machines do the arithmetic at scale now. The arithmetic was always the job.

The Other Side of the Argument

Now the strongest case against everything above, stated fairly, because a foreman who only counts his own nails is not doing takeoffs. Weather is a rounding error next to the true delay drivers: owner changes, material lead times, permitting queues, and labor. NAHB attributes the post-2015 slowdown in build times, from 7.2 months to 8.8, to regulation, rates, and the labor shortage, not rain. Nobody's closing got pushed six weeks by drizzle alone. A builder with twenty years in the Puget Sound carries the rain in his bones, which means this model gives him precision he may not need, since his gut already prices November, his bids already carry the contingency whether or not the contract names it, and the only party the arithmetic truly serves is the buyer reading the contract for the first time. Worse, padding the allowance does not fix anything. It just moves contingency from one line to another, and owners pay for padding either way, whether it rains or not. And the fix that actually shortens weather exposure is not a bigger rain budget. It is drying in faster: panelized walls, roof-first sequencing, getting the envelope closed before the sky opens. Budget the rain all you want. The building gets wet until the roof is on.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The fifty-percent loss rate on light-rain days is an illustrative heuristic, not an empirical finding; real crews range from heroic to sensible. Those loan and overhead figures are a worked example with stated assumptions. Construction interest accrues on drawn balance only, so the full-draw daily rate is the peak case and the average-draw figure is roughly half. ALICE's results are commercial-scale and vendor-reported, not independently verified, and no equivalent residential product was evaluated. This is one start-date scenario; a March ground-breaking in Seattle faces a kinder sky, and a January start faces a crueler one. Those 1991-2020 normals are backward-looking: atmospheric-river extremes of recent years sit outside that baseline, which means even the 36-day figure may be optimistic. Mud and dry-out days, which specs like Clayton's allow after inch-plus events, are not modeled. Station data is not your site; microclimates within a metro can shift the numbers. And Phoenix's real weather risk is heat, not rain, which this model does not touch.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you are signing a custom-home contract anywhere with a real winter or a real rainy season, pull your NOAA station's normals before you sign, because the twenty minutes it takes is the cheapest schedule insurance you will buy on the entire project, and it is the only contingency you will ever price from measurement instead of memory. That data is free at ncei.noaa.gov; the tenth-of-an-inch threshold matches what the industry already uses, and the arithmetic takes twenty minutes. Put the monthly table in the contract the way Clayton, North Carolina does, so the baseline is evidence instead of argument. Negotiate the allowance against the budget you computed, or skip the day-count fight entirely and add a line-item weather contingency: dollars per day times expected shortfall, priced openly instead of buried in margin. If you are the builder, run the seven-day forecast against your critical path every Monday, hold interior work as weather float, and treat dry-in as the milestone that ends your exposure, because it is. And remember the AIA language if a claim ever matters: "not reasonably anticipated" is judged against the normals. Baseline them in the contract now, while everyone is still dry.
