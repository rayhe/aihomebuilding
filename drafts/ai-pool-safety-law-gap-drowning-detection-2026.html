Congress Wrote a Pool Safety Law in 2007. It Skipped 97% of Pools.

By Catherine Chen · September 18, 2026

In June 2002, seven-year-old Virginia Graeme Baker went to a graduation party at a friend's house and died in the hot tub, held against a drain by suction the adults around her could not break in time. Five years later, Congress passed a law in her name, the Virginia Graeme Baker Pool and Spa Safety Act, which requires anti-entrapment drain covers on every public pool in America, with secondary backup systems on the dangerous single-drain designs, and covers exactly none of the 10.4 million residential pools in the country.

That boundary is not an oversight; it is the law's whole shape. Congress wrote the statute for public pools and left everything else to the states, and nineteen years of state-by-state rulemaking have proved it an uneven bet.

Start with the stakes: CDC counts roughly 4,000 unintentional drownings a year in the United States, eleven a day, and drowning is the single leading cause of death for children ages one through four. In 2022, 461 children in that age band drowned, a rate 28 percent higher than in 2019. CPSC's 2025 report found that 74 percent of reported child pool and spa drownings happened in residential settings, and more than half at the victim's own home. Congress wrote the law for one population; a different population is dying.

Now the arithmetic of the gap: America has about 10.7 million swimming pools, according to the Pool and Hot Tub Alliance's census, of which roughly 309,000 are public. Federal drain-safety enforcement reaches about one pool in thirty-five; the other thirty-four run on state and local rules, which is a polite way of saying most of them run on whatever was in the ground when the pool went in.

Against those stakes, one intervention has evidence that clears every bar. It is not a device; it is a fence with a gate. A Cochrane systematic review found that four-sided isolation fencing, a barrier that separates the pool from the house and the yard with its own self-latching gate, is associated with an 83 percent lower odds of a child drowning than three-sided fencing, where the house itself serves as the fourth side. Pediatricians agree, and the American Academy of Pediatrics' 2026 technical report confirms the direction: three-sided setups carry nearly twice the child drowning risk of four-sided ones. A 2025 study in Injury Prevention estimated that proper isolation fencing, paired with life jackets, could prevent 348 American drowning deaths and $4.5 billion in economic costs every year.

Three-sided fencing fails through a back door: most young children who drown in pools were last seen inside the house, had been out of sight for less than five minutes, and were in the care of one or both parents at the time. When the house is one wall of the pool enclosure, a toddler who learns to work a sliding door has defeated the entire barrier. Four-sided fencing moves the perimeter off the house, so defeating it requires defeating a gate, not a door handle.

Pediatricians have turned the failure modes into numbers: a fence at least four feet high, openings no wider than four inches, no footholds a child could climb, a self-closing and self-latching gate that swings away from the pool, and the latch at least 54 inches above the base of the gate, out of a toddler's reach. Every figure in that prescription answers a recorded death.

California wrote a menu, and builders order the cheapest items.

Set that evidence against what California, the ambitious case, actually requires. Since 2018, any permit for a new or remodeled pool at a private single-family home must include at least two of seven drowning-prevention features, listed in the Health and Safety Code: an isolation enclosure, a removable mesh fence built to the ASTM F2286 standard with a self-closing, self-latching gate, a compliant safety cover, exit alarms on the doors that open to the pool, self-closing and self-latching door hardware with the release at least 54 inches up, a pool alarm built to ASTM F2208, or an equivalent the building official accepts. Senate Bill 552, effective in 2025, removed the weakest combinations from the menu and disqualified the manual pool cover as a qualifying feature. When the house sells, the home inspection must identify which of the seven features the pool has.

Read that menu as an economist, and the incentive runs backward. A new in-ground pool averages $65,000 to $73,500. Cheapest compliant pair on the menu, exit alarms plus self-latching door hardware, costs on the order of three hundred dollars, roughly half a percent of the project. Meanwhile the pair the evidence favors, a removable mesh isolation fence at $17 to $35 a linear foot installed, plus an alarm, runs roughly $2,000 to $3,800 for a hundred-foot enclosure, roughly three to six percent of the project. Nothing in the statute steers a builder toward the effective option. A compliance menu that prices the layers and lets the builder pick the two cheapest produces exactly this outcome, while the Academy's own report concedes the alarm layer has "limited research" behind it. We are regulating the purchase of devices, not the prevention of drownings.

And California is the ambitious case: Florida and Arizona write residential barrier rules into state law, but in most of the country pool safety is city and county code, varying by ZIP code, or nothing at all. Nineteen years after Congress placed its bet that the states would fill the gap, the drowning statistics are the report card, and the grade is written in the statistic that has refused to move for a generation: drowning is still the leading cause of death for children ages one through four.

Your drain cover has an expiration date nobody reads.

Go look at the bottom of the pool, where the drain cover carries an expiration date nobody enforces. Every compliant drain cover bears a stamped service life, a "replace by" date embossed in the plastic, because the cover Virginia Graeme Baker died on was about ten years old and had gone brittle. That stamp is the entire reason the marking standard exists. Manufacturers stamp covers for three to ten years of service, commonly five to seven. A pool's structural life runs twenty-five to thirty years. Do the subtraction: somewhere between three and eight full replacement cycles on a part nobody can see without getting wet, on pools in states with no re-inspection regime, enforcing a date nobody is required to check. Nobody skips a drain-cover replacement because of the price; they skip it because nothing reminds them. Nothing in the law does.

This is the quietest failure in the whole system, and it is the one the federal law actually understood. Congress knew a plastic cover degrades, which is why the stamp exists. What Congress did not do was extend that understanding to the 10.4 million pools where the only inspector is the homeowner, who has never heard of the stamp.

Cameras watch the water; the evidence watches the fence.

This is the vacuum the AI products have floated into. Coral Smart Pool sells Mylo, an underwater computer-vision camera with surface entry sensing and escalating phone alerts, directly to homeowners: $999 at preorder, $1,499 at retail, and the pitch is serious on its own terms. Drowning is silent, the company argues, and most of it happens while the pool is in active use, which is exactly the scenario a fence cannot cover. Its commercial competitor Poseidon, sold only to public and institutional pools, claims nineteen saves worldwide, every one of them in a guarded pool with a lifeguard on the other end of the alarm.

Here is the evidence hierarchy, and it is not close: isolation fencing has a Cochrane systematic review, alarms have the Academy's "limited research," and residential AI detection has vendor claims. That roughly-one-false-alarm-every-three-weeks figure is Coral's, and no independent study has validated any home AI drowning detector's sensitivity or false-alarm rate. Nineteen Poseidon saves are nineteen real people, and they are also the strongest possible advertisement for the technology's limits: all nineteen happened where a trained professional stood ready to respond, while the home buyer gets the camera without the lifeguard. No lifeguard, just a phone.

Strongest argument for the camera: the fence has a blind spot, and the point deserves its full weight. A barrier prevents access; it does nothing once a swimmer is already in the water and in trouble. Drowning is silent, and the splashing struggle of the movies is not what it looks like. During a pool party, the scenario manufacturers describe in their marketing, an underwater camera watching for a motionless body on the bottom is the only layer still on duty. Fences protect the unsupervised moment; cameras protect the supervised one. Different moments, different tools. Dismissing the technology because the evidence base is thin is the kind of reasoning that keeps the evidence base thin.

Cameras are not the problem here; their alert's endpoint is. A fence works when nobody is watching; a camera works only when someone is notified, notices, and responds inside the narrow window drowning allows. That converts a passive protection problem into an active response problem, and nothing in the residential evidence base shows parents consistently win that race. Coral's own framing cuts both ways: if most drownings happen during active, supervised pool time, the supervisor the camera pages is the same supervisor who was already there.

Fence rules are written as geometry: 48 inches of height, four-inch openings, a 54-inch latch. Geometry is exactly what computer vision reads best. A barrier-compliance check at permit time, run against the site plan before the concrete is poured, would cost a fraction of a re-inspection regime and reach every pool in a jurisdiction, and it would work while everyone is still at the drawing board, which is the only moment when the fence is cheap to fix. Meanwhile the industry builds cameras to watch the water and ignores the drawing of the fence.

What to actually do

If you are building a pool: buy features one and two off California's menu first, the isolation enclosure and the ASTM F2286 mesh fence with a self-closing, self-latching gate, before spending a dollar on alarms or cameras, because the evidence runs fence first, alarms a distant second, AI detection unproven. On a $65,000 pool, the most effective layer costs roughly three to six percent of the project. That is the cheapest proven life-safety purchase in the entire build.

If you are buying a house with a pool: in California, demand the inspection's list of which drowning-prevention features exist; the statute requires the inspector to produce it. Anywhere else, commission a pool-safety inspection the way you would a sewer lateral, and walk the gate yourself. It should swing shut and latch from every angle of approach, with no propped-open workarounds, no furniture parked within climbing distance, and the latch out of a toddler's reach.

If you already own a pool: read the stamped date on your main drain cover. It is embossed on the cover itself, which means getting in the water or sending the pool service down with a camera. If you cannot find a date, assume it has expired. Then check the arithmetic of your pool's age against the cover's stamped life: a fifteen-year-old pool on its original cover is on borrowed time by every manufacturer's count.

On the AI camera: treat it as a fourth layer, never the first. It belongs after the fence, the gate, and the cover date, in the same category as the pool alarm: a supplement to supervision, not a substitute for a barrier. And ask the vendor for independent validation before you trust the false-alarm rate, because a camera that cries wolf gets muted, and a muted camera is a decoration.

Methodology and limitations

The 1-in-34 figure divides the Pool and Hot Tub Alliance's 309,000 public pools by its 10.7 million total US pools (10.4 million residential, of which 6.1 million in-ground and 4.3 million above-ground). That 83 percent figure derives from the Cochrane review's odds ratio of 0.17 for isolation versus three-sided fencing; odds ratios are not guaranteed household-level risk reductions, and the underlying studies are observational. New-pool cost figures are 2025 to 2026 national averages from RubyHome and a 377-job Shasta Pools dataset via PitchBook; California builds run higher. Mesh fence pricing comes from manufacturer and installer FAQs, not a transaction dataset, and varies by deck, gate count, and region. Cheapest-pair estimate (exit alarms plus door hardware, on the order of $300) is an order-of-magnitude figure from retail pricing, not a bid.

What this analysis could not do: no US dataset tracks residential barrier compliance rates, so the 83 percent figure measures effectiveness, not adoption, and we cannot say what share of the 10.4 million residential pools have compliant isolation fencing. Above-ground pools, 4.3 million of the residential total, face different risk geometry, ladders rather than gates, which this article does not address in depth. CPSC fatality counts are reported drownings, a floor on true incidence. Vendor claims for AI detection products are unattributed to independent studies. None of the California analysis generalizes: most states have no two-feature rule, and local amendments differ.
