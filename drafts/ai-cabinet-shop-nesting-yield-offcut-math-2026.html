Your Cabinet Shop Feeds $25,000 of Plywood to the Dumpster Every Year. The Fix Is $225 a Month.

By Frank DeLuca · September 21, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Walk into any custom cabinet shop on a Friday afternoon and look for the leaning tower. It is not the sheet rack, which is tidy, or the assembly benches, which are swept. It is the pile of offcuts in the corner by the back door, parts too small for the job they came from and too awkward to throw away, held against a future that never quite arrives. In a shop doing six kitchens a month, that pile represents roughly $2,100 a month in plywood that was bought, paid for, and cut into pieces nobody will ever install. He knows the pile is there, and he walks past it the way you walk past a slow leak in a tire.

I have spent twenty years watching projects lose money in the gap between what the takeoff said and what the dumpster said. Sheet waste is the most honest version of that gap, because the math is simple enough to do on a napkin and brutal enough that most shops never do it.

Napkin Math

A standard kitchen needs about 30 sheets of 3/4-inch prefinished maple plywood. At current pricing, call it $70 a sheet, which puts $2,100 of sheet material into every kitchen before a single part is cut. Inside that number, the actual parts, the sides, tops, bottoms, shelves, and stretchers, occupy roughly 22.5 sheets of area. Everything else is the price of turning rectangles into cabinets.

How much of each sheet ends up as a cabinet depends entirely on how the parts get laid out. Cut the traditional way, one operator with a cut list and a panel saw, and shops land around 70 to 75 percent yield. Estimating guides put the industry's own norms at 10 to 15 percent waste on plain sheet goods like melamine and MDF, and 20 to 25 percent on grained material, which is the polite way of saying a quarter of your walnut-veneer sheet becomes decorative mulch. Those numbers come from MaxCut Software's estimation guide, and FDMC magazine has been writing about the remnant problem since before most nesting software existed.

What the Software Actually Does

Nesting software takes your cabinet library, generates the cut list automatically from the design, and packs the parts onto each sheet the way a good Tetris player packs a well. Serious packages do true-shape nesting, which uses the actual outline of curved or angled parts instead of their bounding rectangles, and they respect grain direction on visible parts, kerf width, and the spacing the vacuum hold-down needs to keep small parts from skating across the table mid-cut. Cut lists, labels, G-code for the router: one pipeline, from design to machine.

Here is what surprised me: the price of admission is lower than most shop owners assume. Mozaik's CNC package, which includes true-shape nesting and post processors for more than 170 machine brands, lists at $225 a month. That is the entire software stack from design to G-code for less than the cost of three sheets of plywood a month.

Running Both Numbers

Take the 30-sheet kitchen at $70 a sheet. At a 75 percent manual yield, the shop orders 30 sheets and $2,100 goes in. At a 90 percent nested yield, the parts fit on 25 sheets, and five sheets, $350, stay in the owner's pocket on every kitchen. A shop running six kitchens a month saves $2,100 a month and $25,200 a year. The software costs $2,700 a year. That is not a close call. That is a rounding error with a subscription attached.

Field evidence backs the napkin. A millwork consultant working with a West Coast manufacturer running 150 kitchens a month used CAD analytics to find that one CNC machine was wasting 7 percent more material than its twin, because one operator's setup left wider buffers between parts. Standardizing the nesting logic through Microvellum's BOM and cut-list automation cut waste by $4,000 a month, without changing a machine or a hire.

There is a second number worth knowing, because vendors will not volunteer it. FDMC drew the distinction years ago between nesting yield, what the software claims for the sheet, and manufacturing yield, what the shop actually keeps after the remnants get counted honestly. In their worked example: 89 percent nesting yield, 82 percent manufacturing yield. Those seven points in between are the offcut pile by the back door. Any shop quoting you a yield number should be asked which one it is.

Where the Story Gets Honest

Now the part the software brochures skip. A nesting vendor called CutPlan publishes an FAQ that reads like it was written by someone tired of lying, and it is the most useful document in this entire category. On a small job of simple rectangular carcasses, a good guillotine cut list on a panel saw packs nearly as tight as a free-form nest, because rectangles do not benefit from being rotated at odd angles. That paper yield gap of 5 to 15 percent only turns into money when it removes a whole sheet from the order, and on a small job it usually rounds away, leaving you with the same sheet count plus a CAM programming bill. Nesting earns its keep on large production runs, where setup amortizes, and on curved or irregular parts that straight cuts cannot produce at all. For a one-man shop building closet systems out of melamine boxes, the saw wins, and the vendor says so itself.

Then there is the operator. That 7 percent buffer gap in the millwork case study is the whole story in miniature: the algorithm can be perfect and the setup can still eat the savings, because one operator's comfort margin between parts is a tax the software cannot see. Grain direction is another hard constraint. Visible parts have to run the grain the right way, which locks rotation and hands back a chunk of the theoretical yield, especially on the grained sheets carrying the 20 to 25 percent waste norm.

Finally, a word about the letters A and I. Most of what is sold as AI nesting is two-dimensional bin packing, a problem operations researchers have been grinding on since the 1960s, wearing a 2026 costume. Today's heuristics are genuinely better than they were, the interface is enormously better, and the integration from design to cut list to G-code is the real product. None of that requires a neural network. When a vendor says AI, ask what the model was trained on, and judge by the silence.

What to Do on Monday

If you run a CNC router with no nesting software, the arithmetic above is your business case. One sheet saved per month covers Mozaik's $225. Track yield per machine per month, not per job, because per-job numbers are where the remnant pile hides.

If you run a saw-only shop, do not buy a CNC to chase nesting. Get a guillotine cut-list optimizer first. CutPlan's free tier covers 30 calculations a month, enough to learn whether your jobs have the part mix that benefits. A router earns its place on irregular parts and volume, not on rectangles.

If you are a GC or a homeowner pricing cabinets, ask the shop one question: what is your sheet yield, and is that nesting yield or manufacturing yield? A shop that knows the difference is run tighter than a shop that treats a 25 percent waste factor as weather.

Whatever you run, write a remnant policy. FDMC's two schools are shelf-and-reuse or discard-if-short, and the honest version is that remnants shorter than a few feet cost more in labor to manage than they save in material. Label the offcuts, date them, and throw away anything that has been leaning by the back door for six months. That pile is not inventory. It is a monument.

What This Analysis Cannot Tell You

That $350-per-kitchen figure is my own calculation from a 30-sheet kitchen at $70 a sheet and a 75-to-90 percent yield step. Real kitchens run 20 to 45 sheets, sheet pricing moves with the lumber market, and the 90 percent nested yield is a vendor-adjacent figure; FDMC's manufacturing-yield frame suggests honest real-world numbers land in the low 80s once remnants get counted. That $4,000-a-month case study comes from a millwork consultant's writeup of a single manufacturer and is not independently audited. Mozaik's $225-a-month price is current list pricing and excludes training, implementation, and the operator time to learn the system, which every shop underestimates. I did not test any nesting package against a real cut list, and grain-locked visible parts will shrink the savings on high-end work below what the napkin math shows.

No financial statement in the shop is more honest than the offcut pile. It does not lie about yield, it does not round up, and it does not care what the brochure said. Measure it, price the software against it, and let the pile decide. It has been waiting by the back door long enough.
