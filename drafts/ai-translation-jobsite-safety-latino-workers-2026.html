Your Crew Speaks Six Languages. Your Safety Briefing Speaks One. OSHA Noticed.

By Marcus Washington • September 6, 2026

Picture the morning huddle on a residential framing job in Texas: twenty-two guys around a sawhorse table, the super running through the day in English, which trusses get set first, where the crane needs spotters, a reminder about the unprotected edge on the second floor, and most of the crew nodding, some because they understood every word and some because nodding is what you do when the boss is talking and the other guys are nodding.

One of the guys who nodded will die this year, probably not on this job, but the odds are not abstract.

Researchers at CPWR, the Center for Construction Research and Training, pulled twelve years of federal fatality records and found the kind of number that should have stopped the industry cold: deaths among Latino construction workers more than doubled between 2011 and 2022, from 197 to 408, while deaths among non-Latino construction workers rose 16.5 percent, from 583 to 679, which means the same industry produced two completely different trend lines over the same years, and one of them is a cliff.

408 Latino construction workers died in 2022, which is 1.12 deaths per calendar day. Latino workers accounted for roughly 38 percent of construction fatalities against roughly a third of the workforce, and in the trades where they concentrate, they are the workforce: 75.2 percent of drywall installers, 63.9 percent of roofers, 62.5 percent of painters.

CPWR named the likely causes without hedging: language barriers, cultural factors, concentration at smaller employers with thin safety programs, and training materials that do not exist in Spanish. Federal data makes the picture darker: across all industries 1,250 Hispanic or Latino workers died on the job in 2023 and 839 of them were foreign born. A worker who cannot read the safety manual and cannot report a hazard without fear is not facing one problem. It is three stacked on top of each other.

The technology stopped being an excuse

The uncomfortable part for anyone running a crew is that the translation tools caught up, and not the Google-Translate-shouted-across-the-site kind. Benetics, a Swiss company that launched US operations in Detroit, built an AI voice assistant specifically for field crews, with trade terminology, more than 30 languages and dialects, noise-tolerant audio, and documentation filed in a foreign language automatically translated so the office actually sees it. A worker logs a hazard by talking into a phone. It lands in the trailer in English, in seconds.

GoVoz.ai is building the same category from a different direction: a bilingual safety communication platform with construction-trained translation, reviewed by fluent Spanish speakers, shaped with input from union superintendents and safety leaders. Clemson University researchers tested Habla Seguro, an app pairing English-speaking supervisors with Spanish-speaking craft workers, and found it faster and more precise than general-purpose tools, with safety phrases tuned to construction context and culture. Stranger still is the academic frontier. A 2026 paper from ISARC describes a wearable edge-AI assistant that translates heat-stress safety communication between Spanish and English entirely offline on a Jetson Nano, built around an eighty-sentence bilingual dataset, because cell service on a job site is a rumor and a safety device should not depend on it.

There is a live proof of scale on the other side of the Pacific. Lotte Group's construction arm deployed an AI site translator at a 700-worker Seoul job site where 40 percent of the workforce is foreign born: twenty languages, construction jargon and slang included, a QR code on a poster, translations on every worker's phone. One supervisor reported that relaying instructions through a human translator used to eat twenty minutes. Now the crew reads the translation, in under five.

Run the exposure math

OSHA requires employers to provide safety training in a language workers understand. OSHA publishes the penalty schedule: up to $16,550 per serious violation, up to $165,514 per willful or repeated violation after the 2025 inflation adjustment, so do the comparison yourself. One serious citation at the maximum covers equipping multiple crews with construction-specific translation tools for years, and one willful citation buys a decade of it. That is not a product pitch; it is an accounting observation, because the money is already being spent: on funerals and workers comp and the $165,514 citation that arrives after the inspector asks who translated the fall protection training and the answer is nobody.

For a residential GC running five to ten homes with a rotating cast of subs, the actionable version is simpler than any of this. Count the languages on your sites this week. Ask your supers how they brief the crew in each one. If the answer is a bilingual lead carpenter translating from memory while standing on a stack of two-bys, you have a single point of failure with a name and a hard hat. Pilot one tool on one crew for one project. Measure whether toolbox talks get shorter and whether near-miss reports go up, because reports going up is what honesty looks like at first.

What translation cannot fix

Now the part the vendors will not put in the brochure. A translated briefing does not help a worker who is afraid to report anything because his immigration status is precarious. It does not fix the small subcontractor that will never buy the software, and since CPWR names small-employer safety programs as a cause of the disparity, the workers at highest risk are the least likely to ever see these tools. Noise-cancelled audio in a genuinely noisy environment remains unsolved. Clemson's own researchers flag high-noise performance and trade-specific vocabulary as future work. Text-based translation excludes workers who cannot read in any language, which even the ISARC team admits means visual aids are still needed. Worst case, the app becomes compliance theater: the GC checks a box, the briefing plays in Spanish, and nothing about the underlying crew structure changes. Piecework incentives still reward speed over care, and the sub who bids lowest still wins. And the worker who nods without understanding still nods, except now the nodding is digitized and logged, which makes the problem harder to see instead of easier.

I come from a union family, where the craft deserves respect, and respect includes not treating people as a translation problem to be solved with software. These tools are worth trying. They are not worth believing in.

The limits of this analysis

Be straight about what the data does not prove. CPWR's attribution of the fatality gap to language barriers is correlational, because smaller-employer concentration, piecework pay, and immigration-status vulnerability all ride along in the same data, and no study has isolated translation's share of the 107.1 percent increase. None of the AI vendors publish independent outcome data on injury reduction. Clemson tested usability, not field results. That 38 percent figure is my own calculation, combining CPWR's 2022 Latino count with the BLS 2022 construction total, and the two datasets define their counts slightly differently, so treat it as approximate. Pricing for the construction-specific tools is not public, which is why the cost comparison above uses OSHA's published maximums rather than vendor sticker prices. What the data does prove is that the trend line is real, the gap is widening, and the industry's usual response, which has been nothing, is no longer cheaper than the alternative.
