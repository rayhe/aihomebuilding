Your ADU Plan Arrived in 40 Seconds. Nobody Asked Which Way the Wind Blows.

By Elena Vasquez, September 15, 2026, Architecture & Design

Picture the scene. A kitchen table in Van Nuys, late afternoon, the laptop glowing. On the screen, a rendering of a backyard cottage: cedar siding, a standing-seam roof, light pouring through tall windows onto pale oak floors. It took 40 seconds to generate and it cost nothing. She prints it, tapes it to the refrigerator, and starts imagining her mother living there by Christmas.

Now picture the same cottage in mid-September, after three days of Santa Anas, when the overnight low in the Valley never dropped below 78 and the air inside hangs thick enough to feel. Every operable window in the plan, bedroom and kitchen alike, opens onto the same fence, because the algorithm stacked the plumbing wall for efficiency and the render was lit like a showroom at noon.

This is the part of the AI design revolution nobody renders, and it is the part that matters most.

A million amateur architects

Maket, the Montreal startup its founders call the ChatGPT of architecture, has collected more than a million registered users since its platform launched in 2023, on roughly $4 million Canadian in funding (Tech Funding News). Type in your lot dimensions and a room list, and it generates floor plans, 3D visualizations, style variations, even answers to zoning questions, all in the time it takes to make coffee. Competitors with names like ARCHITECTUREs, ArkDesign AI, Archistar, and TestFit crowd the same space.

They arrived at exactly the moment California needed them. In 2018 the state permitted around 9,000 ADUs; in 2023, Los Angeles County alone permitted more than 45,000 (Brookings, August 2024). ADUs went from under 1 percent of new California construction before 2017 to roughly 20 percent, with more than 23,000 legally completed in 2023 (CA AB 1154 bill analysis). California now requires cities to maintain pre-approved ADU plan libraries. No building type in the state is more common than the backyard cottage, and the free AI plan generator is the first architect most of its builders will ever meet.

That should worry anyone who has ever slept in a badly designed small room in August.

What the algorithm optimizes, and what it cannot see

Generative plan tools are honest about what they do. They explore thousands of layouts against measurable criteria: floor-area efficiency, room count, adjacency, estimated cost. What they cannot measure, they cannot optimize, and the unmeasured list reads like a syllabus for the first year of architecture school.

Start with the breeze. Of six natural-ventilation strategies simulated in a recent review of the research literature, cross ventilation, air entering one wall and leaving through another, was identified as the optimal strategy for energy savings, particularly in the mild seasons when California homes need cooling most (Wiley ADCE review), and wind-tunnel work confirms that a building's shape governs how well it performs; in hot climates, cross night-purge ventilation, flushing the day's stored heat after sunset, was the best-performing summer comfort strategy tested in Cairo residences (Sedki et al.).

Here is the problem, and it is drawn right into the plan. A typical AI-generated ADU is a single-loaded rectangle, kitchen, bath, and bedroom strung along one corridor with every operable window facing the same way, because plumbing walls are cheapest when they stack. Air enters and has nowhere to go. Curtains billow in the render. Physics says the air stands still.

Then there is the egress window, the one the fire code cares about. Every sleeping room needs an emergency escape opening: at least 5.7 square feet of clear opening (5.0 at grade), no dimension under 20 by 24 inches, sill no higher than 44 inches off the floor. On paper, the generator draws a compliant window. Then the homeowner furnishes the room, and the queen bed goes against the only window wall, and the dresser slides in front of the lower sash. Research on indoor airflow has shown that furniture placement materially degrades ventilation and air quality (Yin et al., via the same review), and furniture has a fire-safety twin to that finding which nobody is studying, because the plan was approved with an empty room.

Daylight fails the same way, by the numbers. The working rule of thumb in daylighting design says useful daylight reaches about 2.5 times the window head height into a room, which means a generated plan 14 feet deep, with 7-foot window heads, goes dim in the middle by mid-afternoon, a fact the rendering, lit at a permanent golden noon, never mentions.

And storage, the quiet killer of small homes. A competent architect budgets roughly a tenth of a small home's floor area for closets, linen, and the vacuum cleaner. Generated plans routinely draw closets as afterthoughts or skip them entirely. Part of the square-footage "efficiency" the tool celebrates is storage it deleted.

The Three-Window Audit

None of this requires software to check. Before you pay a permit fee on any AI-generated plan, spend ten minutes with a printed copy and a pencil.

First, trace the breeze. From every operable window, draw a line to the nearest operable window on a different wall; where most lines dead-end, the plan is single-sided, and in a heat wave it will behave like one, so narrow the plan, add a second window wall, or move the bedroom to the corner where two exposures meet.

Second, furnish the egress. Sketch the bed, the dresser, and the nightstands into the bedroom at real sizes, then check that the escape window still opens fully clear with its sill at or below 44 inches, because if the only compliant window sits behind the headboard, the plan has failed its most important test.

Third, measure the daylight. From each window wall, mark off 2.5 times the window head height into the room, and treat everything past that line as borrowed light; where the middle of the plan falls past every line, the answer is a skylight, a narrower building, or windows on the opposite wall, not a brighter render.

Ten minutes, a pencil, no subscription.

What to actually do about it

Use the generator for what it does brilliantly, which is massing exploration: twenty bad ideas before breakfast, free, a genuine gift nobody in the history of residential design has had.

Then put the pencil down and pick up the free plans your city was required to publish. Under California's AB 1332, local governments must maintain pre-approved ADU designs or lists of them, which means code-vetted, structurally real plan sets are available at no cost in most jurisdictions. Start from one of those, and use the AI for siting and style exploration around it.

If you hire an architect, and a California ADU design typically runs $5,000 to $15,000, ask one question in the first meeting: show me the cross-ventilation path. If they cannot trace it on the spot, you hired a renderer, not a designer, and the AI would have done it cheaper.

The honest counterargument

Everything above has a shelf life. Maket already ships a regulatory assistant that answers zoning and code questions in plain language, and there is no technical reason a future generator cannot embed a ventilation simulation or an egress check alongside its cost estimate, because these tools are getting smarter, not dumber.

Meanwhile, human architects draw bad ADUs too, and plenty of permitted, professionally stamped plans would fail the Three-Window Audit. So the argument here is not that AI plans are uniquely bad; it is that their speed and polish launder away the skepticism a hand sketch would invite, since a pencil drawing that looks unfinished invites questions while a photorealistic render at golden hour invites a permit application. It was never the algorithm that posed the danger. It is the confidence the rendering lends to whatever the algorithm produced.

What this article cannot prove

A million registered users is a funnel, not a housing count; no public data shows how many generated plans reach a building department. Ventilation findings here come from simulations, wind tunnels, and CFD models, not randomized trials in occupied California ADUs. That daylight rule is a rule of thumb, and real performance depends on glazing, orientation, and shading. Egress dimensions are the IRC baseline; California amendments and your local building official have the final word. Treat the audit as a screen, not a stamp.

Still, run it: the breeze does not care who drew the plan.
