Your West-Facing Glass Adds $250 a Year to Your Cooling Bill. The Simulation Takes an Hour.

By Elena Vasquez · September 5, 2026

Four o'clock on an August afternoon in the Central Valley. The thermostat says 74. The living room says 81. Along one wall, fourteen feet of glass face due west, because the sunset over the foothills was the feature that sold the floor plan, the rendering the buyers fell in love with, the elevation the neighbors will photograph, and right now that glass is a radiator. The air conditioner has been running since two. It will not stop until the sun drops behind the ridge. Nobody involved in this house, not the architect, not the builder, not the energy consultant who signed the Title 24 forms, ever modeled what that wall of glass would cost to cool. It was never a calculation. It was a rendering.

Windows are the most consequential design decision in a home and the least analyzed one, specified by feel during design development and then locked in by the window order months before anyone with an energy model looks at the elevations. The Department of Energy attributes 25 to 30 percent of residential heating and cooling energy to windows, and estimates that upgrading the nation's window stock to its long-term performance goals could save more than 2 quadrillion BTU. That number is usually quoted by people selling windows, which should make you suspicious, but the physics underneath is not in dispute: glass is the worst insulator in the wall assembly, and afternoon sun through west-facing glass is the single largest cooling load most houses ever see. Every elevation drawing is, whether the architect admits it or not, an energy model, and most are just bad ones.

The simulation tools exist, one market tier above the houses

Commercial architects have been simulating this for years. Tools like cove.tool run daylight and energy analysis in a browser: spatial daylight autonomy, which measures the share of floor area getting enough natural light, and annual sunlight exposure, which flags the glare that makes a room unusable at 4 p.m. Ladybug Tools and Honeybee do the same thing as free plugins for Rhino and Grasshopper, with the academic literature to back them up, which means the most sophisticated daylight analysis available to residential design is free, open-source, and still ignored by nearly everyone drawing houses. A competent designer can test dozens of window configurations against cooling load, daylight, and glare in an afternoon, the way structural engineers have run load cases for decades, except the structural engineer's client is a building department that demands the math while the residential architect's client is a couple who fell in love with a rendering.

On a Georgia Tech campus building, the design team ran their first daylight study on a primitive massing and found the project hitting 31 percent spatial daylight autonomy against a 55 percent target, then used radiation studies to decide which facades deserved glass and which needed shading, and the design moved before a single window was specified. Nothing about this workflow is exotic anymore: it is standard practice on any commercial project with a sustainability consultant, which is to say the knowledge is not rare, the software is not expensive, and the only thing standing between a house and a daylight model is the habit of not doing one.

It is almost nonexistent on houses, where custom residential architects, the people drawing the $1.2 million homes with the fourteen feet of western glass, price by fee and sell by rendering. Simulation licenses and the staff to run them live one market tier up, with the commercial firms, who amortize the cost across fifty-story towers while the residential architect amortizes it across a kitchen remodel and a granny flat. Production homebuilders use platforms like Higharc that tie design directly to cost and constructability, but energy modeling is the missing column in those dashboards. So the most load-bearing aesthetic decision in residential architecture is still made by eye, by taste, by what photographs well at golden hour, and the rendering cannot feel the heat.

What the research actually says

The Pacific Northwest National Laboratory built two identical lab homes, same orientation, same weather, same simulated occupants, differing only in windows. The triple-pane package, with a U-factor roughly 70 percent lower than the standard double-pane, saved about 1,370 kilowatt-hours a year, roughly 13 percent of whole-house energy, in modeling; metered data extrapolated to 1,784 kWh. A peer-reviewed study on window-to-wall ratio and orientation concluded that east and west glass should be minimized or paired with shading, with bedrooms kept to the north and south faces. A separate hot-climate study found that pushing window area from 15 to 75 percent of wall area raised HVAC energy by about 30 percent, while a modest 25 percent ratio carried only a 5 percent penalty. And on the shading side, one detailed variant analysis showed exterior blinds cutting cooling demand by 30 percent, a bigger lever than the glazing itself.

Now the part nobody in the window industry will put in a brochure. Run the payback on those PNNL numbers at California electricity rates, around 38 cents a kilowatt-hour on a PG&E bill, and the celebrated window upgrade saves about $520 a year. A full high-performance window package for a 2,400-square-foot home runs $15,000 at the low end and $25,000 or more for the premium units the brochures picture. That is a 29-to-48-year payback on components warrantied for 20. The windows pay for themselves sometime after the mortgage is gone. Quoted honestly as the marginal premium over standard builder windows, the math improves to roughly six years, which tells you something about how the industry prefers to price the conversation.

But here is the number that should bother architects more, because it has nothing to do with which windows you buy. Take a 2,400-square-foot Central Valley home burning 5,000 kWh a year on cooling. Give windows their DOE-attributed quarter of the HVAC load, and let the afternoon glass carry half of that, which the orientation literature supports: east and west exposures dominate the solar gain. That is roughly 625 kWh a year, about $250 at California rates, spent cooling rooms whose glass faces the wrong direction. A one-hour daylight and energy simulation would have moved that glass to the north wall or sized the overhang to kill the afternoon sun. Nobody ran it. On a residential project, nobody runs anything.

Exterior shading on the west elevation, $3,000 to $5,000 for screens or retractable shades, cuts that penalty by roughly a third according to the variant studies. On energy savings alone the payback is measured in decades, not years, and an honest accounting says so, which is why the window industry's payback brochures always lead with the glass and never with the orientation that actually drives the bill. People buy shades for glare and comfort, and the energy is a bonus. That is exactly why the decision belongs inside a daylight model, where comfort is a metric, rather than inside a payback spreadsheet, where it is invisible.

The case for glass, stated at full strength

Glass sells houses, and this is the counterargument at full strength, which deserves to be stated without condescension. Natural light and views are consistently the top buyer preferences in residential surveys, and a wall of glass facing the sunset commands a real resale premium that no energy model prices in. Daylighting also displaces electric lighting, by 30 to 40 percent at generous window ratios in the hot-climate study, which means naive glass minimization produces dark interiors that burn more lighting energy and feel like bunkers. A home optimized purely for cooling load is a thermos with a great HERS score, and nobody wants to live in one.

There is also the accuracy problem: every one of these simulations assumes occupant behavior: thermostat setpoints, whether anyone actually deploys the shades, how often the windows open. PNNL is explicit that its savings figures are setpoint-specific. A household that keeps the house at 72 and never touches the exterior shades will not see the modeled savings, and the gap between simulated and metered performance is the oldest disappointment in building science.

And the vendors oversell: windows are 25 to 30 percent of HVAC energy, not of the utility bill, a distinction that evaporates somewhere between the DOE fact sheet and the showroom pitch. Anyone quoting you a single payback number without showing the inputs is selling. Not informing.

What to actually do

If you are designing or building: demand a daylight and glare study before the window order is placed, not after the drywall is up. One spatial-daylight-autonomy and glare run on cove.tool or Honeybee costs a fraction of a single window change order, and it answers the only question that matters: which elevations earn their glass and which ones are just heating the afternoon.

Learn the orientation rules, because the studies agree on them: concentrate glass on the south and north faces in cooling climates, minimize the west, and treat east glass with suspicion. On west and east exposures, specify glazing with a solar heat gain coefficient at or below 0.25, or accept that you are buying afternoon heat by the kilowatt-hour. California's Title 24 already caps west-facing glass in some compliance paths; a performance model can trade glazing area for other efficiency measures instead of just deleting windows.

Shade from the outside, where the heat actually is. Exterior shading, overhangs sized for the latitude, retractable screens, exterior blinds, intercepts solar radiation before it crosses the glass, which is why it outperforms both interior blinds and premium glazing on cooling payback. Interior shades are a glare solution; exterior shades are an energy solution. The difference is where the heat gets stopped.

If you are buying: walk the house at 4 p.m. in August, or ask which way the big glass faces and what the SHGC rating is. A living room wall of west-facing, high-SHGC glass is a design choice with a monthly invoice attached. It can still be the right choice. For the view. For the light. For the way the room feels at sunset. It should be a choice someone made on purpose, with the number attached. Not a rendering nobody questioned.

Methodology and limitations

The $520-a-year figure multiplies PNNL's modeled 1,370 kWh annual saving by $0.38/kWh, a representative California residential rate. The 29-to-48-year payback divides $15,000 and $25,000 package costs by that saving; the "roughly six years" marginal figure assumes a $3,000 premium over standard windows, which is illustrative rather than quoted. The $250-a-year west-glass penalty assumes 5,000 kWh of annual cooling, assigns windows one quarter of the HVAC load per DOE, and attributes half of the window load to east and west exposures per the orientation literature: 5,000 x 0.25 x 0.5 = 625 kWh, times $0.38. The "roughly a third" shading reduction comes from the variant study's 30 percent cooling cut with exterior blinds. Extrapolating PNNL's Pacific Northwest heating-climate data to California cooling loads is conservative on rates and aggressive on climate; the direction of the comparison is robust, the decimals are not.

What this analysis did not do: no single U.S. study measures AI-optimized window placement on a tract home, so the savings are stitched across climates and building types. Heating-dominated climates invert part of the advice, since south glass is a net winter benefit there, and a designer in Minneapolis who reads this and deletes the south glass has missed the point as thoroughly as the one in Fresno who added the west glass. Adoption rates for simulation tools among residential architects have no public market data; the characterization comes from vendor case libraries and the commercial-residential fee divide. Cooling savings also assume the shades get used, the thermostat stays reasonable, and the occupants are not running the AC at 68 with the windows open, which the model cannot prevent and the literature politely declines to discuss.
