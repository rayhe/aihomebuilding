Catherine "Code" Chen July 29, 2026

Denver Spent $4.6 Million on AI to Review Building Permits. It Checks Whether You Remembered to Attach the Site Plan.

In March, Denver approved a five-year, $4.6 million contract with Clariti for its AI-powered platform, CivCheck. Press releases used words like "modernize" and "accelerate." Officials wanted to rescue a permitting process so broken that only 37 percent of applications cleared review on the first attempt.

Sixty-three percent failed. Not because architects drew bad buildings or engineers miscalculated load paths or anyone violated a fire code. Because applicants forgot to attach a document. Left a field blank. Submitted plans missing a required sheet. CivCheck catches those errors before a human reviewer ever sees the application, which makes it, in the most literal sense available, a spell-checker for paperwork.

Useful, yes, but not what the headlines describe.

What Nineteen Permits Prove

Honolulu's Department of Planning and Permitting launched CivCheck in December 2025 and now reports that average review times fell from 73 days to 32.5 days, a 55 percent reduction that sounds transformative until you find the methodology note buried in the department's own disclosure. Nineteen CivCheck-processed residential permits were compared against 17 non-CivCheck projects.

Nineteen.

Honolulu processes roughly 20,000 building permit applications per year and authorizes about 1,400 new private housing units annually, which means the entire statistical foundation for that 55 percent claim could shift by a week if a single outlier were added or removed from either group. A department spokesperson acknowledged the limitation to GovTech: "As additional applications are completed, DPP will continue to validate and report these results using a much larger dataset." When that larger dataset might arrive was not specified.

Per-review numbers hold up better because they measure a mechanical process rather than an outcome. Honolulu reports that time spent on a single residential plan check dropped from roughly 90 minutes to 20 or 30 minutes, a 64 percent reduction, though that figure originates with Clariti, which claims "97%+ accuracy" in flagging compliance issues at intake. No independent validation of that number appears in any public document this reporter could locate.

Intake, Not Judgment

Every city deploying these tools is solving the same problem, and it is not the one most applicants assume. Permit delays do not stem primarily from slow reviewers. Delays compound because applicants submit incomplete or inconsistent packages that bounce back for correction, re-enter the queue, and force reviewers to spend time on identical projects multiple times before anything gets approved.

Denver's 37 percent first-try rate meant the average application consumed roughly three review cycles. In Honolulu, before CivCheck, the average was 3.4 cycles, each carrying a queue wait of approximately 21 days. CivCheck compressed that to 1.4.

Now run the carrying-cost arithmetic on a $400,000 residential construction project financed at current construction loan rates near 8 percent, which comes to roughly $87 per day in interest alone, meaning each avoided 21-day resubmission cycle saves approximately $1,830 in carrying costs, and eliminating two unnecessary cycles saves roughly $3,660 per permit while Denver's contract, amortized across an estimated 15,000 residential permits per year over five years, costs about $61 per permit. Sixty-one dollars to save thousands. Good math.

But only for permits that would have been incomplete without help. If 63 percent of Denver's submissions historically needed correction, CivCheck delivers value on those and adds a $61 cost to every application that was already fine. In San Jose, where a pilot found 75 percent of accessory dwelling unit applications arrived missing required documents, the economics are stronger still. In jurisdictions where application quality already runs high, they invert.

Spell-Check, Not Judge

Dawn Takeuchi Apuna, who directs Honolulu's permitting department, described CivCheck as "kind of like TurboTax for permitting." Jerome Thibaudeau, who manages planning technology for Surrey, British Columbia, called Archistar's competing product "spell-check for zoning." Both analogies are more honest than anything in vendor press releases, and both point at a structural reality that matters: these tools reduce how many times a permit gets reviewed, not how long each review takes.

A plan reviewer spending 20 minutes checking a residential addition for setback compliance, structural adequacy, fire separation, and egress does identical work regardless of whether CivCheck pre-screened the submission. AI does not evaluate structural engineering here. It does not interpret ambiguous code provisions where reasonable professionals disagree, or coordinate reviews across water, sewer, fire, and transportation agencies, which is often where residential permits stall for months, or adjudicate zoning variances requiring public notice and discretionary board decisions that no algorithm can shortcut.

What it does: confirm that page three of your structural drawings is present. Flag a blank lot-coverage field. Verify you checked the correct box for construction type. Fast, automatable, and genuinely useful for homeowners who have never assembled a permit package before and would otherwise discover their missing site plan six weeks later when a reviewer sends everything back.

Fewer Staff, Same Promise

Denver approved CivCheck the same year it cut 59 budgeted positions from Community Planning and Development, bringing total staff to 251. Not a coincidence and not a scandal, but a deliberate choice. Cities under budget pressure are using AI intake tools to maintain processing times while reducing headcount, and that trade-off works until it doesn't.

Clariti's own materials emphasize that "human reviewers still evaluate final submitted plans." CivCheck's CEO, Dheekshita Kumar, has stressed repeatedly that her platform is "designed to augment rather than replace staff." Augmentation with fewer staff is a different proposition than augmentation alongside the same team, though, and the distinction becomes load-bearing when a surge of applications overwhelms reduced review capacity or a project falls into the gap between what AI flags and what only a human catches.

No city has published data on that failure mode. At 97 percent accuracy, CivCheck misses roughly three items per hundred checks. Whether those missed items are trivial (a redundant form field, a duplicate signature block) or consequential (a setback encroachment triggering a zoning variance, a fire separation deficiency changing occupancy classification) is a question nobody outside Clariti has tested. Their accuracy metric does not distinguish between the two, and no public audit has forced the distinction.

Where It Lands

If you are a homeowner planning a renovation, addition, or ADU, the guidance is simple. In cities deploying CivCheck or Archistar, use it before you submit. Costs you nothing and can save weeks by catching errors that would have sent your application back to the end of the queue. Run it, fix what it flags, understand that passing does not mean your project is approved.

If you are a builder running multiple projects, you already know how to submit complete applications, so the direct benefit is marginal while the indirect benefit might matter: clearing amateur submissions from the queue faster, freeing reviewer bandwidth for your work, assuming staffing levels hold in the cities adopting this technology most aggressively, which is exactly what Denver's 59-position cut calls into question.

Broader claims that AI is "reviewing" building permits deserve the same scrutiny you would apply to any vendor marketing a government contract. Intake automation is real, but judgment automation is not. Twenty minutes a plan reviewer spends deciding whether your foundation detail meets seismic provisions of the 2024 IRC is twenty minutes no algorithm is prepared to replace, and the cities writing these checks know it even when their press releases suggest otherwise.

Limitations

Honolulu's published results rest on 19 completed CivCheck permits compared against 17 non-CivCheck projects. Denver has not published outcome data. Clariti's 97 percent accuracy figure has no independent validation in any public study. Edmonton's same-day permit processing applies only to single and semi-detached homes in certain zones, not to full residential construction. Carrying-cost calculations here use 8 percent construction loan rates and a $400,000 project value as representative inputs; actual costs vary substantially by market, project type, and financing structure. No city has published data quantifying what share of total permit delay is attributable to application quality versus staffing shortages, inter-agency coordination, or discretionary review complexity.
