Your AC Runs All Night on the Hottest Air of the Day. A $1,800 Fan Waits for Sunset.

By Priya Greenwood · October 8, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

At 9 p.m. on an August night in Sacramento, the air outside is 68 degrees and falling. Inside a typical ranch house, it is 84. Meanwhile the air conditioner grinds away at peak evening rates, cooling air that the night already cooled for free, because nobody opened a path between the two. This happens in millions of homes, every summer night, and it is one of the purest wastes of electricity in residential construction: we pay to manufacture cold air while cold air sits outside the window, waiting.

A whole-house fan is the path. It is a big, quiet fan mounted in the ceiling, usually in a central hallway, that pulls cool evening air in through open windows and pushes the day's accumulated heat out through the attic. No refrigerant. No compressor. Just the temperature difference the planet provides every evening, moved by a motor the size of a breadbox.

The numbers are almost embarrassing: a modern ECM whole-house fan draws around 400 watts. Run it five hours on a summer evening and you have burned 2 kilowatt-hours, which at California's 35 cents per kWh costs 70 cents. A 3-ton central AC doing the same five hours of cooling draws roughly 3.5 kilowatts, or 17.5 kWh, which costs $6.13. Same house, same evening, at one-tenth the electricity. Figures from the California Energy Commission put the cooling-energy reduction at 50 to 90 percent versus central air, and PG&E field data showed 63 percent less electricity in peak cooling months, with a Lawrence Berkeley National Lab test dropping interiors from 84 to 68 degrees in under 15 minutes.

Seventy cents a night, and that is the number to hold onto.

Payback Math

Run the ledger the way a utility bill would. A professionally installed ducted whole-house fan for a 1,500 to 2,500 square foot home costs $1,500 to $2,800, with most Southern California installs landing between $800 and $1,800 according to contractor surveys, and QuietCool's own installed packages starting around $1,449, so call it $1,800 for a typical job.

At $5.43 saved per usable night ($6.13 of AC minus $0.70 of fan), ninety cooling nights a year returns about $489. Payback: under four years. After that, the fan is a small machine printing $400-plus a year in avoided electricity, with a 15-year motor warranty backing the bet. There is also a bonus the ledger misses: summer attics run 40 to 50 degrees hotter than outside air, and purging that heat in the evening means the AC starts the next day against a cooler attic instead of an oven, which trims the daytime load too.

California liked this math enough to write it into law: in 2013, the Title 24 update made whole-house fans a prescriptive requirement in new homes, and current standards require the fan's airflow and wattage to be HERS-verified in the field. When the energy code treats a technology as the default rather than the upgrade, the debate about whether it works is over.

Your Attic Will Starve the Fan

Here is the finding that should be on every quote and is not. A whole-house fan does not cool the house directly. It pressurizes the attic, and the attic vents are the exhaust. If the exhaust is too small, the fan suffocates: airflow drops, noise climbs, and the 4,800 CFM on the spec sheet becomes a fantasy number.

Sizing math is simple. Almost nobody runs the second half of it, though the first half is arithmetic every contractor knows: manufacturers size fans at 2 CFM per square foot of living space, so a 2,400 square foot home needs 4,800 CFM. Those same manufacturers require 1 square foot of net free attic vent area per 750 CFM of fan, which means that 4,800 CFM fan needs 6.4 square feet of unobstructed upper venting. Ridge vent delivers roughly 18 square inches per linear foot, so 6.4 square feet demands about 51 feet of ridge vent. A typical 2,400 square foot ranch has 55 to 65 feet of ridge, just enough if the ridge vent is continuous and the soffits are clear.

Now walk into the housing stock where retrofits actually happen. Homes with two small gable vents and no ridge vent have perhaps 2 to 3 square feet of exhaust area, less than half of what the fan needs, while homes with painted-shut soffits or insulation stuffed into the eaves have plenty of exhaust and no intake, which is the same failure with the plumbing reversed. A contractor's quote covers the fan, the electrical, and the ceiling cut. It does not cover the $200 to $500 of additional venting the attic needs, because nobody measured the attic. Ask your installer for the net-free-area calculation before you sign, and watch the room go quiet. That silence is the sound of a number nobody computed.

"Smart" Controllers Are Not Smart

This is the AI angle, and it is mostly a gap. Today's smart controls, QuietCool's app-based Smart Control being the flagship, adjusts fan speed from attic temperature and humidity presets and shuts off at 182 degrees as a fire precaution. Useful. Not intelligent.

What no mainstream controller does is the obvious thing, fusing the weather forecast with indoor and outdoor temperature sensors, humidity, and the home's thermal-mass lag to pick the run window automatically, because the optimal start time is not "when the attic hits 80" but a function of tonight's low, tomorrow's high, the humidity curve, and how many hours your walls hold cool. A $30 pair of temperature sensors and a free forecast API could beat the $200 hub on every metric that matters. Manufacturers sell hardware presets, not algorithms, which means the smartest whole-house fan controller you can buy today is a thermostat with ambition. Buy the ECM variable-speed motor for the efficiency and the quiet, and skip the premium for "AI," because there is none in the box.

Where This Can Hurt You

A whole-house fan is an exhaust fan the size of a doorway. Run one in a house with an atmospherically vented gas water heater or furnace, and the negative pressure can pull combustion gases down the flue and into the living space. NYSERDA's ventilation guide treats this as a pre-installation check, not a footnote, because backdrafting "can quickly cause severe injury or even death." The test is old and free: turn on every exhaust device in the house, including the new fan, hold a smoking incense stick at the water heater's draft hood, and watch which way the smoke goes. If it drifts into the room instead of up the flue, you have a problem that predates the fan and the fan just exposed it. Sealed-combustion appliances are immune. Atmospheric ones need the test, full stop.

Then the honest list of where this does not work, because the strongest case against the fan deserves its full weight. Roughly the entire Gulf Coast and South Florida are out: summer nights stay above 75 degrees with punishing humidity, and a whole-house fan imports moisture it cannot remove, since it does not dehumidify. It does not filter, so pollen season and wildfire smoke season are shutdown seasons and running one during a smoke event is actively dangerous; old belt-drive units are loud enough to end conversations, and open windows at night are a security tradeoff not everyone will take. And lightweight construction with little thermal mass holds the night's cool for hours, not all day, which shrinks the benefit toward the mild-climate wood-frame homes that need it least.

One more, because the Department of Energy insists and it is right: a whole-house fan is "essentially a large, uninsulated hole in the ceiling" all winter, unless you cover it. An insulated cover or motorized R-38 damper doors are not accessories; they are the difference between a cooling upgrade and a heating penalty.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The dollar ledger is built from stated assumptions, 400 watts, five hours, 35 cents per kWh, ninety usable nights, and your nights, rates, and climate will differ, especially outside the dry West. That 50 to 90 percent CEC range spans multiple studies rather than one controlled trial, and the PG&E and LBNL figures come via commission reporting rather than peer-reviewed papers I could independently verify. My attic-vent starvation math uses manufacturer guidance for the 1-square-foot-per-750-CFM rule, which is not code, and real attic venting varies with every roof. The no-forecast-optimization claim rests on current product pages and manuals, not an audit of every device on the market, and the field moves fast. Prevalence of under-vented attics is inferred from inspector experience and forum reports, not a measured national survey.

What to Do Before Sunset

If your summer nights drop at least 10 degrees below your indoor comfort target, price the fan: 2 to 3 CFM per square foot, ECM variable speed, installed by someone who will also compute your attic's net free vent area and add venting if you are short. Budget the extra $200 to $500 for vents the way you would budget the electrical. They are part of the machine.

If you have atmospherically vented gas appliances, run the incense test before the fan goes in, not after. If you are in a humid climate, near wildfire country, or on a street where open windows at night feel wrong, this is not your technology, and no payback math changes that.

And buy the insulated winter cover on day one. The cheapest cooling upgrade in the house should not become the most expensive heating leak.

Seventy cents a night: the air is free, the math is not.
