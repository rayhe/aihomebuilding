Your Lot Was Upzoned on July 1. The State Says 160 Units Per Acre. Your City Might Disagree.

By Catherine Chen · September 12, 2026
← All Stories

Somewhere in El Cerrito sits a 5,000-square-foot lot with a one-story ranch house on it, a ten-minute walk from BART. On June 30, that lot was zoned for exactly one home. On July 1, the state of California said it could hold eighteen apartments. City hall has not finished deciding whether it agrees. Eighteen to one.

That gap, between what Sacramento now allows and what your city will actually permit, is the most important number in California housing right now. It is also a number that no consumer-facing tool will compute for you, because half of it is still being drawn on planning-department whiteboards.

Behind the upzoning is Senate Bill 79, the Abundant and Affordable Homes Near Transit Act, signed by Governor Gavin Newsom on October 10, 2025 and effective July 1, 2026, after a legislative fight in which Los Angeles Democrats joined Republicans in opposition and lost anyway. It is the most aggressive state override of local zoning California has ever enacted. Near qualifying transit stops in eight urban counties, it sets minimum heights and densities that cities must allow, no matter what their zoning maps say.

How the Tiers Work

Eight counties are covered: Los Angeles, Orange, San Diego, Alameda, San Francisco, San Mateo, Santa Clara, and Sacramento. Within a half-mile of a qualifying transit-oriented development stop, projects must clear the greater of five homes, 30 homes per acre, or the local minimum density, and the average home cannot exceed 1,750 square feet of habitable space. Affordability requirements are written into the statute itself: 7 percent for extremely-low income, 10 percent for very-low income, or 13 percent for low income households, with the local inclusionary ordinance applying if it demands more, and developments of ten homes or fewer are exempt entirely.

Headline numbers are tiered by transit quality. Tier 1 stops, heavy rail like BART and the LA Metro B and D lines, or commuter rail running 72 or more trains a day like Caltrain, get the biggest allowances: within a quarter-mile, at least 120 homes per acre, 75 feet of height, and 3.5 floor-area ratio, while Tier 2 stops, light rail and rapid bus, get less. Parcels within 200 feet of a station entrance, the statute's definition of "adjacent," earn an intensifier on top: 40 more homes per acre, 20 more feet of height, and 1.0 more FAR, which is where the headline 160-per-acre figure comes from. Los Angeles published the clearest version of the distance decay in its phased implementation model, and while that model is an implementation choice rather than the statute itself, it remains the best available picture of how the allowances attenuate with distance: within 200 feet of a Tier 1 stop, 160 homes per acre at 4.5 floor-area ratio; within a quarter-mile, 120 per acre; within a half-mile, 100 per acre at 3.0 FAR.

The Math on One Lot

Nobody publishes the per-lot arithmetic. So here it is, on that 5,000-square-foot El Cerrito lot, which is 0.1148 acres, assuming it sits within 200 feet of a Tier 1 station entrance, close enough to earn the adjacency intensifier:

Distance from Tier 1 stopDensity allowedMax homes on 5,000 sq ft
Within 200 feet160 homes/acre18
Within 1/4 mile120 homes/acre13
Within 1/2 mile100 homes/acre11

Two constraints bind at different points, which is the part most summaries skip. At the closest ring, the floor-area ratio is 4.5, so the lot can hold 22,500 square feet of building. Eighteen homes averaging 1,000 square feet fit inside that envelope. But if you build to the 1,750-square-foot average the law allows, density is no longer the binding constraint: 22,500 divided by 1,750 is roughly 12 large homes. Small homes are capped by density; big homes are capped by FAR, and your pro forma is irrelevant to the code on this point, which enforces both constraints at once.

Now the money: multifamily land in the inner East Bay trades around $60,000 to $100,000 per entitled home, a range, not a quote. At the midpoint, eighteen homes imply about $1.44 million of residual land value on a lot that, as a single-family parcel, might be worth $400,000 to $600,000. Overnight, the upzoning created on the order of a million dollars of latent value. That is the number that should make every owner within a half-mile of BART sit down with a calculator. Do not call anyone yet.

But before you call a contractor, the offsets: at eighteen homes, the inclusionary requirement means two to three of them must be income-restricted. Cross 85 feet and state labor standards attach. And eighteen homes at, say, 1,000 square feet each is 18,000 square feet of mid-rise construction at California costs of roughly $450 to $550 per square foot, which is about $9 million before soft costs, financing, or the land itself. That arithmetic clears only if the rents or sale prices at the end justify it.

An Asterisk the Size of City Hall

SB 79 lets cities draw their own maps, and they are drawing them right now. Under the statute, a city can permanently exclude industrial employment hubs, parcels with no walkable route under a mile to the station, very-high fire severity zones, historic resources, and sea-level-rise areas. Los Angeles has already carved out 2,377 sites in six industrial zones, from 3,623 acres around LAX to 312 acres in Atwater Village and Cypress Park, plus another 341 sites where its planners, running street-network analysis in ArcGIS, found no walkable sub-one-mile path to a station.

Separately, cities may adopt a transit-oriented alternative plan instead of the state defaults, subject to state housing department review. Any alternative must preserve the same total zoned capacity, cannot cut any individual site below half of its SB 79 density, and cannot concentrate capacity by crediting any site at more than double its baseline. San Francisco's planning department calculates SB 79 at 763,000 homes of net zoned capacity citywide and is pursuing its Family Zoning Plan as the alternative vehicle, while San Diego's planners put the required capacity at 861,000 homes with current zoning covering about 57 percent; state guidance issued March 20, 2026 added four bus rapid transit stations to the map after the initial analysis, so even the denominator is moving, and both cities' alternative plans are exercises in distributing enormous paper capacity without tripping the statute's per-site or concentration limits. According to Holland & Knight's analysis, most cities could not complete a compliant alternative plan before the July 1 effective date and are using temporary exclusions as the near-term tool instead.

Read that again, because the implications are uncomfortable: the exclusions are temporary, the alternative plans are unfinished, and the state guidance is being revised after publication. Anyone telling you today exactly what your parcel allows is selling certainty that does not exist yet.

What the Software Knows, and What It Does Not

This is where AI enters the story, honestly and with limits. Serious parcel-capacity modeling in California housing policy runs on tools like MapCraft Labs and UrbanFootprint: the Terner Center used MapCraft's financial feasibility model on UrbanFootprint's parcel dataset, covering some 7.5 million single-family parcels, to analyze SB 9. Firms like Gridics sell cities 3D parcel-level zoning visualization; Cupertino is a customer. Generative site planners like TestFit can mass a mid-rise envelope on a lot in minutes. These tools compute the state-law default with real precision.

What none of them tracks is the moving target. City implementing ordinances, temporary exclusion lists, alternative-plan drafts, and HCD review letters live in planning-commission packets and staff memos, not in any parcel database. There is no consumer product that will tell you your lot is inside Los Angeles's 341-site walk-path exclusion or outside it. No software currently bridges the gap between the algorithm's answer and the city's answer. It is bridgeable by reading your city's SB 79 staff report, which is less satisfying but more accurate. Paper beats software.

A Precedent Nobody Wants to Discuss

SB 9 was supposed to end single-family zoning. In its one-year review, the Terner Center found that in 2022, Los Angeles received 211 applications for new SB 9 homes and approved 38. San Diego received seven; San Francisco approved four of 25. Terner's modeling concluded that nearly 97 percent of single-family homes would never make financial sense to redevelop. Upzoning is not building. Zoning capacity sat on paper while construction costs, interest rates, and the hassle factor did the real regulating.

SB 79's bar is higher in every dimension that mattered for SB 9. Mid-rise construction costs more per square foot than a backyard duplex, inclusionary requirements bite above ten homes, and the 85-foot labor threshold turns a wood-frame project into a different kind of project entirely. Demolition protections under SB 330 and the Housing Crisis Act can block redevelopment of tenant-occupied properties outright. Honestly, SB 79 creates option value, not housing, until financing costs fall, labor rules clarify, and the local maps settle. Anyone modeling eighteen homes on a ranch-house lot should model the scenario where the answer is still one ranch house in 2030.

If This Is Your Lot

First, find your city's SB 79 implementation page and read the staff report, not the press release. Look for the exclusion list and whether an alternative plan is in draft. Second, measure the half-mile as the city measures it: several cities are using walk-path distance, not straight lines, and San Diego's rule excludes sites with no walking route under a mile. Third, identify your stop's tier, since heavy rail and rapid bus produce very different numbers. Fourth, if tenants live on the property, understand the SB 330 demolition protections before spending a dollar on design; they can end the conversation. Fifth, price the building honestly: get an architect's read on whether your envelope wants cheaper wood-frame or a concrete podium, because that choice, more than the density number, decides whether the project pencils.

Sacramento gave you the density. Your city is still deciding how much of it survives contact with the map. Do the math yourself; nobody else will do it for you.

Limitations of This Analysis

The worked example is illustrative, using Bay Area cost and land-value ranges that will not transfer to Los Angeles, San Diego, or Sacramento markets. Exclusion maps and alternative plans are city-specific and actively changing; this article cannot tell you whether a specific parcel is excluded. I found no consumer tool that tracks city-level SB 79 exclusions in real time, which is itself the finding, but the AI feasibility tools named here are described from their public materials and I did not independently verify their current feature sets. One more boundary worth stating plainly: in unincorporated county areas, the statute does not apply until the seventh regional housing needs cycle, so a parcel in unincorporated San Mateo County near Caltrain is on a different clock than the same parcel inside city limits. Construction costs of $450 to $550 per square foot and land values of $60,000 to $100,000 per entitled home are ranges drawn from industry reporting, not quotes for any specific project. Inclusionary rounding conventions vary by jurisdiction, and this is zoning analysis, not legal advice, and not a substitute for a project pro forma.
