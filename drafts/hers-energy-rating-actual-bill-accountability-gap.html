Your Builder Promised a $1,200 Energy Bill. Nobody Checked Whether He Was Right.

The HERS Index compares your home to a 2006 building code. The gap between that score and your actual energy bill averages 30 percent. A device that costs half the rating fee would close it.

By Priya Greenwood July 24, 2026

Heavy stock. Bold font. A HERS Index score of 52, meaning the house was 48 percent more efficient than a reference home. That reference home was built to the 2006 International Energy Conservation Code. It was 2026. Twenty years separated the benchmark from the buyer.

Nobody mentioned that.

Estimated annual energy costs: $1,247. Marketing materials called the home "high-performance." She closed in March. By September, her actual utility bills totaled $1,780 for a period the model had predicted $830. She called her builder. He pointed to the fine print.

D.R. Horton, the largest homebuilder in the United States by volume, publishes that fine print on its HERS page. It reads: "D.R. Horton does not warrant or guarantee that the home as-built or as used by its occupants will achieve the stated HERS Index score." And further: "D.R. Horton does not warrant or guarantee the monthly or annual utility bills for the home." It also notes that not all homes sold have been individually rated. Some scores derive from average performance for home categories, not from the specific house you bought.

Read that again. America's largest homebuilder prints an energy score on your paperwork, uses it to market efficiency, and simultaneously disclaims responsibility for whether it's accurate.

A Benchmark Nobody Updated

HERS was not designed to predict your energy bill. It was designed to compare homes. RESNET, the organization that manages it, built the scale around a reference home pegged to the 2006 IECC. A score of 100 means your home performs at that 2006 baseline. Lower is better. Zero is net-zero.

Any home built to current code, without a single efficiency upgrade, scores around 70 automatically. RESNET's national average is 61. In Arizona, where 63 percent of new-construction homes carry a HERS rating, that average sits at 59.

Certificates cite savings "relative to an average U.S. home." Green Builder Media has pointed out the ambiguity: Is that an average new home? An average existing home? An average across all homes? Nobody defines it on the document. Green Builder concluded: "The only assumption I can make is that homebuyers will interpret the savings cited on a customized certificate for their home as the utility bill they should experience, a huge over-promise."

Mortgage bankers created this system in the early 1980s. They wanted a way to factor energy savings into lending decisions. RESNET formalized it. HERS raters became the verification layer. Over the decades, the rating migrated from lending niche to marketing centerpiece. What it actually measured never changed.

Thirty Percent, Give or Take a Hundred

Building scientists have a name for this mismatch between prediction and reality. They call it the energy performance gap. Researchers have been measuring it for decades, and their findings are not reassuring.

Mahdavi and colleagues reviewed 144 studies of the EPG in residential buildings and found a mean gap of approximately 30 percent. But variance was staggering: actual energy use ranged from 25 percent below predictions to 96 percent above them. Your certificate might be close. It might be off by half. You have no way to know which.

Bai and colleagues analyzed 76 low-energy building case studies. Actual energy use was 86 percent lower to 483 percent higher than predicted, with an average relative difference of 58 percent. When a range spans nearly sixfold, calling it a "prediction" is generous.

Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory studied this in 1996. Rating systems overestimated energy costs by approximately 50 percent, and the lab found "no clear relationship between rating score and actual cost." That was thirty years ago. More data has accumulated since. A fix has not.

A study of nearly 90,000 renovated dwellings in the Netherlands found that 57 percent had lower energy savings than expected. Homes where pre-renovation performance was worst showed the largest gap, exactly the homes where accuracy mattered most.

Jeff Ross Stein, writing in Home Energy, described what HERS actually predicts: "individual potential" for energy use, not actual energy use. DOE's own Home Energy Score documentation calls its output an "asset rating," meaning it reflects structure and systems, "not how the occupants use the home." Both statements are honest. Neither appears in bold on the certificate.

How Disclaimers Became a Business Model

D.R. Horton is not an outlier. It is an indicator.

Roughly 90,000 homes a year. Publicly traded. Lawyers reviewed every word of that HERS disclaimer. In effect, it says: we will show you a number, we will use that number to sell you a home, and we will not stand behind that number once you move in.

Fine Homebuilding has flagged another flaw. A 10,000-square-foot home with a HERS score of 50 appears more efficient than a 2,000-square-foot home scoring 100. It is not. A house five times larger will almost certainly consume more total energy despite the lower score. HERS measures intensity, not consumption. Nobody explains that on the sales floor.

RESNET's certificate design places the savings figure in bold, extra-large font. Caveats appear in standard text below. Not an accident of graphic design. Persuasion architecture applied to building science.

Commercial Buildings Get a Check-Up. Yours Gets a Certificate.

In a commercial building, the gap between predicted and actual performance is a liability. ASHRAE Standard 211 defines energy audits. ASHRAE Guideline 14 sets measurement and verification protocols. LEED buildings can pursue performance credits requiring actual metered data. IPMVP provides a standardized framework for confirming that energy measures deliver what they promised.

Building management systems, the software and sensors that track this performance, represent a global market exceeding $7 billion, growing at roughly 12 percent annually. Commercial landlords monitor energy because tenants demand it, ESG reporting requires it, and utility incentive programs reward it.

Residential homes get none of this. You receive a rating before the drywall goes up and a certificate at closing. After that, the feedback loop closes. No measurement. No verification. No accountability.

Here is the irony: residential monitoring hardware already exists. Emporia's Vue energy monitor costs about $200, clamps onto your circuit panel, samples current at 24,000 readings per second, and reports whole-home and circuit-level consumption to an app with plus-or-minus 2 percent accuracy. It includes an AI assistant that analyzes usage patterns and identifies anomalies. Sense makes a similar device. Span builds an entire smart panel that replaces your breaker box and monitors every circuit.

None of them reconcile your actual consumption against your HERS prediction. Data exists on one side. Predictions exist on the other. No product connects them.

What an AI Feedback Loop Would Look Like

A $200 monitor records circuit-level consumption. HERS models already contain predicted loads broken down by heating, cooling, hot water, lighting, and appliances. An ML model trained on divergence between actual and predicted values, corrected for weather data and occupancy patterns, could tell you within three months whether your home performs as rated.

It could also tell you why it doesn't. If heating loads exceed predictions by 40 percent but cooling matches, the problem is probably envelope-related: insulation gaps, air sealing failures, thermal bridging. If everything runs high, suspect duct leakage. If hot water dominates, you have a behavioral issue, not a construction issue. Different problems. Different solutions. A monitoring system that understands the prediction model could distinguish them automatically.

Commercial buildings already do this. Fault detection and diagnostics platforms from Clockworks Analytics, Brainbox AI, and CopperTree Analytics ingest BMS data and compare it to expected performance models continuously. They flag drift. They identify failing equipment. They quantify the cost of each deviation. All of it works. None of it has been adapted for residential because nobody demanded it.

Warranty timing makes the silence easier. Most builder warranties cover HVAC systems for two years. Many new-home buyers don't receive their first full year of utility bills until that window has significantly narrowed. By the time they realize energy performance doesn't match the certificate, their builder's obligation has expired.

A Score That Compares You to Yourself

A useful version of this system would rate the home before occupancy using existing HERS or DOE methodology, then verify performance after 12 months using metered data. Post-occupancy scores would adjust for weather via degree-day data from the nearest NOAA station and for occupancy using self-reported data or smart thermostat metadata. One number would result: the ratio of actual performance to predicted performance.

A ratio of 1.0 means the home performed as rated. A ratio of 1.3 means 30 percent more energy than predicted. A ratio of 0.85 means outperformance. Simple. Auditable. Comparable.

RESNET could add this as a voluntary program. Builders who participate would demonstrate confidence in their construction quality. Builders who decline would face the obvious inference. Insurance companies could use the ratio to adjust premiums. Mortgage lenders, the same industry that originally requested HERS, could recalibrate their energy-cost assumptions with data instead of models.

Original HERS ratings cost $400 to $800. Post-occupancy verification using a circuit monitor and automated analysis could cost less than half that. Hardware is a one-time purchase. Analysis is software.

What You Should Do

If you are buying a new home with a HERS score: ask your builder what happens if actual energy bills exceed estimated costs by 30 percent or more. Watch the response. If they point to the fine print, you have your answer.

If you already own a rated home: install a circuit-level energy monitor. Prices range from $150 to $400 depending on sensor count. Track consumption for a full year across all four seasons. Compare your annual total to the estimated annual energy cost on your HERS certificate. If the gap exceeds 25 percent, document it and contact your HERS rater for an explanation. They won't be required to give you one. But asking is worth doing.

If you are a builder: consider what it would mean to voluntarily verify energy performance post-occupancy. Whoever does this first will own a credibility gap that everyone else is pretending doesn't exist.

HERS measures what it was designed to measure. It compares homes to a two-decade-old code baseline. It does not predict your bill, verify your builder's claims, or hold anyone accountable for performance. An algorithm trained on your actual data, running on hardware that costs less than the rating itself, could do all three. Ready technology. Missing incentive.
