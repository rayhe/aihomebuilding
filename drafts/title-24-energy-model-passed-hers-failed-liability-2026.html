Your Energy Model Passed Title 24. The Field Test Failed It. You Are Holding the Bill.

By Catherine Chen · September 6, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

A production builder I know in the Central Valley got the news every builder wants in March. His energy consultant's model showed the new plan beating the Title 24 standard budget by three percent. Clean pass. His Certificate of Compliance went into the permit file, the city stamped the plans, and forty lots started moving dirt.

Six months later the field verification rater showed up with a duct blaster, and Lot 14 failed the duct leakage test. Under Title 24's own sampling rules, that single failure forced the rater to pull a second home from the same sample group and test it too. Lot 22 failed as well, and at that point the group's verification status collapsed entirely: every untested home in the group lost its standing until each one was fixed, retested, and re-registered. One leaky duct system became a multi-home remediation event, with the final sign-off on every affected lot waiting on Certificates of Verification that did not exist yet.

Understand what happened here, because the structure of it matters more than the ducts. California law recognizes two different houses: there is the paper house, the one that exists inside the compliance model, which beat the energy budget by three percent and earned a certificate, and there is the real house, the one with actual ducts in an actual attic, which failed. California's code is written to be skeptical of the paper house, and the only question that matters is who pays for the skepticism.

Four Programs, Zero Signatures

For a single-family home, the California Energy Commission has approved exactly four computer programs that are allowed to say your design complies: the public-domain CBECC-Res, and the third-party EnergyPro 9.4, IES VE, and Right-Energy, each approved under the Alternative Calculation Method provisions of Public Resources Code section 25402.1(a). Permits issued from January 2023 through December 2025 fell under the 2022 code, while the 2025 standards apply to new applications now.

Read what the Commission actually approves, though, because it approves the calculation method, not the result. A docket approving EnergyPro 9.4 confirms the software's math matches the Commission's compliance managers. It does not promise the model's prediction will resemble your house. That distinction is not fine print; it is the entire legal architecture. A human being signs the Certificate of Compliance, the CF1R: the documentation author, whose name and license sit on the form. Nothing in the file carries the software vendor's signature, which means nobody can sue the vendor for a house that leaks.

Then comes the backstop: field verification and diagnostic testing, which the Commission recently moved out of the old Title 20 HERS program and into the administrative section of Title 24 as the Energy Code Compliance program, and which requires that installed measures be tested by certified raters. If a test fails its threshold, the contractor fixes the feature and retests until it passes. No passing test, no registered Certificate of Verification, no final sign-off, because your enforcement agency does not care what your model said; it cares what the manometer said.

Enter the Fast Models

None of the AI-powered modeling tools reshaping early design are on that approved list, and that is precisely the wrinkle. Tools like cove.tool, which the Department of Energy has documented bridging reduced-order AI modeling to detailed EnergyPlus compliance runs, and Ekotrope, which has been piloting HERS-adjacent modeling tools for builders, and the ASHRAE-140-based load calculators now circulating among designers, have made energy modeling fast, cheap, and iterative. A designer can run a dozen parametric variations before lunch.

Speed is wonderful for design and a liability vector for compliance, not because an AI model runs the official compliance file, but because of the quiet migration of inputs: a reduced-order model that assumed ducts in conditioned space, or a fan efficacy the plans never specified, or window performance values from a product line the purchasing department already value-engineered away, gets carried, sometimes copied, straight into the compliance run, so that every optimistic assumption the early model was allowed to make now lives inside a file that is not allowed to be optimistic.

And the person who discovers the optimism is the rater with the duct blaster, standing in lot 14, six months after the certificate was signed.

The Gap Is Measured. It Is Large.

How far apart can the paper house and the real house be? A Pacific Northwest National Laboratory field study of recently constructed buildings across five states and nineteen enforcement jurisdictions, cited in the 2025 Title 24 code-change record, found a 60 percent compliance rate for HVAC controls measures, and observed that installed system capabilities consistently exceeded the as-configured condition. The equipment could do the job; nobody had set it up to do it.

Residential performance-gap research points the same direction: a Ryerson University study of LEED for Homes certified houses found actual consumption running 23 to 77 percent above design-stage predictions; only after researchers revised the models to match as-built conditions did the gap narrow to 9 to 40 percent. Sanders and colleagues put the best estimate of the gap between technical potential and in-situ performance of efficiency measures at roughly 50 percent.

Strip away the journals and the failure modes are unglamorous. A California HERS provider's 2025-code job-site checklist reads like a catalog of ways the real house betrays the paper one: standard cloth duct tape, which does not comply and fails the duct leakage test. Duct boots never sealed to the drywall, the single largest source of duct leakage failures; dirty filters depressing airflow; fan speeds left at factory defaults instead of the values in the Manual J/S report. Nobody's model assumes the installer used the wrong tape, but the tape is what the rater finds.

Nobody Has Priced the Failure Cycle Until Now

One failed verification costs, at published California provider rates, $350 to $600 per dwelling for the retest visit alone; call it $475. Fix costs depend on what failed: resealing a leaky duct system runs $800 to $2,500, correcting airflow and refrigerant charge $400 to $1,200, envelope re-sealing $600 to $1,500. A single failed dwelling typically lands between $1,500 and $4,000 in fixes plus the retest.

That is the benign case; where the arithmetic turns predatory is in the group sampling rules. One failure forces re-sampling of a second dwelling in the group. A second failure in a closed group strips verification status from every untested dwelling in it, each of which must then be individually tested, fixed, and re-registered. Add one to three weeks of schedule drag per affected lot while final sign-off waits on the CF3R, at carrying costs this site has previously estimated near $1,066 per month for a custom home, and the per-dwelling total for a cascaded failure reaches $2,500 to $9,000. On a 40-lot phase tested in groups of five, one bad duct system in the wrong group can put eight homes through the retest mill.

All of it lands on the builder, since consultant scope letters rarely allocate retest costs and the software license disclaims them outright. What remains is the CF1R signature, yours or your consultant's, and that signature owns the bill.

An Objection, at Full Strength

Stated at full strength, the way a good building official states it: this system is working as designed. Title 24's two-step structure, approved model at design and field verification at construction, exists precisely because models are imperfect, and the backstop caught the failure: nobody moved into a non-compliant home, which is the outcome the code exists to produce. Liability lands on the builder because the builder controls the as-built condition, and the code regulates the as-built condition, so concentrating responsibility on the party with the screwdriver is a feature, not a bug. AI-assisted early modeling means more energy analysis per project than ever before, and the field test is gloriously indifferent to which software produced the paper. If the complaint is that verification costs money, the answer is that unverified compliance costs more, and the buyer pays it in utility bills for thirty years.

That objection is mostly correct, which is exactly why the actionable part of this article is not "abolish field verification" but "price it, contract it, and preempt it."

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

No California-specific study publishes the actual failure rate of Title 24 field verification for single-family homes, so the PNNL 60 percent figure is used here as the closest available proxy, not as a California residential statistic; it measured nonresidential HVAC controls. Ryerson's 23-to-77 percent performance gap comes from Ontario LEED homes modeled in HOT2000, which demonstrates the direction and magnitude of the model-to-reality gap rather than a defect rate in CEC-approved software. Retest and fix cost ranges are assembled from publicly posted provider rate sheets and contractor estimates, not from a survey of California builders, and real costs vary by market and rater. And to be explicit: this article does not claim the AI modeling tools named here are defective or that they hold CEC compliance approval; the claim is narrower and, I would argue, more uncomfortable, which is that faster early modeling multiplies the number of optimistic inputs available to migrate into a compliance file, and nothing in the approval docket for any of the four approved programs protects you from your own inputs.

Three Things Before Your Next CF1R

If you are a builder or developer: first, read the CF1R before it is registered, and check four inputs against your actual spec: window U-factor and SHGC; duct location, because ducts modeled in conditioned space and installed in a vented attic are two different compliance outcomes; fan efficacy; and the HVAC model numbers matched to their AHRI certificates. A model is only as honest as its inputs, and you are about to sign them.

Second, put the retest money in the contract. Your energy consultant's scope letter should state who pays for re-verification visits and corrective re-modeling if field testing fails, and the consultant should carry professional liability insurance that names the exposure. If the consultant will not accept any allocation of verification risk, that refusal is information, and you should price the engagement accordingly.

Third, walk the checklist before the rater walks the house. Pre-drywall: boots sealed to drywall with mastic, no cloth duct tape on any joint, fan speeds set to the Manual J/S report values, filters new or removed before airflow testing. A $200 pre-inspection by someone who has read the 2025-code checklist beats a $4,000 failure cycle every time, and it beats it on schedule too, because the failure cycle's real cost is the three weeks your closings wait on a certificate.

If you are buying the home rather than building it: understand that "Title 24 compliant" on the plan set is a statement about the paper house. Ask for the registered Certificate of Verification for your specific lot. If the builder cannot produce it, your house has not been verified yet, whatever the marketing sheet says. This code was written to be skeptical of the paper. You should be too.
