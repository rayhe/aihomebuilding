Your EV Is Nine Powerwalls on Wheels. Wiring It to Your House Costs $8,000 After the Drywall Goes Up.

By Priya Greenwood · September 24, 2026
← Back to articles

One windy October evening, just after seven, the power went out across a hillside neighborhood east of Pasadena, one of those public-safety shutoffs Southern California now treats like weather. My neighbor rolled his Cybertruck into the driveway, connected a thick cable to a box on the side of his house, and had his refrigerator, his lights, and his Wi-Fi back within the hour. Two doors down, another family sat in the dark with a dead phone and a warming freezer, waiting on a $15,000 whole-home generator they had priced but never bought.

Between those two houses stood one decision, made months earlier: whether the house was wired to accept power from the car. That is what this article is about, and if you are building, remodeling, or buying an EV in the next few years, it may be the highest-value electrical decision you make.

Nine Powerwalls, parked in the driveway

Start with the comparison your utility bill already taught you to make. A Tesla Powerwall 3 stores 13.5 kilowatt-hours and sells installed for roughly $9,000 to $18,000, depending on your market and your panel. A Cybertruck carries a 123 kWh battery pack, so dividing one by the other gives you nine Powerwalls on wheels, a battery you already paid for when you bought the truck. This month Tesla confirmed that Powershare can now back up a home alongside a Powerwall 3, stretching the combined system past three days of runtime.

Bidirectional output is no longer a concept vehicle promise. Ford’s F-150 Lightning pushes up to 9.6 kW back to the house, while the Cybertruck delivers up to 11.5 kW, enough for everything except the heaviest loads running at once. GM’s V2H bundle, pairing its PowerShift charger with a home hub, inverter, and a dark-start battery, lists at $7,198 on promotion before installation. You already own the battery. In effect, the car industry has started selling home batteries with wheels attached.

The study that changed the math

Researchers at the University of Michigan asked a harder question than backup power: what happens to your electricity bill if the car also arbitrages it? Simulating 6,480 vehicles across 432 American regions with NREL’s Cambium and ResStock datasets, they found that vehicle-to-home charging cuts lifetime charging costs by 40 to 90 percent, with the median household saving $3,800 in electricity costs over the vehicle’s lifetime and cutting greenhouse-gas emissions from household electricity use by 70 to 250 percent, a median of 38 metric tons of CO2 equivalent. In regions covering 60 percent of the American population, the reductions more than fully offset the emissions from charging.

That is a remarkable headline number, and it deserves the caveat the authors themselves print: the model assumes adding V2H capability to the home costs nothing. Remember, the $3,800 is the most a household can save, not a promise. So let’s price the actual equipment, because the gap between the model and the invoice is where this decision lives.

What it actually costs, line by line

Hardware breaks into three buckets: the bidirectional charger itself runs $3,000 to $6,500, an automatic transfer switch or critical-loads subpanel adds $800 to $2,000, and electrician labor and permits add another $1,000 to $2,500. All in, before any panel upgrade, the retrofit lands between $4,800 and $11,000. Need a bigger panel and add $2,000 to $4,500. That is the retrofit tax.

Set those numbers against the alternatives: a Powerwall 3 installed runs $9,000 to $18,000, and a standby generator $10,000 to $25,000 plus fuel and maintenance. On paper, V2H is the cheapest backup option for anyone who already owns a compatible EV, though the paper tells only part of the story. In practice, one Cybertruck owner was recently quoted over $30,000 for the Powershare installation while others reported quotes under $5,000. That spread does not reflect different equipment; it reflects an immature contractor market where some installers have never done one of these and price the risk into the bid.

Two more facts for your spreadsheet: the federal 30C residential charging credit’s window closed in mid-2026, so do not count on a credit that no longer exists. California’s utilities are moving toward dynamic hourly electricity rates, which the CPUC requires statewide by the end of 2027, exactly the rate structure that makes V2H arbitrage most valuable.

What this means for your buildIf you own a bidirectional-capable EV and you are on a time-of-use rate, V2H is the cheapest backup power you can buy, but only if the installation stays near the low end of the range, and timing is the single biggest lever on that price.

The other side

Now the honest objections, because a $3,800 headline minus an $8,000 install is a payback period measured in decades. Those Michigan savings assume costless installation, so on pure bill arbitrage alone, a retrofit at the high end of the range may never pay for itself. Outage protection is where the value actually sits, and that value is real but lumpy: it depends on how often your utility shuts you off and how much you would pay to avoid it. Price the outages, not the arbitrage.

Battery degradation is the second objection, and it is legitimate. A DOE-funded study at NREL cycled two 16 kWh packs around the clock to isolate the extra wear from bidirectional use, confirming that depth of discharge, state of charge, temperature, and total energy throughput all drive degradation. Most EV warranties were written before V2H existed, so read the warranty before you cycle the most expensive component you own. Keeping discharge shallow and avoiding the extremes of the charge range is what the battery science recommends.

Third, the car has to be home and plugged in when the outage hits; a commuter whose truck sits in an office lot during a daytime safety shutoff gets zero backup. Fourth, vehicle-to-home is not vehicle-to-grid: pure V2H, where the car only feeds your house during an outage, is generally permitted like a backup generator. Should power ever flow back to the utility line, you need an interconnection agreement, an approved inverter on the utility’s equipment list, and sometimes an export meter, with approval timelines measured in weeks or months. Salespeople promising grid income should be handing you the utility program documents in writing.

The decision is a construction decision

Which brings us to the part that matters for this publication. Almost every dollar of that $4,800-to-$11,000 retrofit is labor, conduit, subpanels, and transfer switches: work that costs a few hundred dollars when it happens during rough-in and thousands when it happens after the drywall goes up. Running a 2-inch conduit from the garage to the panel, designating a critical-loads subpanel, leaving a mounting location for the gateway hardware, and specifying 200-amp service are line items an electrician prices in the hundreds during construction. Retrofit, they are the line items that push a quote from $5,000 toward $30,000. Conduit is cheap. Regret is not.

That reframes V2H from a product purchase into a construction provision. Even if you do not own a compatible EV today, even if you never buy one, pre-wiring costs less than a nice faucet and it future-proofs the single largest electrical load a house will ever see. Automakers representing most of the American EV market have committed to bidirectional charging.

Houses built in the next decade will either be wired for that world or retrofitted for it at a multiple of the cost, and the grid is heading the same direction. California requires dynamic hourly rates by the end of 2027, and PG&E already holds the nation’s first vehicle-to-grid export compensation rate. Rate structures are being redesigned around exactly the flexibility your car provides.

Ask your electrician one question before the walls close: “What would it cost to pre-wire for a bidirectional EV charger?” If the answer is a few hundred dollars, you have just bought the cheapest backup power, the cheapest peak-rate hedge, and the cheapest emissions cut available to an American homeowner, all for the price of conduit and foresight. A battery is already sitting in your driveway; whether it can keep your lights on is a decision you make with a stud finder in your hand, not a showroom brochure.
