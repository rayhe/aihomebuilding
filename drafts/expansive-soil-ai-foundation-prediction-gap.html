An AI Can Predict Which Soils Will Destroy Your Foundation From Two Data Points. Your Builder Checks a Map From the 1990s.

By Jake Kowalski • July 28, 2026

In Dallas, Texas, 120 companies will repair your cracked foundation, but only fifteen will design one properly in the first place, which means the city that arguably knows more about expansive clay than anywhere else in America has built an entire economy around cleaning up a problem that a $2,700 soil test could have flagged before the first truck rolled onto the lot. Eight wrecking crews for every prevention team. That ratio tells you everything.

According to the American Society of Civil Engineers, expansive soils cause more than $15 billion in annual damage to buildings and infrastructure in the United States, a figure that dwarfs the combined destruction from earthquakes, hurricanes, tornadoes, and floods by a factor of two. One in four American homes has already experienced foundation disturbance from these soils, per ASCE estimates cited in a HUD Cityscape analysis of residential foundations on expansive clays. Unlike a hurricane, which at least has the decency to announce itself on radar and give you forty-eight hours to board the windows, expansive clay sits quietly beneath your slab for years, swelling imperceptibly through wet seasons and shrinking through dry ones, until the doors stop closing and a diagonal crack splits your drywall from the ceiling to the baseboard.

Montmorillonite is the mineral you need to remember if you are buying land anywhere from central Texas to the Front Range of Colorado, because it can swell to fifteen times its dry volume when saturated, generating pressures exceeding 30,000 pounds per square foot according to the Virginia Department of Energy's geology division. A concrete slab-on-grade, by comparison, exerts about 150 PSF on the ground beneath it. Your foundation is not fighting the soil. It is being crushed by it, slowly and invisibly, while you argue with your spouse about whether that crack was always there.

A Quarter-Million New Bets Every Year

More than 250,000 new homes are built on expansive soils each year in the United States, according to the Colorado Geological Survey, and while sixty percent will sustain only minor damage during their useful lives, a full ten percent will experience significant structural failure that in some cases proves beyond economic repair. Those are not great odds for a purchase that will cost most buyers thirty years of mortgage payments and represent the single largest financial commitment of their lives.

USDA estimates that half of all homes in the country sit on expansive soils, which makes this not a regional quirk confined to the red clay subdivisions outside Fort Worth or the bentonite badlands of the Front Range, but a continental-scale hazard hiding in plain sight under suburban cul-de-sacs from the Piedmont of Virginia to the alluvial valleys of Southern California.

By the numbers: A geotechnical report for a residential lot costs $1,000 to $5,000, with a national average of $2,700 (HomeAdvisor, HomeGuide, Angi, 2025-2026 data). Foundation repair after failure costs $5,000 to $15,000+. Simple math: the test-to-fix ratio runs at least 5.5 to 1 in favor of testing. Most builders skip it unless the local code forces their hand.

Why do they skip it? Because most jurisdictions do not require a geotechnical report for single-family residential construction unless the project involves a hillside, a known hazard zone, or a structural engineer's explicit demand, which means flat suburban lots get a pass even when the clay beneath them has a plasticity index that would make a geotechnical engineer wince. A builder pours a post-tensioned slab, crosses fingers, and moves on to framing.

Two Numbers, 94% Accuracy

Meanwhile, in the research literature, a remarkably simple machine learning model has been quietly solving this problem with an elegance that makes the residential construction industry's continued ignorance of it genuinely difficult to explain. A 2025 study published in MDPI Buildings demonstrated a domain-guided random forest classifier that predicts expansive soil swell potential from just two inputs: plasticity index and clay fraction, both obtainable from a basic Atterberg limits test that costs $150 to $350 and returns results in days, not weeks.

Validated through stratified hold-out testing, k-fold cross-validation, and bootstrap uncertainty analysis, the model's predictions align with established geotechnical principles as confirmed through SHAP (Shapley Additive Explanations) and partial dependence plots, and its computational footprint is small enough to run on a smartphone, making it suitable for integration into BIM workflows, GIS-based site screening tools, or even a permit clerk's desktop application that flags lots before a foundation pour is ever scheduled.

A separate 2025 study in Scientific Reports went further, building a GrowNet hybrid neural network trained on a harmonized global dataset of 400 soil samples that predicts the residual friction angle of clay soils with an R-squared of 0.94, outperforming every traditional empirical correlation and prior ML approach tested against the same data. Explainable AI techniques confirmed that clay fraction and plasticity index dominated the predictions, consistent with decades of geotechnical field experience that the algorithm had never been shown.

Put plainly: the science is not waiting for a breakthrough, because the breakthrough already arrived, and it needs exactly two soil properties, a laptop, and a trained model to flag a dangerous lot before a single truck rolls onto the site.

Free Data, Zero Adoption

You might assume the bottleneck is data, but it is not, because USDA's SSURGO database, the Soil Survey Geographic Database maintained by the Natural Resources Conservation Service, provides free, nationwide soil data including shrink-swell ratings, clay content percentages, and Atterberg limits for virtually every mapped soil unit in the country. Anyone can access it through the NRCS Web Soil Survey by drawing a boundary around a lot and pulling the report in minutes.

Nobody does, not the builder trying to close four lots this quarter, not the buyer who spent six hours researching school districts but zero minutes checking what kind of clay sits fourteen inches below the basement slab, and not the municipality issuing the permit that authorizes someone to pour $400,000 worth of house on top of soil that has been heaving and settling since the Cretaceous.

Combining SSURGO data with a trained swell-prediction model would create an automated risk screening layer that could flag every building permit application in a high-risk soil zone at essentially zero marginal cost, requiring no new hardware, no new sensors, and no new legislation, just a software layer connecting a free federal database to a published, validated algorithm that already exists and has already been peer-reviewed.

Instead, we wait for cracks, sticking doors, tilting floors, and then we call one of those 120 companies in Dallas.

Climate Is Making the Math Worse

Expansive soil damage correlates tightly with moisture cycling, because long droughts dry the clay and shrink it while heavy rains saturate it and cause dramatic rebound, and Colorado's geological survey notes that arid and semi-arid regions with seasonal moisture swings experience the highest frequency of expansion problems precisely because those regions subject the same clay to the widest range of moisture conditions over the shortest time intervals.

Climate models project exactly this pattern intensifying across the American West and Southwest: longer dry periods punctuated by more intense precipitation events, which is the worst possible combination for montmorillonite clay that has been sitting under a slab for fifteen years at equilibrium moisture content. A HUD-funded research program titled "Residential House Foundations on Expansive Soils in Changing Climates" is developing updated computational modules to predict how these shifting moisture boundaries will affect foundation performance, but the research targets design standards that most residential builders do not currently follow and may not even know exist.

Building a home in 2026 on a lot with high-plasticity clay using a foundation designed for 1990s moisture patterns is not conservative engineering; it is underwriting a loss that climate change is making larger with every passing decade.

What Would Actually Fix This

Three interventions would close the gap between what the technology can do and what residential construction actually does, and none of them require new legislation, new hardware, or new funding streams.

First, municipalities could integrate SSURGO soil data into their permitting systems so that any lot with a shrink-swell rating above "moderate" automatically triggers a mandatory geotechnical report before a foundation permit is issued, at a cost to the jurisdiction of one GIS overlay and a conditional rule in the permitting software, which amounts to a few thousand dollars protecting millions in future claims and insurance payouts.

Second, builders working in high-risk soil zones could adopt the published ML models as a rapid screening tool, running the two-feature swell classifier against SSURGO data for every lot in a subdivision before pouring a single footer, flagging the lots that need enhanced foundation design while clearing the ones that do not, at a per-lot cost that rounds to zero and a time savings measured in weeks of avoided full-site borings on low-risk parcels.

Third, homebuyers could check the Web Soil Survey themselves, pulling the shrink-swell report for any lot in America in five minutes and demanding a geotechnical investigation before closing if it says "high," because a $2,700 report is a rounding error on a $400,000 mortgage and skipping it is not saving money but deferring a larger expense with compounding interest in the form of structural damage that reduces resale value by far more than the test ever cost.

Limitations

SSURGO data resolution varies, and in some areas the mapped soil units span hundreds of acres and may not capture hyper-local variation within a single lot, which means a screening flag from the database is not a substitute for site-specific borings when the risk is elevated and the stakes justify the cost of a full investigation. ML models trained on global datasets may also perform differently in regions with unusual mineralogy or groundwater conditions not well represented in the training data, a limitation the researchers themselves acknowledge.

And the two-feature swell classifier, while remarkably effective for a model of its simplicity, still requires someone to collect a soil sample and run a plasticity test, which carries a lab fee of $150 to $350 and is not zero, but is an order of magnitude cheaper than a full geotechnical investigation and two orders of magnitude cheaper than the repair bill it can prevent.

Strongest Counterargument

Foundation engineers will argue that swell prediction alone is insufficient for foundation design, and they are correct, because a proper geotechnical report includes bearing capacity analysis, settlement estimates, groundwater assessment, and site-specific recommendations for slab type, reinforcement, and drainage that no ML classifier can replace.

But that argument misses the point entirely, because most residential lots in expansive soil zones never receive any analysis at all, and a swell classifier does not replace a geotechnical engineer any more than a smoke detector replaces a fire department. Moving from zero information to a calibrated risk flag is not marginal improvement. It is the difference between building blind and building with a flashlight, and right now a quarter of American homes are showing the cracks to prove that building blind is exactly what we have been doing.
