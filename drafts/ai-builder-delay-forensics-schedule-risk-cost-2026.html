Your Builder Promised 8 Months. The Data Says Plan for 13. The Slip Alone Costs $20,000.

By Frank DeLuca · September 27, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Slab day was a Tuesday in March. Their builder shook the owner's hand and said eight months, tops, weather permitting, with the easy confidence of a man who has never once had to pay for his own optimism. By November the roof was on and the windows were in, which is the part of a project where everybody relaxes, because the hard stuff looks done. Then the drywall crew no-showed for three weeks, and cabinet delivery slipped twice, and somewhere inside that lost month the owner's rate lock started its quiet countdown to expiration. Their tile guy, who had been "two weeks out" since August, turned out to be one guy with a van and a waiting list. Move-in happened in June of the following year. Fifteen months, and nobody stole anything or sued anybody; the schedule simply dissolved, one optimistic assumption at a time.

I have watched this movie for twenty years. Your builder is not lying, exactly. He is telling you the schedule for the project he intends to build, staffed by the crews he hopes to get, supplied by vendors he has not called yet, which is why the document in your hand is less a plan than a composite of everyone else's best intentions, assembled on a day when the sun was out and the subs were answering their phones. What he hands you is not a forecast. It is a wish with a Gantt chart attached.

The Census Has Receipts

Every year the Census Bureau's Survey of Construction measures how long single-family homes actually take, and every year the numbers embarrass the handshakes. For homes completed in 2024, the average ran 9.1 months from start to finish: 1.4 months from authorization to breaking ground, 7.6 months of actual construction. That average blends production builders cranking out their 26th copy of the same floor plan with genuine custom work. Split it by who is building, and the picture sharpens. Homes built for sale finished in 7.6 months, while homes built by hired contractors, which is the custom-home category, took about 12 months.

Newer data, for 2025 completions, puts the overall average at 8.8 months, down from the 2023 pandemic-era peak of 10.1. Progress, sure. Geography matters too: New England averaged 13.5 months and the Pacific division, our backyard, averaged 10.3, which means a Bay Area buyer signing an eight-month contract is betting against a regional average that has never once, in the survey's history, been beaten by a custom project of any real complexity. And the distribution has a long tail: roughly 12% of homes took 13 months or more from start to completion.

12 months Average start-to-finish time for a contractor-built custom home in 2024, per the Census Survey of Construction. The builder's "eight months, tops" was optimistic by half.

So when your builder promises eight months on a custom home, he is promising to beat the national average for his category by a third, a feat some builders manage and most do not. Can you tell which kind you hired before the drywall crew ghosts you?

What the AI Tools Actually Do

Two different kinds of AI now attack the schedule problem, and it is worth understanding that they attack different ends of it.

nPlan, a London company founded in 2017, works the forensics end. It trained machine-learning models on a dataset of more than 750,000 past project schedules, representing roughly $2 trillion in capital expenditure across 10 countries. Feed it your schedule and it forecasts, activity by activity, where the plan is likely to break, drawing on three-quarters of a million prior projects the way a veteran superintendent draws on twenty years of watching slabs get poured in March. Its signature demo is Crossrail, the London rail megaproject that finished five years late and billions over budget: nPlan ran its model on schedules available up to 2012 and produced a completion forecast that matched what actually happened, years before the human experts converged on the same answer. That got the attention of the people running the Transpennine Route Upgrade, where AI schedule assurance is now written into the contracts.

ALICE Technologies works the planning end. Founded out of a Stanford PhD on AI in construction, its platform ingests a 3D model plus crew counts, production rates, and constraints, then simulates millions of sequencing permutations to find the fastest buildable order of operations. ALICE claims users see roughly 17% shorter durations and 14% lower labor costs, numbers that sound impressive until you remember they were measured on industrial projects with dedicated schedulers, not on a six-man residential crew working off a whiteboard. Heavy civil contractor Zachry Construction adopted the platform to simulate staging scenarios it could never have modeled by hand. nPlan tells you your schedule is fiction. ALICE tries to write you a better one.

For a homeowner, nPlan's pitch is the more interesting of the two. Before you sign, somebody runs the builder's promised schedule through a model trained on three-quarters of a million real projects, and the model says: your framer's duration assumptions have failed on 60% of comparable projects. That is a conversation worth having in March, not a lawsuit worth filing in June of next year.

The Three-Month Slip, Priced

Tools are nice. Money is better. So let us do the math nobody does at the kitchen table when the contract gets signed: what does a three-month slip actually cost on a typical custom build?

Take a $750,000 custom home, where our buyer carries a $600,000 takeout mortgage and a $500,000 construction loan, with roughly $380,000 drawn on average at an interest-only 8.5%. While the house is late, the family rents at $3,200 a month. Meanwhile the rate lock on the takeout mortgage expires. Here is the ledger. Read it twice.

Lock-extension pricing: Bankrate puts extension fees at 0.25 to 1% of the loan principal. At the top of that range the lock line alone becomes $6,000 and the slip totals nearly $25,000. Construction-loan extension fees, per Capital Funding, run 0.25 to 0.50% of the loan balance, and a denied extension can trigger maturity default.

Twenty thousand dollars: that is not a rounding error on a $750,000 project, That is a kitchen upgrade, or a year of property taxes, evaporating because the tile guy had a waiting list, and it is worth pausing on the fact that nobody in this story did anything wrong: the tile guy is good at his job, which is exactly why he has a waiting list, and your schedule was built as though he did not. Scale it to the nPlan-adjacent reality, where half of comparable large projects run five or more months late, and a five-month slip on these same assumptions costs roughly $33,000, which is more than most buyers set aside for their entire contingency, and the contingency was supposed to cover the surprises inside the house, not the calendar. In a market where rent is $4,500 instead of $3,200, the slip costs scale with it, and so does everything else: the loan interest, the storage bill, the slow erosion of the contingency you were counting on for the actual house.

Now read that number again and ask yourself whether the builder's eight-month promise deserved the scrutiny you gave it. Most buyers interrogate the cabinet allowance harder than the schedule. Cabinets are in the contract, and so is the schedule, but nobody prices its failure.

What the Tools Cannot Do Yet

Here is where I put the brakes on my own story, because the honest version matters more than the exciting one.

First, the datasets are not your project. nPlan's 750,000 schedules skew toward infrastructure, energy, and transit megaprojects. Its public case studies are Crossrail and rail upgrades, not a 2,800-square-foot farmhouse in Boise. Delay patterns on a $15 billion tunnel program rhyme with the delay patterns of a custom home, they do not replicate them. ALICE's 17% duration improvement is a vendor-reported average across industrial work. No published case study shows it shaving weeks off a single-family residential build, which is the gap between a tool that works in a conference room in Houston and a tool that works on a muddy lot in February.

Second, and more important: these tools eat schedule files. Feed them nothing, get nothing. Real ones, in Primavera P6 or MS Project format, with task logic and resource loading. Your custom-home builder's "schedule" is a spreadsheet, a whiteboard photo, or a confidently delivered verbal timeline, and no machine-learning model ever built can extract a delay forecast from a man pointing at a calendar and saying trust me. You cannot run delay forensics on a document that does not exist. Residential GCs who most need schedule discipline are the least equipped to feed these systems, and the vendors price for commercial contractors, not the four-crew builder doing six customs a year.

Third, the human layer does not model. nPlan can flag that framing durations fail 60% of the time. It cannot flag that your specific framer's lead carpenter is going through a divorce and will be unreliable for the next four months. Schedules dissolve for human reasons as often as structural ones. It sees the pattern, but it does not see the people. People slip.

None of this makes the tools useless; it makes them early for residential, and it means the homeowner's version of schedule forensics is gloriously low-tech: demand a task-level schedule in writing before you sign, call three past clients and ask specifically about on-time delivery, and budget the slip into your contingency in dollars, not hope. Hope is not a line item. If your GC cannot produce a written schedule with task durations, that tells you everything the AI would have told you, faster and for free, because the most sophisticated delay-forecasting system in the world is a builder who has kept every promise he ever made, and the second most sophisticated is a buyer who asks for proof.

What We Did Not Prove

This slip budget is a planning model, not a survey result. Rent, drawn balances, and loan rates vary widely; in high-cost coastal markets every line item runs hotter, in the Midwest cooler. An 8.5% construction-loan rate is typical for 2026 residential construction lending, and interest-only on the drawn balance is standard, but your lender's terms are your lender's terms. nPlan's "50% late by five months" figure comes from a vendor slide deck about capital projects and is cited here as context, not as a residential statistic. Census figures are the solid ground: 12 months for contractor-built homes in 2024, 8.8 months overall in 2025, with the Pacific division at 10.3. We could not verify ALICE's or nPlan's performance claims against any independent audit, and we say so because the vendors' marketing departments will not.

The Ask List

If you are about to build, here is the short version. Get the schedule in writing, at the task level, with durations, before money changes hands. Ask the builder how many of his last ten projects finished on the promised date, and then call the owners of the ones that did not. Price a three-month slip the way we priced it above, with your rent and your loan, and make sure your contingency covers it. Time your rate lock to the realistic completion date, not the promised one; extensions run 0.125 to 0.25% of the loan per week, which adds up fast across a multi-month delay. And if your builder is a commercial-scale GC running real scheduling software, ask whether anyone has run delay forensics on the plan. It exists. It is just waiting for residential construction to produce schedules worth analyzing.

Twenty years of watching slabs get poured in March has taught me one reliable rule: the schedule is the only part of the contract that costs you money when it is wrong and costs the builder nothing. Price it like you mean it.
