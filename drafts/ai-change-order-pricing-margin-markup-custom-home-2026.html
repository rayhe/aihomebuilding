Your Builder Bid at 10% Margin. Your Change Orders Billed at 25%.

By Frank DeLuca • October 5, 2026

It starts with six inches.

You are standing in the framed great room on a Tuesday afternoon, sunlight coming through the studs in stripes, and your spouse says the island feels tight against the range wall. The framer is right there, and so is your builder, tape measure already in hand. Somebody says the words that have quietly sunk more household budgets than lumber prices ever did: while you are at it, can we push that wall six inches?

The builder nods, the framer pulls his tape, and nobody writes anything down, which tells you everything about how the next invoice will read.

That was a financial event, though nobody in the room treated it like one.

I have spent twenty years watching fixed-price contracts move, and they never move at the kitchen table where the contract got signed. They move on Tuesdays, in framed rooms, in conversations that feel too small to document. By the time the paperwork catches up, the price has already changed, and the person who changed it had no competition.

Two margins, one contract

Here is the structure of the business you hired: your builder won your job by bidding against other builders, and competitive bidding does what it always does, which is compress the margin until the profit barely covers the risk.

On a $750,000 fixed-price custom home, the base contract might carry something like 10 percent, or $75,000 of gross profit, and that number had to survive a beauty contest in which every bidder sharpened a pencil to the nub.

Change orders face no beauty contest, because once the foundation is poured, you cannot rebid a wall move to three hungry framers. It gets priced sole-source instead, which carries a different margin entirely, set by one party with no rival bid to answer to. One production builder told Pro Builder, on the record, what every foreman already knows: "If anyone comes back and asks us for something after the budget is done, we know that because it was added as a change order, any dollars that (were) added after that initial budget, we made a nice margin on."

Run the arithmetic on that $750,000 house. Suppose change orders total a conservative 12 percent of the contract, or $90,000, and price them at 25 percent margin instead of 10: they contribute $22,500 of profit against the $75,000 from the base bid, which is where the arithmetic starts to explain the behavior. Twelve percent of the revenue, twenty-three percent of the gross profit, a ratio that should make any homeowner sit up. Read it again. Push change volume to 20 percent, a figure one Phoenix remodeler cites as the low end of the industry range for remodeling work, and the changes become roughly a sixth of revenue carrying a third of the profit.

The two-margin model, with every assumption labeled rather than buried in a footnote. Base contract $750,000 at 10% margin = $75,000 profit (illustrative; research confirms competitive bidding compresses margins, but no public dataset fixes the number). Change orders $90,000 (12% of contract) at 25% margin = $22,500 profit (modeled from builder statements and markup research, not measured). Change-order share of revenue: 10.7%, and share of gross profit: 23.1%, which is the entire argument in two numbers. At 20% change volume ($150,000): 16.7% of revenue, 33.3% of profit.

Those margin figures are modeled, not measured, and I will say plainly what I do not know, which is that no public dataset of residential change-order margins exists; but the structure is what matters here, because incentives do not need precise calibration to shape behavior. Competition disciplines the base bid. Nothing disciplines the change order except your willingness to sign. Sign carefully.

Where the margin hides

If you ever do get an itemized change order, and you should demand one every time, read past the markup line. A six-project academic survey of change-order pricing found the stated markups almost modest: 5.4 percent on the contractor's own work, 6.2 percent on subcontracted work. A homeowner reading those lines would conclude the builder barely cleared expenses, until they read the labor burden.

That same study found actual labor burden averaging 29 percent of base wages, and in four of the six projects it exceeded what the contract allowed. Workers comp, unemployment insurance, payroll taxes, the whole stack, loaded onto every change-order hour at rates the base bid never carried. The markup line is the number the builder shows you, while the burden line is where the profit actually lives, compounded across every change-order hour on the job. Small sample, not American residential work, but every estimator I have ever met recognizes the shape of it immediately.

How allowances manufacture change orders

Most change orders are not even discretionary. They are manufactured months earlier, in the allowances.

An allowance is a placeholder budget for selections you have not made yet, covering appliances, lighting, tile, and plumbing fixtures, and every one of those placeholders was priced by someone who needed the proposal to look affordable. One contract advisor puts it bluntly: a $12,000 appliance allowance against $22,000 taste becomes a $10,000 change order the day you pick the range, "often with added markup because the builder manages procurement and warranty." A Utah custom builder describes $85,000 lighting allowances on 7,000-square-foot homes as placeholders "designed to keep a proposal looking competitive." Tile, plumbing fixtures, millwork, appliances, windows: the usual suspects, every one of them.

Do the compounding across ten allowance lines, each underfunded by 40 percent on $120,000 of total allowances, and you get $48,000 in allowance-driven change orders, or 6.4 percent of a $750,000 contract, before you change a single thing voluntarily. The budget did not drift on its own; it was built to drift, one optimistic placeholder at a time, because the low proposal wins the job and the true-up always arrives at the moment you can least afford to walk away. That timing is not accidental.

An Arizona custom builder states the fix plainly: convert allowances to fixed, priced line items before signing, because "a contract with no allowances is a contract where the price can only move through change orders you personally sign." It costs the builder time and costs you two weeks of decisions. It is the cheapest insurance in the transaction.

What the software does now

Software vendors finally noticed the paperwork problem, and their products have arrived in layers, each one promising to drag the change order out of the group text and into something auditable. Buildertrend and CoConstruct, the two big residential management platforms, both run change-order approval workflows: the price gets written, the client taps approve on a phone, the budget updates. A newer AI-native entrant, KonstructIQ, folds estimating, markup calculation, and change-order management into one system where an approved estimate becomes the budget and every change order reprices it automatically. AI takeoff tools and estimating services reprice changed scope from updated drawings in hours instead of days, and run what-if comparisons when you are choosing between two options.

This is genuine progress over the group-text era. A written price with a timestamp beats a nod in a framed room, and anything that forces the number into existence before the work starts is on your side, which is why the approval workflow matters more than the algorithm behind it.

What the software cannot do

But listen to what one builder-technologist wrote this summer about the new AI models: "If your change order workflow lives in your head and three group texts, a smarter model has nothing to grab." The model prices from data, which means if the data is a handshake, the model is guessing, confidently and to three decimal places. Garbage in, gospel out.

That pricing data has its own problems, starting with staleness. AI estimating leans on historical costs and published references like RSMeans, which lag what your actual subs charge this month in your market. No model knows your electrician walked off last week and the replacement charges time-and-a-half. Nobody publishes an accuracy dataset for AI change-order pricing, so every vendor claim about precision is unverified by design. Arithmetic was never the most dangerous failure on a jobsite. It is the sentence "we will price it later," which one Utah builder calls the four most dangerous words in construction, because ten unpriced changes and you have lost control of the number. No software fixes a builder who will not write the number down; it only files the absence faster, with better search. The tool is not the process.

In defense of the change order

Fairness requires the other side of this, stated at full strength, because most change orders start with the client, not the builder. Peer-reviewed research ranks owner-driven scope changes as the second most significant cause of change orders, behind only recycled paperwork from previous projects. You asked for the wall to move, after all; those six inches were your idea, and the invoice will remind you.

Mid-project changes also genuinely cost more than base-bid work, for reasons that survive scrutiny: remobilizing a trade, resequencing work already scheduled, ordering materials in small quantities, the administrative drag of revised drawings and purchase orders, none of which appear in the base bid because the base bid assumed none of it would happen. The base bid really is thin, and research on contractor cost modeling confirms that competitive bidding compresses margins, which means change-order margin partly offsets a base bid that was priced to win rather than to profit. Some builders lose money on changes they manage sloppily. None of which proves extraction. It proves the incentive exists, and understanding the incentive is more useful than assuming the motive.

Five moves, in contract order

First, zero out the allowances before you sign. Convert every allowance to a fixed, priced line item with tax, freight, and waste included, and if you cannot decide in time, raise the allowance to a number built from comparable finished homes rather than from the proposal's optimism. Two weeks of decisions now beats $48,000 of true-ups later.

Second, cap the markup in writing. Change-order markup is negotiable, and builders expect it negotiated about as much as they expect the headline price negotiated, which is to say not much, which is your opening. Write in maximums: 15 percent on subcontracted work, 10 percent on the builder's own, with labor burden itemized separately instead of buried in the hourly rate.

Third, ban "we will price it later." Require written pricing within 48 hours of the request, and no changed work proceeds without your signature on that price. Verbal approvals are how $4,800 wall moves happen, while paper is how they get questioned first.

Fourth, demand the breakdown every time. Labor hours and burden rate, materials with receipts available, markup shown as its own line. You are not auditing because you distrust your builder. You are auditing because sunlight is cheaper than disputes, and the one change order you question closely disciplines the pricing of the next ten.

Fifth, set a tripwire. When cumulative change orders cross 10 percent of the contract value, work stops for a formal budget review: what drove it, what remains in allowances, what the final number now projects to be. Ten percent is early enough to change course and late enough to mean something.

Limitations of This Analysis

The two-margin model is illustrative. No public dataset measures residential change-order margins, and the 10 percent base and 25 percent change figures are assumptions chosen to show the structure of the incentive, not to certify any builder's books. Those 5.4 and 6.2 percent markup figures come from a six-project survey outside American residential construction; the labor-burden finding is directionally useful, not transferable. That 20 to 32 percent industry range is vendor-cited for remodeling work, and the 2.1 percent counterexample is self-reported by the company publishing it, with definitions that vary across the industry. AI pricing accuracy is unverified across every product mentioned. Nothing here is legal advice; have your contract reviewed by a construction attorney in your state before you sign it, because the clauses above are only as good as their enforceability where your foundation gets poured.

The wall moved six inches; measure what moved with it, and make sure the next one gets measured before it moves.
