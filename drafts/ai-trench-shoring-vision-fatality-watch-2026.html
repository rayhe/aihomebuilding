Three of Every Four Trench Deaths Happen on Residential Jobs. The Fix Rents for $400 a Week.

By Marcus "Steel" Washington · September 17, 2026

In June 2022, two workers were leveling a sewer pipe in Jarrell, Texas, at the bottom of a trench 23 feet deep and 34 inches wide, with vertical walls and no protective system of any kind. It had rained the afternoon before, and water had pooled at the bottom, soaking the soil and loosening it. Their nearest way out, a ladder, was more than 130 feet away. Then the wall came down and buried them both. They died where they worked. OSHA later fined the contractor, WBW Construction, $250,272 for failing to protect the two men, a number that sounds large until you divide it by two and realize what the agency believes a life is worth on paper. That number is the fine. Two families absorbed the actual cost.

2022 was the worst year for trench deaths in recent memory. Thirty-nine workers died in trench collapses that year, the highest count since at least 2017, more than double the year before. OSHA responded the way it does when the body count embarrasses everyone: a national enforcement emphasis program, a declared "zero tolerance" policy for unprotected trenches, immediate inspections whenever an inspector spots one, and criminal referrals where the facts warrant them. According to the Labor Department, the deaths fell to 15 in 2023 and 12 in 2024, a nearly 70 percent decline that the agency credits to enforcement and outreach. Then 2025 arrived with 11 trench fatalities already on the board by July, according to Occupational Health & Safety, and the familiar question came back around: was the decline a trend or a pause?

Now AI vendors are pitching a new answer, cameras that watch the trench and flag the violation before the wall comes down, and it is worth asking at length whether that technology addresses the actual reasons men die in holes, because those reasons are well documented, they have been counted and tabulated by the Bureau of Labor Statistics for decades, and they are not mysterious in the slightest.

The Dead Are Not on Skyscrapers

When people picture a construction death, they picture a tower crane or a scaffold forty stories up, but trench deaths happen somewhere else entirely. Bureau of Labor Statistics data compiled by trench-safety researchers shows that about three of every four trench fatalities occur at residential worksites, not commercial towers. Sewer laterals. Water lines. Foundation footings. This is the ordinary underground work of building houses, performed by the smallest companies in the industry: 42 percent of the employers in fatal trench incidents had fewer than 10 workers, and 71 percent had never been inspected by OSHA at all.

What kills is never the dramatic trench. Seventy-two percent of fatalities happened in trenches less than 9 feet deep. A sewer lateral for a single-family home runs 6 to 8 feet. That depth feels routine, which is exactly why it kills. One cubic yard of soil weighs about as much as a compact car, and a collapsed 7-foot wall can drop several yards onto a worker's chest before he finishes exhaling. Cave-ins cause about three of every four trench deaths; the rest are mostly struck-by incidents and electrocutions from severed utility lines.

Two statistics explain almost everything else: in 86 percent of fatal incidents, the competent person OSHA requires on every excavation was not on site, and in 88 percent of the incidents OSHA investigated, schedule pressure was judged more important to the employer than safety. A disproportionate share, 36 percent, happened on Mondays, because rain or frost over the weekend changed the soil while nobody was watching. Nobody died from an exotic hazard. They were killed by routine work, done too fast, by small crews, with nobody qualified standing at the edge of the hole.

The Rules Already Exist, and They Are Not Complicated

This is the part that makes trench deaths infuriating to people who work in the trade. OSHA's excavation standard, 29 CFR 1926 Subpart P, has required the same basic protections for decades. Any excavation 5 feet or deeper needs a protective system: slope the walls back, shore them with hydraulic supports, or shield the workers with a trench box. Spoil piles and equipment stay at least 2 feet back from the edge. A ladder, stairway, or ramp must sit within 25 feet of lateral travel for anyone working below grade. A competent person, meaning someone who can identify hazards and has the authority to stop work, inspects the excavation daily and every time conditions change.

Doug Parker, then the assistant secretary of labor for OSHA, said it plainly when the 2022 numbers came out: there is no excuse for ignoring requirements this well understood. And the data backs him up. Protective systems were properly employed in only 24 percent of fatal trenches, and in the rest the system was available but unused, or improperly used, or simply never brought to the site at all, which means the equipment that would have saved these men was often sitting on a trailer a hundred yards away while they worked unprotected. Sixty-five percent of the time, the employer had never even identified the soil type, which is the first input to every protective-system decision under the standard, the soil classification that determines whether the walls must be sloped back gently in loose Type C soil, cut steeper in stable Type A clay, or held with hydraulic shoring, and the trench was dug anyway. That Jarrell trench, 23 feet deep with vertical walls in wet soil, violated nearly every line of the standard at once.

So the technology question is narrow and uncomfortable: can a camera enforce rules that already exist, rules written in blood decades ago, on jobsites where the human enforcement mechanism, the competent person, was absent 86 percent of the time anyone bothered to check?

What the Cameras Can See

DroneDeploy's Safety AI, described in detail by MIT Technology Review in 2025, takes a different approach from the older generation of hard-coded computer vision. It uses a vision-language model trained on what the company calls a golden dataset: tens of thousands of images of actual OSHA violations, pulled from years of customer reality-capture imagery with permission, then refined by construction safety professionals who train the model to analyze a scene the way an experienced inspector would, step by step. When it misses a violation or flags a false positive, the human trainers correct it and feed the failure back into the model, a loop the company describes as teaching the system to think the way an experienced inspector thinks rather than merely recognize objects in a frame. DroneDeploy claims the model generalizes to violations it was never explicitly trained on, drawing conclusions about novel scenes from its understanding of how jobsites fail, which matters on residential work where no two trenches look alike and the training data will always be thinner than anyone admits.

EarthCam took the hardware route, running AI-based computer vision on board its construction cameras so the analysis happens at the edge instead of waiting on a server. EarthCam trained its models by having staff annotate real jobsite imagery, boxes around excavators and dumpsters, drawn from its own installed base rather than catalog photos. Sitemetric's newer AI camera goes further into workforce tracking, tying alerts to individual workers through badge identification, which is either accountability or surveillance depending on which side of the badge you stand.

Applied to a trench, the detection categories write themselves. Vertical walls below five feet with no shoring or shield in frame. Workers below grade with no ladder visible within the required distance. Spoil piles heaped at the lip of the excavation. Standing water in the bottom after rain, an excavator swinging over an occupied trench, spoil heaped where the standard says it cannot be, none of this requires exotic AI to recognize, which is both the promise and the indictment, because if a camera can see it, so could the competent person who was supposed to be standing there. It requires a camera pointed at the hole and a model that knows what a violation looks like, and both of those now exist as commercial products.

The Math Is the Strongest Argument Anyone Has Made

Forget the technology for a moment and look at the rental counter. A small stackable aluminum trench box, the kind that breaks down into pieces one person can handle and fits a residential sewer lateral, rents for $100 a day, $400 a week, or $1,200 a month. Larger steel shoring boxes run $150 to $300 a day in 2026 market pricing. A typical residential sewer lateral takes a crew about two weeks. All of which means the shoring for the job costs roughly $800.

Now the other side of the ledger. As of January 2026, OSHA's maximum penalty is $16,550 per serious violation and $165,514 per willful or repeated violation, with failure to abate running $16,550 per day. One serious citation, the kind an inspector writes in twenty minutes with a camera of his own, buys 41 weeks of trench box rental, which means a single citation funds the better part of a year of doing the job right. One willful citation buys 414 weeks. The $250,272 fine in the Jarrell case equals 625 weeks of rental, which is twelve years of trench boxes, spent on a two-week sewer job that rented none.

That arithmetic is the original contribution of this analysis, and it is devastating to the standard excuses. Nobody in residential excavation is skipping shoring because it is expensive. They are skipping it because the crew is small and the foreman is also the operator, because the schedule is tight and the inspector has never visited, because nobody qualified is watching the hole and the Tuesday deadline does not care about soil mechanics. A $400-a-week box loses to a Tuesday deadline every time, right up until it doesn't.

The AI camera enters this math as a witness, not a solution. A camera cannot shore a wall. What it can do is make the violation visible to someone who is not standing at the edge of the hole: the GC's safety director, the owner, the insurer. The question is whether visibility changes behavior on jobsites where 88 percent of fatal incidents already involved schedule pressure beating safety. A camera that records you choosing the deadline over the trench box, day after day, with timestamps, is a camera that records evidence, and the discovery process in the wrongful-death suit will find it.

The Case Against the Camera

The strongest argument against AI trench monitoring has nothing to do with accuracy. It is about who gets watched and who gets protected.

Continuous AI surveillance lands hardest on the smallest crews, which are precisely the crews dying in trenches. A five-person excavation outfit does not have a safety department, a compliance officer, or a lawyer to negotiate the data policy. When the camera flags a worker standing in an unshored trench, the alert goes to the boss, and the boss's response on a tight-margin residential job is as likely to be discipline as protection. Worker advocates have made this point about every generation of jobsite monitoring, from sign-in sheets to GPS-tracked trucks to badge readers, and the pattern never changes: safety data becomes discipline data the moment it is convenient for the person holding the spreadsheet. Sitemetric's badge-linked alerts, which identify the specific worker in each violation, make the tension explicit.

Then there is the reliability problem. Soil conditions change after rain, and environmental factors contributed to 68 percent of trench fatalities. A model trained on dry-afternoon imagery may not read a rain-soaked Monday morning correctly, which is exactly when the risk spikes. False positives breed alert fatigue; the super who gets twelve nuisance alerts a day stops opening the thirteenth, and the thirteenth is the one that matters. False negatives are worse, because they create a paper trail of algorithmic all-clears that a builder's lawyer will wave at a jury.

And here is the legal reality that ends the fantasy of the camera as competent person. OSHA requires a human competent person with the training to identify hazards and the authority to stop work immediately. No regulation, federal or state, recognizes software as a substitute. A builder who leans on the camera instead of the qualified human has not complied with the standard. He has documented his noncompliance in high definition.

There is also the cost barrier nobody in the AI safety business likes to discuss. The trench box rents for $400 a week. The AI camera systems are subscription services, and none of the vendors publish per-site pricing for residential-scale deployments. For the sub-10-employee contractors who account for 42 percent of fatal incidents, a camera subscription that costs more than the shoring itself is a non-starter. The technology will reach the large commercial GCs first, the jobsites with safety departments and compliance officers and lawyers on retainer, which are also the jobsites where trench deaths are rarest, while the residential crews keep digging unmonitored, which is where the bodies are.

What We Did Not Prove

This analysis has blind spots, and they matter. The fatality counts lag reality: the 2024 figure of 12 was partial-year when reported, the 2025 count of 11 runs only through July, and the Bureau of Labor Statistics' final Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries numbers take roughly two years to publish, so the apparent improvement may shrink on revision. The nearly 70 percent decline from 2022 to 2024 coincides with intensified enforcement, industry outreach, and the first deployments of AI monitoring, and this article cannot apportion the credit among them. The residential share of fatalities, about three in four, comes to us through a safety-center compilation of BLS data rather than a directly linked BLS table, and we could not independently re-derive it. We could not verify per-site pricing for DroneDeploy Safety AI, EarthCam's AI safety monitor, or Sitemetric's AI camera, so we treated them as subscription camera services without stated prices rather than inventing numbers. The trench box rental rates are 2026 snapshots from Phoenix, Charlotte, and one Ohio vendor, not a national survey. Finally, the fine-to-rental arithmetic compares maximum statutory penalties to market rental rates; actual OSHA penalties are frequently negotiated down in settlement, which narrows the ratio but does not change its direction.

What This Means on Your Jobsite

If you run a residential excavation crew, the takeaway is blunt. The box costs $400 a week, the serious citation costs $16,550, the willful citation costs $165,514, and the Jarrell fine cost $250,272 and two lives. Rent the box. Designate a competent person in writing, give that person actual stop-work authority, and inspect the trench every morning and after every rain. The Monday-morning spike in the fatality data exists because weekends change soil and nobody re-inspects. That is a procedure problem, and procedures are free, which is what makes the Monday-morning number the most damning statistic in the dataset: nobody died because shoring was unaffordable, they died because nobody walked out to the trench and looked at it after the rain.

If you are a GC or a homebuilder hiring excavation subs, the camera systems are worth evaluating, but evaluate them as documentation and escalation tools, not as safety programs. Ask the vendor what happens when the model is uncertain, who sees the alerts, and whether the system retains footage your insurer and your lawyer will eventually subpoena. Understand that no camera satisfies the competent-person requirement, because the standard demands a human being with the training to identify the hazard and the authority to stop the job on the spot, and you still need that human at the edge of the hole.

If you are a homeowner hiring a contractor who will dig on your property, ask two questions before anyone breaks ground: who is your designated competent person for the excavation, and what protective system are you using below five feet? A contractor who cannot answer both is telling you everything about how your job will be run. The trench that kills is almost never deeper than 9 feet, almost never on a commercial site, and almost never watched by anyone qualified. Your sewer lateral is the most dangerous excavation in American construction. Treat it that way.
