## Kill test
A GC or super running a residential job in a hot state reads this and changes how they onboard new hires next week. An owner-builder in Texas/Arizona learns the heat plan is a legal exposure, not a courtesy. Passes.

## Core thesis
The most dangerous day on a residential construction job is not the hottest day. It is the new hire's first day. OSHA's own case review found that nearly three in four heat fatalities occur during the first week of work, and more than half of those happen on the worker's first day. The prevention is known, cheap, and almost never done: graduated acclimatization schedules, buddy observation, and (newly viable) core-temperature wearables that predict heat illness before the worker feels it. AI enters through predictive core-temp algorithms and automated scheduling around heat-index forecasts.

## Original contribution (novel analysis for this article)
1. **The first-week fatality concentration × residential hiring reality:** Combine OSHA's "3 in 4 deaths in week one, >1 in 2 on day one" with the residential GC reality — high-turnover crews, day labor, temp staffing, summer hiring surges. The deadliest risk factor is not the thermometer, it is the onboarding paperwork. No prior article computed this intersection for residential.
2. **Fine-to-prevention cost arithmetic:** Cal/OSHA's Parkwood willful citation ($276,425) vs. the cost components of a compliant program for a 12-person residential crew — shade structures, coolers/water, written plan, supervisor training time, and a 20% ramp schedule. The 20% rule costs a fraction of one serious-violation penalty ($25,000).
3. **Heat-priority-day exposure calendar:** OSHA's NEP defines heat priority days as heat index ≥80°F. In Sun Belt residential markets, that is effectively the entire building season (April-October) — meaning federal OSHA can now proactively inspect a Texas/Arizona residential site on most workdays, not just after an incident. The enforcement surface area is far larger than most small GCs assume.

## The 20% rule (actionable core)
OSHA's acclimatization guidance: new/returning workers start at no more than 20% of normal workload on day one in heat, increasing ~20% per day. A 5-day ramp: 20/40/60/80/100%. Cost: one week of reduced output per new hire. Alternative: buddy pairing + mandatory water/shade checks on days 1-3.

## Skepticism / counterarguments
- Wearables are surveillance-adjacent: workers (especially immigrant/day-labor crews) may distrust body monitoring; data-privacy design (Kenzen: only the worker sees personal health detail) is not universal.
- Core-temp prediction has error (RMSE 0.30°C) — false alarms erode trust; a beeping vest that cries wolf gets ignored by day three.
- No federal standard exists; General Duty Clause citations are legally vulnerable. Small residential GCs rationally underinvest because inspection probability is low — until it isn't.
- Heat is one hazard among many on residential sites (falls still kill far more). A heat-only program that displaces fall protection spending is a bad trade.
- Acclimatization science: 7-14 days for fit individuals, longer with medical conditions; the 20% rule is guidance, not law, in most states.

## Limitations (for the article's honest accounting)
- BLS heat fatality counts are acknowledged undercounts (misdiagnosis, misclassification); industry suppressed/missing in >1 in 4 cases.
- Wearable accuracy study: n=19 GI-pill trials, n=33 rectal-probe trials — small; lab/controlled conditions, not full-shift framing work in 105°F.
- Fine arithmetic uses California penalty schedule; federal OSHA penalties differ.
- No data on what share of residential GCs actually have written heat plans (OSHA's 20-case review found zero complete programs, but that sample is enforcement-selected).
