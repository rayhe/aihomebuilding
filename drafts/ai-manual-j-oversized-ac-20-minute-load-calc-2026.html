Your AC Is 40% Too Big. The Load Calculation That Would Have Caught It Takes 20 Minutes Now.

By Jake Kowalski · September 6, 2026

Back in the nineties, researchers at the Florida Solar Energy Center did something nobody in the industry wanted them to do. They took 368 recently built homes, ran a proper engineering load calculation on each one, and compared the answer to the air conditioner actually sitting in the yard. More than half of those systems were sized at over 120 percent of the calculated load, and on 78 heat pump homes, nearly a third had heating capacity over 120 percent too. This was not a rounding error. It was the industry standard: guess, add a margin, bump up a size for safety, cash the check.

In the same study, the researchers had earlier surveyed the contractors doing the sizing. Only 33 percent even used the ACCA Manual J load calculation, the engineering procedure that has been the recognized sizing standard for decades and is written into the energy code in most of the country. The other two-thirds were sizing the single most expensive mechanical system in your house by feel. Think about that. Your furnace, your heat pump, the machine that runs every single day of its working life: sized by vibe.

The math nobody bothers to do

Now, honesty before heat. If you have read industry scare copy about oversized equipment doubling your electric bill, you have been sold something. NREL ran the physics in simulation, published as NREL/TP-5500-60801, and found that in hot-humid climates oversizing had "very little impact on overall energy use." Cycling losses are real but small. Modern single-stage equipment has a degradation coefficient around 0.07, and when you plug that into the part-load model for a unit oversized by 40 percent against one sized to the Manual S ceiling of 15 percent over the load, the energy penalty from short cycling works out to under one percent. One percent. I ran the numbers myself because I did not believe it either.

So the oversizing-is-bankrupting-you story is mostly fiction, and anybody who leads with it is either selling you a smaller unit, a utility rebate program, or a retrofit package built on fear rather than physics. What the scare copy hides is that the real penalties are worse in ways a kilowatt-hour meter barely notices. An oversized single-stage unit slams on, blasts cold air, and shuts off before the coil has time to wring moisture out of the air, which means the house sits clammy at 74 degrees and you drop the thermostat to 71 to compensate, and every degree of overcooling costs roughly 3 percent more cooling energy, a penalty that comes from your thermostat, not your compressor. Meanwhile the compressor is short-cycling its way to an early grave, and ENERGY STAR says it plainly: oversized equipment cycles more frequently, makes the home less comfortable, and shortens the life of the equipment. You paid a premium for a bigger box that delivers worse comfort, dies sooner, and tricks you into running it harder. That is the actual scam, and it does not need a fake doubling of the power bill to be outrageous.

The excuse died this year

For decades the industry had a defense, and I will give it its due. A real Manual J is tedious. You measure every wall, window, and door. You pull the actual ASHRAE design temperatures for the ZIP code, not the regional default the software helpfully pre-fills. You need a real infiltration number, real internal gains, a duct gain calculation for ducts baking in a 130-degree vented attic. Done by hand, that is most of a contractor's day. Bill Wright saw the bottleneck back in 1984 and built Wrightsoft, the first software to automate Manual J, and even then, as ACCA noted last year, a proper calc on a 3,000-square-foot house still took a contractor about an hour. An hour of unbillable engineering per bid. You can see why two-thirds of the trade skipped it.

That excuse is now dead, and the thing that killed it reads floor plans, because the AI wave of the last two years turned the tedious part of the job, measuring every wall and window and pulling ZIP-code design temperatures, into a computer vision problem with a coffee-break runtime. HVAKR, covered by AEC Magazine this year, runs an AI layer its own marketing calls a mechanical design agent: feed it the architectural PDF, the same drawings that already arrive at every MEP consultancy, and it models the spaces, assigns zoning, sets envelope properties, and flags the rooms with the highest cooling load per square foot along with what is driving them. Satellite imagery plus generic envelope templates produce rough block loads in minutes. It is not alone. This month's industry roundup lists Energent.ai parsing PDFs and 3D models into room-by-room loads, TruCalc generating reports from scanned plans on a phone, and an iPad app that recalculates loads in the field during verification. Minutes. The pattern across all of them is the same: computer vision eats the drawings, physics-informed models do the thermal math, and the part that used to take an afternoon takes a coffee break.

The argument against everything I just wrote

Now the case for the defense, stated at full strength, because a bad load calculation is not better than no load calculation. Manual J is only as good as its inputs, and a rushed calc built on builder-grade defaults, assumed infiltration, and a guessed duct location can be as wrong as the rule-of-thumb guess it replaced, just wrong with fancier documentation. The people who wrote the FSEC study would recognize the problem: garbage envelope data in, garbage tonnage out. And the technology has quietly reduced the stakes anyway. Inverter-driven, variable-capacity heat pumps modulate their output across a wide range, which means a sizing error that would have doomed a 1990s single-stage unit, condemning a homeowner to a decade of clammy summers, short-cycled compressors, and a utility bill with a permanent limp, is largely absorbed by equipment that simply ramps down and keeps running. If your contractor installed a modern cold-climate heat pump and skipped the calc, you will probably be fine. I am not going to pretend otherwise.

There is also the uncomfortable fact that Manual J itself runs hot. Building science folks like Allison Bailes at Energy Vanguard have shown that a Manual J cooling load still lands 10 to 20 percent above what the house actually needs, and Manual S then allows up to 15 percent oversizing on fixed-capacity equipment, 30 percent on variable. Stack those and your "by the book" system can be a third bigger than reality before anyone has done anything wrong. The standard has safety factors baked into safety factors, which is fine for engineers and lousy for anyone pretending the number is precise.

What to do on Monday

Ask for the Manual J before the equipment is ordered, in writing. It is required by code in any jurisdiction on the 2018 IECC or newer, which is most of the country. If your contractor cannot produce one, you are not getting engineering. You are getting a guess with a warranty.

Run the 30-second check yourself. Divide your conditioned square footage by the tonnage on the nameplate. Per Bailes' rule of thumb, a high-performance home should land at 1,000 square feet per ton or more. If you have a reasonably built 2,000-square-foot house with a 4-ton unit, 500 square feet per ton, something is off, and you should ask what, specifically, the calc says the load is.

Demand the latent number, not just the total. A proper Manual J produces sensible and latent loads separately. In humid climates the latent piece is the whole game, and many high-SEER units are mediocre dehumidifiers. A contractor who only talks tons is not sizing for your climate.

Put the software excuse in the contract. If your builder claims a load calc is too expensive or slow, point them at the tools: ACCA-approved desktop software does it in about an hour, and the AI plan-readers do block loads in minutes. "It takes too long" stopped being true around the time your phone got a better camera than your first digital camera.

Prefer variable capacity if the sizing is uncertain. When the envelope data is fuzzy, which it always is on a retrofit, a modulating heat pump absorbs the error that would punish a single-stage unit. Right-sizing still matters. It just matters less when the machine can think.

Limitations of this analysis

The FSEC field data is Florida, from the nineties, on nineties construction. It is the best field study of its kind and also thirty years old, and I could not find a comparable modern national field survey, which means the "over half oversized" figure describes that place and that era, not necessarily your suburb today. The NREL energy-impact finding is simulation, not field data, and it is specific to hot-humid climates; heating-dominated climates have their own dynamics. The under-one-percent cycling penalty is my own calculation using NREL's published model with a degradation coefficient of 0.07 and a 40-percent-oversized case against a Manual-S-compliant baseline at 60 percent part load; change the assumptions and the number moves, though it stays small. The AI tool capabilities described here come from vendor claims and trade-press coverage; I found no independent accuracy benchmarking of HVAKR, Energent.ai, or TruCalc against ACCA-certified software, and it is unclear which of them produce code-compliant Manual J reports versus block loads. Equipment price premiums per ton vary enough by market that I did not put a dollar figure on the upfront cost of oversizing; the premium exists, the amount is local.

Your air conditioner was probably picked by someone who never did the math, and the math now takes twenty minutes. There is no version of this story where that is acceptable. Ask for the calculation. It is your house, your money, and, as of this year, your contractor has run out of excuses.
