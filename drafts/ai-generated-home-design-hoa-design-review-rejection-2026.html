Your AI Designed Your House for $20. The Design Review Committee Thinks in Months, Not Minutes.

By Elena Vasquez · September 27, 2026
← Back to articles

Picture the street at dusk. Every house on the block wears a pitched roof and a brick or clapboard face, the way streets in this zip code have looked for sixty years. Then there is the new one: a flat-roofed glass box, glowing from within like a lantern someone set down in the wrong neighborhood. It is, by every measurable standard, the best-designed house on the block. Inside, rooms flow and the energy model sings. And the Architectural Review Committee is going to have opinions about it.

This is the collision nobody in the AI home-design business wants to talk about. Tools like Maket can now generate a complete residential design from a text prompt in minutes, for twenty dollars a month. They optimize for everything they can count: rooms per square foot, cost per square foot, even zoning compliance through a built-in regulatory assistant. What they cannot count is the one thing that kills more single-family designs than any code violation: whether a committee of your neighbors finds the thing beautiful enough to live next to.

A machine answering the wrong question

Maket's numbers are genuinely impressive. Maket's free tier hands you 50 credits with no card required; the $20-a-month homeowner plan buys 300 credits, with each generated floor costing 20 credits and each photorealistic render costing 10. It will even answer zoning questions in plain language. For a homeowner sketching options before hiring an architect, it is the fastest twenty dollars in residential design.

But notice what the regulatory assistant regulates. It answers setbacks, height limits, lot coverage. It does not answer the sentence that actually governs most suburban design review: the proposed design must show "similarity in architectural style" with adjoining houses and the neighborhood setting. That line comes from a real HOA's architectural guidelines, the Salem Run community's, and it is echoed in thousands of CC&Rs across the country: compatible, consistent, traditional, in character. None of those words are computable. They are judgments, made by volunteers, in rooms, with long memories.

Design review boards have always been capricious about exactly this. In Seattle, all-volunteer boards with final say and no formal appeal have held up projects for months or years over the color of brick facades, adding hundreds of thousands of dollars to apartment projects. Portland just moved to temporarily suspend design review in most cases because the process was shaving weeks or months off housing timelines. Seattle exempts single-family homes from its program, which is precisely why the HOA architectural committee is the single-family equivalent: same aesthetic authority, smaller room, no appeal.

Rejected: the design, not the drawings

Here is the failure mode, stated plainly. Generators optimize the plan. Committees reject the elevation. And a rejected design is not a drafting problem. You cannot fix "incompatible with neighborhood character" with another 20-credit regeneration pass. You need a different house.

That math is what makes this hurt. A pre-construction analysis of San Diego County residential projects found that drafting, Title 24 compliance, and permit preparation typically run 3 to 4 percent of total project cost: $12,000 to $16,000 on a $400,000 addition. San Diego's plan check process compounds the pain, since each correction round addresses only the previous round's corrections, and incomplete drawings beget more cycles. California's SB 543 now requires agencies to finish permit reviews within 60 days of a complete application, but the clock starts only when the application is deemed complete. An AI-generated design that sails through code review and dies at the design committee has spent the cheap part of the process failing at the expensive part.

Zoom out and the stakes are structural. A 2026 analysis of 1.1 million permits across 60 American cities found that permitting delays, not construction time, account for 30 to 43 percent of the gap in completion times between fast and slow cities. In Los Angeles, developers pay a 50 percent premium, $48 per square foot, for land that arrives with permits already approved. Time to approval is quite literally priced into dirt. Anything that adds a redesign round, a committee cycle, a neighbor dispute, is not an aesthetic footnote. It is money.

Run the pre-flight audit

None of this means the generator is the enemy. A photorealistic render communicates intent to a committee far better than hand sketches ever did, and the honest version of this story includes the possibility that AI visualization actually improves approval odds. What actually kills these projects is the unbriefed prompt: a design generated in a vacuum, optimized for metrics the committee does not grade.

So before you open the generator, take a thirty-minute walk. Count the dominant roof profiles on your block, the cladding materials, the window rhythms. A rule from HOA roofing guides generalizes to whole houses: match the dominant architectural vocabulary of your street, not your personal preference, because the closer the match, the easier the committee's decision. Then read your actual ARC guidelines and highlight every adjective. Compatible. Similar. Consistent. Traditional. Those adjectives are the real design brief. Feed them to the generator as style constraints, in the first prompt, not as a rescue operation after the rejection letter.

And show the render to the affected neighbors before you submit anything. Some HOA documents require their signatures anyway, and a committee that hears "the neighbors have seen it and they're fine" is a committee halfway to yes. Renders are free. A resubmission costs months.

What we did not prove

There is no published dataset of AI-generated residential designs and their approval or rejection rates at design review boards or HOA committees. This rejection risk is reasoned from criteria language and delay data, not measured outcomes. Seattle's and Portland's figures describe multifamily design review; single-family HOA timelines are anecdotal and vary wildly by CC&Rs and state law. Maket's pricing and credit figures come from third-party reviews of its pricing page in August and September 2026; confirm them before quoting. And the pre-flight audit is a heuristic, not legal advice: your CC&Rs, your state, your street.

Here is the deeper truth Elena keeps returning to: a house is not an optimization problem with a roof on it. It is an object in a landscape, on a street, among neighbors who will look at it every day for decades. Machines see square feet. Committees see the street. Until the machine learns to see the street, walk it yourself first.
