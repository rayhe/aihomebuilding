Dallas Found 29,000 Code Violations With Cameras on Garbage Trucks. Your Unpermitted Addition Is in the Frame.

By Catherine Chen · September 19, 2026

Alex Kozlowski runs the zoning office in Sawyer County, Wisconsin, a place better known for lake cabins than for enforcement innovation. Earlier this year he stood before the county board and described his department's best year on record: 162 orders for correction issued, roughly three-quarters of them resolved, and a plan to do better. His plan was to feed the county's 2025 aerial photography and its 2020 aerial photography into an AI overlay, flag every structure that appeared in five years without a permit attached to it, and work the resulting list, a sentence that would have been science fiction in a 2019 zoning office and is now a line item in a county board meeting. No neighbor complaints required, no inspector driving the routes, just two photos and a permit database asking each other questions.

This is the part where homeowners who built the deck on a long weekend start doing mental arithmetic. They should. Unpermitted work used to be found slowly and unevenly. That discovery model has quietly inverted, and almost nobody doing unpermitted work has noticed.

For most of the twentieth century, code enforcement worked like this: an officer drove a route, or a neighbor called in a complaint, and the county found out about your addition the way it found out about everything else, slowly and unevenly. That rationing was the whole system. There were never enough officers to look at every parcel, so discovery was effectively random, and random discovery meant rational people could discount the risk. Build the thing, don't tell anyone, and the odds were on your side.

Your garbage truck has a camera now

Dallas decided the cheapest place to put a camera was on a vehicle already driving past every property. Dallas's code compliance department mounted vision cameras on sanitation brush trucks, chosen because the high vantage gives what one official called "a good overview of the city," and ran the footage through a system called City Detect that identifies high weeds, code violations, blighted properties, graffiti, and more. That first rollout flagged nearly 29,000 potential property maintenance concerns across 49 issue types, plus about 12,900 right-of-way encroachments. Cleveland signed on in August 2025, bolted two side-facing cameras to a city car, and by the following May had surveyed roughly 158,000 parcels, work that previously would have taken 40 officers six months. Cathedral City, California ran a similar drive-by system between October 2025 and January 2026: 186,297 images, 12,489 parcels analyzed, 623 parcel violations across 36 categories, every one GPS-tagged and photographed, with before-and-after imagery good enough to close complaints without a second site visit.

None of these systems writes a ticket. Every program in this article has humans in the loop, a fact worth stating before the outrage arrives: Dallas reviews and validates every AI-generated detection before any enforcement action, and Cleveland looks at the pictures before it sends anyone out. Software nominates conditions for remediation; a person decides whether you get a letter. But the nomination used to be the bottleneck, the part where the county ran out of officers, and the bottleneck is gone. Inspectors now work a ranked queue of conditions already found, which is a fundamentally different job from driving around hoping to find them.

France ran the same play, for money

If you want to see where this goes when the incentive is revenue instead of compliance, look at France. France's tax authority built a system called Foncier innovant that sifted aerial imagery for undeclared additions to properties and cross-referenced them against tax records. Nine regions got the pilot. It found the pools. In Bouches-du-Rhône alone it identified 7,200 undeclared swimming pools, each one worth roughly 200 euros a year in additional property tax. Then the authorities extended the program beyond pools to house extensions, verandas, and garden sheds, with a projected 40 million euros a year in additional levies. Same imagery. Same computer vision. A completely different motive: not a correction letter but a tax bill.

That asymmetry matters, because the technology is identical on both sides of it. France's tax-worker union raised concerns about staff cuts. American civil-liberties critics will raise concerns about something else. But the camera does not know which country's tax code it is enforcing, whether the rectangle it found earns a correction letter or a 200-euro-a-year tax increase; it just finds the rectangle that was not there last year, and that indifference is what makes the same system feel like good government in Dallas and a shakedown in France.

What getting caught costs now

Here is the calculation nobody in the enforcement conversation has published, so I did it. Sawyer County's numbers are unusually transparent: 162 orders for correction, about 75 percent compliance, 14 citations. That means discovered issues convert to citations at roughly 8.6 percent. Sawyer County's stated posture is correction first, citations for the egregious cases, and three-quarters of recipients fix the problem when asked. Getting discovered, in the American compliance-driven model, is mostly an invitation to come into compliance, not a fine, which is why the rational response to discovery is a permit application rather than a lawyer, a distinction that reverses entirely in France, where the same discovery arrives as a tax assessment and the permit office is not the venue where the dispute gets resolved.

Now add the timing finding. A June 2026 preprint from the property-intelligence firm DirtSignal, working with Jacksonville, Florida code-enforcement records, found that public street-level and aerial imagery carries a statistically significant signal of a future first citation, strongest within six months before the citation and detectable up to a year out. Some properties were visibly neglected for years before any record existed, which means the condition was photographable long before it was actionable. There is a window between when the camera sees it and when the officer knocks. A homeowner who self-reports inside that window converts an 8.6-percent-citation discovery into a routine permit fee. Cheapest move in the new enforcement era: call the permit office before the permit office calls you.

Another asymmetry: who benefits. Aerial-imagery vendors already sell change detection to assessors and insurers, the same before-and-after comparison Sawyer County is building in-house. A homebuyer can do the county's trick for free: pull the permit history from the municipal portal, pull the assessor's property sketch, compare both against the listing photos, and treat any mismatch as the same discrepancy the county's overlay will surface when it gets around to your street, except you found it before closing, when it is still the seller's problem. Any mismatch is now the county's business too, which makes it your leverage at the negotiating table and your liability after closing. Unpermitted work has always been a disclosure and pricing problem; it is now a disclosure and pricing problem with a timestamped photograph attached.

Why the panic may be overblown

Stated at full strength: none of these systems is a robot ticketing machine, and pretending otherwise is how you write a bad article. Every detection gets human review. No program has published a false-positive rate or an appeal-overturn rate, so we do not know how often the AI is wrong, only that a person is supposed to catch it. Aerial imagery cannot see interiors, which biases the entire apparatus toward exterior footprint changes: the unpermitted bathroom gut that creates the actual safety hazard stays invisible while the well-built deck gets flagged, a targeting error built into the physics of the camera. France's critics argue, with some force, that a revenue-driven system corrupts enforcement into taxation, and the American version has its own ceiling, currently on display in Washington, where the House Oversight Committee is investigating the D.C. Department of Buildings' plan to mount panoramic cameras on city vehicles and use AI to find vacant properties and maintenance violations. Chairman Comer's concern is that automated discovery feeding fines feeding tax debt puts owners "on a path towards foreclosure," which is the enforcement pipeline at its most regressive. The technology is neutral; the fine schedule is not. And a homeowner in a wealthy suburb with a permit expediter on retainer experiences this system very differently from one without.

What to actually do, by who you are

If you are selling a house with unpermitted work, you have two legal options and one fantasy. Fantasy: the buyer, the appraiser, the lender, and the county all fail to notice. Disclose the work and price it, or legalize it before listing through retroactive permitting, which often costs multiples of the original permit fee, plus possible fines, and is still cheaper than a deal falling apart in escrow when the appraiser flags it. If your county has announced an aerial or drive-by program, assume the before-and-after already exists.

If you are buying, pull the permit history and the assessor's sketch before you waive contingencies, and walk the property against both. A finished basement the permits never mention is a negotiation item and a future enforcement item. Get the seller to permit it or discount it, in writing.

If you are the DIYer eyeing a weekend deck, price the permit into the project the way you price the lumber. The permit is the cheapest component. Retroactive permitting is not, and the discovery lottery you used to play has been replaced by a spreadsheet that updates itself. "Nobody will notice" expired the year the garbage truck got cameras.

If you are a contractor, know that scope-match and permit-expiration are now machine-checkable. Pulling a permit for the kitchen and building the ADU, or letting the permit lapse while the work continues, creates exactly the kind of discrepancy these overlays are built to find.

What this analysis did not prove

The 8.6 percent citation conversion is Sawyer County's, a single rural Wisconsin jurisdiction, and urban programs with different fine schedules will convert differently. DirtSignal's finding is a preprint from one city, Jacksonville, and its striking result, that a four-number greenness measure beat 768-dimensional foundation-model embeddings, needs replication before anyone generalizes it. France's 40-million-euro figure was a projection for 2023, not collected revenue, and per-pool tax figures are departmental averages. D.C.'s camera program is under congressional investigation, not confirmed deployed, and municipal self-reporting (Dallas's 29,000, Cathedral City's 623 violations) has no independent audit. None of the programs publishes how often the AI is wrong. And everything in this article covers exterior changes only: the interior remodel done without a permit remains, for now, a matter between you and your conscience.
