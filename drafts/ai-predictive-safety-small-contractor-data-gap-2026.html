Oracle's AI Predicts Jobsite Accidents From 10,000 Project-Years of Data. Your Roofer's Four-Man Crew Isn't in It.

By Marcus Washington, September 13, 2026, Workforce & Labor

About twenty construction workers will not come home this week. Next week, about twenty more. That is the arithmetic behind OSHA's published fatality count: 1,075 construction deaths in a single year, divided by 52 weeks, which lands at 20.7 men and women per week, every week, in an industry that accounts for roughly one in five of all private-sector worker deaths in America. Falls kill more of them than anything else, followed by the rest of the grim procession OSHA calls the Fatal Four: struck-by, electrocution, caught-in or caught-between. The Fatal Four have topped that list for decades, which tells you everything about how little the underlying work has changed while the technology around it got smarter.

I come from a union household, so I read safety statistics the way other people read box scores, and I have learned to be suspicious of any technology that promises to fix a body count with a dashboard. This March, at ConExpo in Las Vegas, Oracle unveiled Advisor for Safety, an AI platform that ingests safety observations, incident reports, payroll data, and project schedules, then spits out a weekly forecast ranking a contractor's projects from most to least likely to produce an injury. Josh Kanner, Oracle's senior director of analytics and AI, framed the ambition plainly: move from lagging indicators to leading ones, from reacting to predicting, which is a genuinely good idea and one the industry has chased since Oracle started chewing on field data back in 2014. Oracle says early customers cut incident rates by up to half and workers' compensation costs by as much as three-quarters in the first year.

Oracle is not alone in this bet. Autodesk's Construction IQ trained its models on 150 million issues and checklist observations across 30,000 projects, and it specifically learned to flag which open issues were most likely to end in a fall injury. This May, ISN launched Hazard Assistant, which reads jobsite photos with AI and maps what it sees onto hazard categories so a foreman can act before the energy in the picture finds a worker. Carnegie Mellon's Safe-Construct research pushes further, teaching cameras to recognize safety violations in 3D across cluttered sites. Serious engineering. Serious money. Same blind spot.

Read the input list, not the brochure

Now read what these systems eat. Oracle's list: safety observations, incident reports, payroll data, project schedules, pulled from enterprise platforms like Aconex and Primavera Unifier. Autodesk's list: 150 million digitized issues and checklist observations from BIM 360, a platform whose sweet spot is projects from $5 million to over a billion. ISN's list: structured contractor qualification data from its global customer base.

Every item on those lists is something a large general contractor produces as a matter of routine, and almost nothing a four-man roofing crew in Fresno produces at all. That is the finding nobody in the trade press has spelled out, so I will: predictive safety AI is trained overwhelmingly on data from the safest, most bureaucratized tier of the industry, and it is sold back to that same tier. Residential home building, where a huge share of the dying actually happens, runs on small specialty contractors whose injuries barely enter the structured record, kept on paper if they are kept at all, in the gang box or the foreman's truck, which is to say nowhere a model can learn from.

Mechanism matters here, because this is not an accident of product design but the predictable output of a reporting regime built for factories and applied, badly, to an industry of pickup trucks and subcontractors. Federal electronic injury reporting covers establishments with 100 or more employees, and employers with ten or fewer workers are partially exempt from keeping injury and illness records at all. Your roofer, your framer, your stucco sub: most operate far below those thresholds, which means their sprains, their falls, their near-misses dissolve into anecdote instead of becoming training data. When Oracle says its model learned from 10,000 project-years, picture whose project-years those were. Not the two-story re-roof on Elm Street.

Put it bluntly. The crews with the highest fatality rates generate the least training data. So the AI safety revolution is being built for the companies least likely to kill someone. Hispanic workers, who make up 34 percent of the construction workforce and 63.9 percent of roofers, died at a rate 41.6 percent higher than non-Hispanic workers in 2020, per CPWR figures summarized by the American Society of Safety Professionals. Those are the workers on the small residential crews. They are the missing rows in the dataset. Rows, not people. The models never met them.

Even the government's algorithm whiffed

Skepticism about prediction is not theoretical. OSHA itself runs an algorithm-driven targeting program, Site-Specific Targeting, which picks establishments for inspection based on their DART rates, upward injury trends, and failure to file injury reports. A few years ago the Department of Labor ran the gold-standard test on it: a randomized controlled trial plus a regression discontinuity design. Result, quoted from the department's own evaluation: neither design "found statistically significant impacts." The letters didn't move injury rates. The inspections didn't either, at least not measurably within three years.

And note the fine print from the lawyers who advise employers on the current targeting guidance: small employers that aren't required to file injury data electronically are excluded from the program entirely. The government's list, like the AI's training set, has a hole exactly where the small residential contractor stands, which means the two most sophisticated injury-prediction systems in American industry are both blind to the same six guys on the same roof.

The strongest case against all of this

Let me steelman the other side, because the workers deserve better than a strawman. First, prediction is not prevention: a weekly ranking tells a safety director where to look, but the fix remains a human being walking the deck and telling a crew to tie off, and no model has ever tied a knot. Second, these systems can curdle into surveillance fast. A model that scores subcontractors by predicted risk becomes a blacklist with a math degree, and workers have earned their distrust of cameras that grade them, especially when the same technology never seems to grade the schedule pressure that created the hazard. Third, the vendor numbers deserve a hard stare: Oracle's 50 percent and 75 percent figures come from customer internal documentation, not peer review, and nobody independent has audited them. If your safety director's bonus depends on the pilot succeeding, the pilot succeeds, which is why customer-reported ROI figures in enterprise software deserve to be read the way you would read a fishing story.

What you can actually do Monday morning

If you are building or remodeling a home, none of this AI will ever touch your project, and that is precisely why the boring old tools matter more for you. Here are four moves, and every one of them is free.

Search the public record: OSHA's Establishment Search publishes inspection and violation history by company name. Run your general contractor and the big subs through it before you sign. A pattern of repeat fall-protection violations is a better predictor of your project's risk than any AI on the market, because it is your contractor's actual history.

Ask for the EMR: The Experience Modification Rate compares a contractor's workers' comp claims to the industry average. Below 1.0 beats the average; above 1.2 should make you ask hard questions. Any legitimate contractor knows this number cold, and hesitation is an answer.

Ask about the fall plan, specifically: Falls are the number one killer in construction, residential roofing is where it happens, and the crew most likely to fall is the one the models have never seen. Ask who enforces fall protection on your roof, and whether the safety talk happens in the language the crew actually speaks. If the answer is vague, the plan is vague.

Discount the clean record: "We've never had an accident" from a six-person shop is statistically meaningless; a crew that small could go years between incidents by luck alone. Ask what they do, not what they've avoided.

What this story can't prove

Here is the honest accounting of what this story cannot prove. I could not verify Oracle's incident-reduction claims against any independent audit, and Oracle has not published pricing, so I can't tell you what this costs or whether the ROI math survives contact with a residential P&L. I could not pull a live OSHA statistics page during reporting, so the fatality figures above are OSHA's published numbers as I have them, rounded and attributed. And the core of this piece is structural inference, not a leaked dataset: I am arguing from the reporting thresholds and the vendors' own input lists that small residential contractors are underrepresented in the training data, which is a strong inference but not a number I computed from inside anyone's model. If Oracle or Autodesk publishes the firm-size distribution of their training data tomorrow, I will update this gladly.

Here is the uncomfortable bottom line. The industry is building genuinely impressive machines that predict injuries for companies with safety departments, and the workers dying at twenty a week mostly work for companies without one. Until the data includes the four-man crew, the forecast will keep missing the roof where it matters, and no dashboard, however elegant, changes what happens on a roof at 7 a.m. when the foreman is running late and the tie-off point is a forty-minute drive away. Check the public record, ask for the EMR, and make somebody say the fall plan out loud. The AI can't do that part for you. Nobody can. Not yet.
