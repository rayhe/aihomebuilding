$6.8 Billion in Termite Damage a Year. Your Inspector Spent 20 Minutes in the Crawl Space With a Flashlight.

By Catherine Chen, September 15, 2026, Policy & Regulation

Somewhere under a California ranch house this week, a man on his belly shone a flashlight at a mud tube the width of a pencil lead and wrote down a finding that will move thousands of dollars. That is not a metaphor; in this state the entire financial defense against a $6.8 billion national problem is one licensed human, one flashlight, one flat-headed screwdriver, and about twenty minutes of looking at wood.

Termites cause an estimated $6.8 billion in U.S. property damage every year, according to the National Pest Management Association, and "most homeowners insurance doesn't cover the cost of repairs" (NPMA via BusinessWire). Standard HO-3 policies exclude insect damage, which means the single most expensive pest category in residential America is a risk you cannot insure against no matter how much premium you pay. Your only financial instrument is the inspection. Nothing else exists.

Which makes the inspection ritual, with its statutory backbone and its billion-dollar consequences, worth examining with considerably more care than the twenty minutes the ritual itself allows.

A flashlight with unusual legal weight

California's wood-pest report is not a home inspection; it is a legal document with a statutory backbone that divides the world into Section 1 conditions, meaning evident infestation or infection, and Section 2 conditions, meaning things likely to lead to infestation, and that single division decides who pays for what in escrow. Sellers typically clear Section 1 while Section 2 is negotiable, and once a report exists, the seller's broker must deliver it, the certification, and the notice of work completed to the buyer under Commissioner's Regulation 2905. California's Department of Real Estate has made clear that agents do not get to curate the record (Expert DRE Compliance).

Case law on this point is not subtle. In Godfrey versus Steinpress (1982), a seller's agent delivered the first of two conflicting termite reports and kept the second one quiet, and the second report showed an active infestation. Liability followed: the agent owed the buyer's repair costs, because an undisclosed report is a material fact and agents cannot pick and choose which reports to deliver (FirstTuesday Journal). Forty-four years later, the law's answer to inspection fallibility is not better inspections. It is full disclosure of every fallible one. The code does not care about your feelings. It never has.

So consider what the underlying evidence actually is. USA Today, quoting NPMA's senior vice president for public affairs this spring, puts a proper inspection at $100 to $400, with most homeowners paying in the low $200s (USA Today). That same piece concedes that do-it-yourself checks with a flashlight and a screwdriver "can be tedious and unsuccessful." Professionally, it is the same tools, plus a license, and nobody in the industry pretends otherwise.

Math nobody publishes

Here is the original finding in this article, and it is arithmetic, not journalism: the industry publishes the $6.8 billion figure and the $2,300 average treatment cost, but nobody runs the ratio that matters to a homeowner, so we did. Inputs first. Here is every input, with sources.

Inspection: $250, the midpoint of the NPMA's $100-to-$400 range. Spot treatment, caught while localized: $375, from PestControlPricing's 2026 range of $250 to $500 (PestControlPricing). Fumigation, missed one cycle: $5,500, their range's midpoint between $4,000 and $8,000, plus structural wood repair on top, which routinely adds thousands more.

Caught one cycle early, the bill is $250 plus $375, about $625 total. Missed one cycle, the bill is $250 plus $5,500 plus repairs, somewhere between $8,000 and $12,000. One early detection is therefore worth roughly $7,400 to $11,400 in avoided cost, a 30-to-45-times return on a $250 inspection, which means a single catch pays for three decades of annual inspections while the inspections themselves were always the cheap part.

A second finding inverts the usual framing: because insurance covers $0 of the $6.8 billion, the inspection is not a fee. It is the deductible on an uninsurable risk. Think about that. No actuary prices it and no adjuster processes it; it is the only line of defense a homeowner can buy, and it costs less than a tank of gas in a Suburban.

Cameras are coming

Two kinds of AI are now pointed at this problem, and they are different products that answer different questions.

First, inspection software: Spectora, one of the largest home-inspection platforms, spent more than a year building AI tools that analyze inspection photos and operations, now in early access for inspectors (BusinessWire). Paraspot, reviewed by Inman last November, uses computer vision on a phone to identify common defects and auto-categorize photos by room, producing shareable reports in minutes (Inman). These tools make the evidence trail photographic and timestamped instead of a clipboard note written from memory, which matters because in a disclosure dispute the photograph outlives everyone's recollection of what the flashlight actually showed.

Second, pest-industry hardware: at PestWorld 2025, Groupe Berkem exhibited NOVATERM, a connected bait station that uses AI to alert pest professionals when subterranean termite activity is detected or when a station needs servicing (PCT Online). That answers "is there activity right now," continuously, rather than "was there damage last Tuesday."

Underneath, the research is surprisingly solid: a Taiwanese team collected 24,000 smartphone images across four termite species and trained a MobileNetV2 classifier that hit 0.947 accuracy on soldiers and 0.946 on workers, comparable to three human experts while processing 8,000 images 65 times faster (bio-it-station/DL-termite-identification). Separately, an MDPI-published study trained a YOLOv8 variant on soil-dwelling termite damage signs like mud tubes and deadwood, reaching 0.88 overall accuracy (MDPI Sensors).

None of this is the inspection yet. Not close. Classifying termite specimens in a lab is not finding hidden damage in a dark crawl space, but the direction of travel is obvious, and it runs straight into the legal framework.

Counterargument, stated at full strength

Better detection may make everything worse before it makes anything better, and the mechanism is the law itself.

A more thorough AI-assisted inspection finds more Section 2 conditions, every Section 2 finding is disclosable, every disclosable finding is negotiable, and in a California escrow a cleaner report is worth real money to a seller, which is exactly why Godfrey versus Steinpress happened: the incentive to shop for the gentler inspection is already built into the transaction. Make inspections more sensitive and you increase the number of deals where buyer and seller fight over findings the old flashlight would never have produced. Courts will absorb this exactly as they absorbed the first termite report: with more litigation about which of two conflicting reports was right.

Then there is the false-positive problem, and it is not theoretical: frass, mud tubes, and blistered paint are visually confusable with water damage, wood rot, and carpenter-ant activity. A classifier trained on specimen photos has never crawled under a 1950s ranch in the Central Valley, in August, with fourteen inches of clearance and a flashlight dying, which is where the actual classification task takes place. Taiwan's 0.947 accuracy is for labeled insects, not for damage hidden behind insulation in a 14-inch crawl space under bad lighting. In an escrow, a false positive is not a harmless alert; it is a $3,000 negotiation weapon pointed at a seller, or a dead deal.

And nobody has published the study that matters: the same set of homes, inspected by traditional WDO inspectors and AI-assisted ones, with ground-truth verification by opening the walls. Until that trial exists, every accuracy number in this article, including the impressive-sounding 0.947, describes a different task than the one homebuyers actually pay for, which is not classifying insects but finding damage in the dark.

What this means for you

If you are buying in California, order the WDO inspection early in escrow and ask for the inspector's photo set, not just the checkbox report. Section 1 versus Section 2 determines who pays, and the photographs are the only evidence that survives a dispute, because memories fade and inspectors retire but a timestamped photograph of a mud tube does not.

If you are selling, understand that once a report exists, it is disclosable, and getting a second inspection to shop for a cleaner report is the exact fact pattern that created liability in Godfrey versus Steinpress. Do not do the thing the case is named after. Seriously.

If you own the home you live in, $250 a year for an annual inspection is the cheapest position anywhere on the termite cost ladder. By that math, one early catch pays for thirty years of inspections, which means a homeowner who inspects annually from age thirty to age sixty has bought six decades of protection for the price of a single fumigation, provided the catch comes early. Set the reminder.

And if an inspector or a pest company pitches you AI, ask the specific question: does the tool flag damage, or does it transcribe what the human saw? One of those is a second set of eyes. Its competitor is a dictation machine with better marketing. That $6.8 billion figure has not moved in years, which tells you something about how well the flashlight has been working, and it tells you something else about what the camera will have to prove, and proof, in this business, means surviving a crawl space, a cross-examination, and a buyer's attorney, in that order.

Limitations

What this article did not prove, stated plainly: the $6.8 billion is the NPMA's figure, an industry trade group's estimate repeated unchanged for years with no independent audit we could find, so treat it as directional. Treatment costs are 2026 vendor and aggregator ranges with wide regional variance, not transaction data, and anyone who has collected three termite quotes in the same zip code knows the quotes themselves vary by thousands, which is why the dividend math uses midpoints rather than promises. That 0.947 accuracy describes lab-classified specimen photos, and no published false-positive rate exists for AI termite-damage detection in real crawl spaces. Our inspection-dividend math assumes early detection actually leads to spot treatment; if the colony is already widespread at first inspection, the dividend is zero. California's disclosure framework is the legal lens here, and other states' wood-destroying-organism regimes differ, many with no Section 1/Section 2 analogue.
