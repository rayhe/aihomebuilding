Your House Has 120 Studs It Doesn't Need. The Framer Knows. Now AI Can Prove It.

By Marcus Washington · October 7, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Stand at the cut station on a framing job for one morning and count what goes into the waste pile: not the warped stock or the split ends, but the clean, straight offcuts. Fourteen inches of perfectly good spruce arcing off the saw and into the pile, because the plans called for a stud every sixteen inches and the wall ran out of wall fourteen inches from the last one, and by lunch the pile is knee-high with pieces specified by somebody who never has to carry them.

Ask the men setting those studs which ones the house doesn't need and watch what happens. A framer with twenty years in the trade can point to them the way a mechanic points to a stripped bolt: triple-stud corners, double jack studs under a three-foot window, cripple studs packed under every sill like the window might try to escape. He knows. Decades ago, the NAHB Research Center published the same finding in blunter accounting language: for every piece of unneeded lumber, the builder pays three times. Once to buy it, once to haul it around the job, once to throw it away.

A Wall From 1975

America's standard wall hasn't fundamentally changed since the postwar boom: studs every 16 inches, double top plate, three studs mashed into every corner. It works. It also wastes, in a way you can measure with a tape and a calculator. Building scientists who actually counted found that about a quarter of a conventional wall is solid wood rather than insulation. Building scientists call it the framing factor: the share of the wall that is lumber instead of cavity, and it runs around 25 percent. Each of those studs is a thermal bridge, a small highway conducting heat straight through whatever insulation sits between them.

Multiply that waste across an industry that frames roughly 1.5 million new housing units in a projected 2026, and the pile gets enormous. Framing lumber touched $535 per thousand board feet this summer, a four-year high, up 23 percent from a year earlier, driven by a 10 percent U.S. softwood tariff and Canadian duties topping 45 percent. When lumber costs that much, every unnecessary stud is a small, avoidable tax on the house, and somebody pays it: usually the buyer, folded silently into the price.

An Answer From the 1960s Nobody Used

Optimum value engineering, OVE, has been sitting in the literature since the 1960s, which makes its obscurity the real story. It comes down to a simple recipe: frame 2x6 walls at 24 inches on center instead of 16. Use a single top plate instead of a double. Build corners with two studs and drywall clips instead of three-stud lumber sandwiches. Set ladder blocking where partitions meet exterior walls. Size each header for its actual load instead of defaulting to a doubled 2x12 everywhere. Stack the framing so studs, joists, and rafters align and loads travel straight down to the foundation.

Joe Lstiburek of the Building Science Corporation has spent a career documenting what that recipe buys: roughly 5 to 10 percent less lumber by board feet, 30 percent fewer pieces to cut and carry, and a framing factor cut from 25 percent down to 15 percent, with cavities 60 percent deeper for insulation. None of this requires an engineer's stamp in most jurisdictions. Table R602.3(5) of the International Residential Code permits 2x6 studs at 24-inch spacing in bearing walls. Working with more than 300 production builders across 42,000 homes, the Department of Energy's Building America program documented per-unit savings of $300 to $500 when builders adopted advanced framing. This was never a structural question. It was a habit question, and habits in construction outlive the people who formed them.

A Machine That Counts

What changed is the cost of finding out. A lumber takeoff used to mean an estimator with a scale ruler and the better part of two days. Now a builder uploads a PDF plan set to an AI takeoff service and gets a material list back before lunch. LLAIE, built for lumber yards, advertises a blueprint-to-material-list in under five minutes. Blueprint Pro AI sells same-day takeoffs with a framing package it calls The Sticks+. BuildVision and Bolster automate the symbol detection and measuring, with Bolster claiming its users estimate 84 percent faster.

Read every one of those figures as vendor marketing until proven otherwise, because I did not independently test any of these tools and neither should you take their word for it; the underlying capability is real all the same, which is that machines now count studs faster than any human alive, so a builder can price the same plan twice, once as drawn and once redesigned for advanced framing, and see the difference as a line item instead of a hunch. Finally, the sixty-year-old argument has a receipt, and it arrives before the lumber order goes out.

Running the Numbers

So I ran it, for a representative 2,400-square-foot two-story house on a 40-by-30 footprint. Roughly 280 linear feet of exterior wall across two floors, plus about 200 feet of interior partitions. Nothing exotic, just the kind of box going up by the thousand in every growth suburb in America.

At 16 inches on center, those exterior walls need about 210 studs; at 24 inches, about 140, while interiors run roughly 150 versus 100. Total it up: around 360 studs conventional, 240 advanced. That is 120 fewer studs, a third fewer pieces, which lands exactly where the field data says it should.

~$490 in studs alone 120 fewer 2x6x8 studs at late-September 2026 pricing ($506.76 per thousand board feet, Madison's index). Add the deleted second top plate, right-sized headers, and eliminated cripples: roughly $700 to $850 in lumber per house.

A 2x6x8 stud is 8 board feet, so at $506.76 per thousand board feet each stud costs about $4.05, and 120 of them come to roughly $490. Dropping the second top plate saves around 190 board feet, about $100. Right-sized headers and the vanished cripples and jack studs add a few hundred more. Call it $700 to $850 in lumber for the house, and that is only the most conservative route to the number.

A second route into the number: Home Innovation Research Labs puts the average new single-family home at roughly 15,000 board feet of framing lumber. Shave 5 to 10 percent off that, the board-foot range Lstiburek documents, at about fifty-one cents a board foot, and you get $380 to $760. DOE literature cites around $500 in material savings per 1,200 square feet, which scales to $1,000 for this house. Three different routes into the calculation, and they all land in the same neighborhood: on the order of $400 to $1,000 per home in lumber alone.

Labor is the second prize, and the conditional one. Thirty percent fewer pieces means thirty percent fewer cuts, carries, and nails, and the DOE literature cites framing labor cost reductions of up to 5 percent. That "up to" is doing serious work in the sentence. It assumes a crew that has framed this way before. A crew's first advanced-framing house will go slower, not faster, while everybody learns the new layout and the new corner details. Budget the labor savings starting around house number four, and be honest with the crew about the learning curve instead of pretending it doesn't exist.

Energy is the third prize, and the smallest. Dropping the framing factor from 25 to 15 percent, plus deeper insulation cavities, models out to roughly 5 percent lower heating and cooling bills per the DOE figures. On an $1,800 annual HVAC bill that is $90 a year. Real money over the life of a mortgage, but nobody should adopt advanced framing for the utility savings alone, because lumber and labor are the argument.

Why the Crew Can't Just Do It

Now the part the OVE evangelists tend to skip, because the counterargument here is strong and it deserves its full weight. A single top plate only works with stacked framing: studs, floor joists, and rafters aligned within about an inch so that loads travel straight down. That demands the framer, the truss plant, and the plan set all agree before the first cut. One trade working off an older detail breaks the load path, and the inspector red-tags the wall. Precision is not free; it is a different kind of labor, and it has to be planned. Then there is the pay structure: production framers are typically paid by the square foot or by the piece, and nobody pays them extra for thinking harder. A framer who has set 16-inch walls for twenty years will frame his first 24-inch house slower, and his bid does not care about your lumber savings. Drywall hung at 24-inch centers often needs 5/8-inch board on ceilings to prevent sag, which hands money straight back to the drywall contractor. In high-wind and seismic zones the double top plate and tight nailing schedules are not suggestions, and full OVE there needs engineering. Most plan sets never get drawn for advanced framing in the first place because the architect's insurance carrier flinches at single top plates.

Sequencing, though, is the deepest problem, because OVE has to be designed in before the permit is issued. Value-engineering it in after the plans are stamped costs more than it saves, because every downstream detail assumed the old walls. It is sixty years old and still rare, for a reason nobody in the industry likes to say out loud: the savings accrue to whoever buys the lumber, while the risk accrues to whoever swings the hammer and whoever stamps the plans, and those are different people: the ones holding the risk have no incentive to volunteer, and the people holding the savings have never been asked to share them.

218,950 carpenters work in residential building construction at a mean $28.04 an hour (BLS). The bureau projects 35,000 more carpenters needed by 2035, while ADP data shows the median carpenter's age falling as veterans retire out. The industry cannot afford to spend a third of its framing labor on studs the plans didn't need.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The dollar math uses a national composite lumber index from late September 2026; species, grade, and region will move it 20 percent in either direction. Fifteen thousand board feet is a national average, and the representative house is illustrative rather than a real takeoff from a real plan set. Labor savings assume an experienced crew, and the first-house penalty is real. Energy savings are modeled by the Department of Energy, not metered house by house. Across the literature, the NAHB savings figures date to studies from the 1990s, in then-year dollars; they show direction, not precision. Vendor marketing covers the AI takeoff speed claims, which I could not independently verify. Code citations reflect the base International Residential Code; California, Florida, and high-seismic jurisdictions amend the details, so check local amendments before deleting a top plate.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you build ten or more homes a year, run one plan through an AI takeoff twice: once as drawn, and once redesigned for advanced framing by a designer who actually knows the details. At roughly $850 a house in lumber, twenty houses is $17,000 a year, and the redesign has to happen before permit, never after. If you frame for a living and you are paid lump sum, OVE is margin: fewer pieces, same bid, and the layout work is skilled work worth charging for. If you are paid by the piece, renegotiate, because the thinking is the work now.

Smaller builders can cherry-pick the low-risk details without touching stud spacing at all: two-stud corners with drywall clips, ladder blocking at intersections, headers sized for the actual span. No engineering, no inspector fights, and still roughly 15 percent fewer pieces. And if you are buying a house rather than building one, ask the builder for the framing factor of your walls. Fifteen percent versus twenty-five is the difference between a wall that is mostly insulation and a wall that is one-quarter solid wood. It is also a reliable test of whether your builder is still framing like it is 1975.
