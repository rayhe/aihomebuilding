Your Adjuster Denied the Claim. A $400 Drone Flight Is How You Get It Back.

When Hurricane Nicole tore across Lisa Cimaroli's roof, her project manager did everything by the book: climbed the slope, photographed the damage, filed the claim, and watched the insurance company say no.

Then Cimaroli sent up a drone. An autonomous flight mapped her roof while the AI damage detector counted 240 impact marks the human inspection had missed, and once those annotated images landed on the adjuster's desk, the denial reversed and the insurer paid for the entire roof. Read that twice.

That story comes from EagleView's marketing blog, so read it like any vendor case study: the numbers are theirs, the outcome is theirs, and nobody independently audited it. But the dynamic it describes is real, and it rides on an ugly number. A 2026 industry guide puts the initial denial rate on roof insurance claims at 15 to 20 percent, with missing documentation as the most common reason. Your adjuster didn't see the damage, because a ladder inspection covers a fraction of the slope in fading light while a drone photographs every square foot, timestamps it, and counts the impacts before the adjuster ever climbs a ladder.

Small Hail, Big Argument

Carriers deny hail claims using a test called functional damage, and they make the argument like this: small hail bruises shingles cosmetically without shortening their life, so there is no covered loss. Your roof aged; the storm just photographed it.

Peer-reviewed science now disagrees, and the disagreement is not a blog post or a vendor white paper. Researchers at the Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety published a study in Frontiers in Materials finding that concentrations of sub-severe hailstones, under an inch in diameter, significantly reduce a shingle roof's resistance to future large hail and accelerate asphalt aging. Those small hits turn out to be load-bearing evidence for the next storm's failure, which is a problem if your carrier already wrote them off as cosmetic.

Plaintiffs' lawyers are already citing the study to support roof claims, while defense lawyers read the same paper and see gradual wear, which most policies exclude. Two sets of attorneys, one dataset, opposite conclusions, and Carrier Management's writeup captures the split cleanly. That is your signal for how unsettled this is at the claims desk, and it is exactly why documentation quality matters more than it used to.

What the Drone Actually Buys You

Residential drone inspections come in three tiers, and the pricing is refreshingly legible for this industry. Tier 1 is imagery only, $150 to $250: a licensed pilot flies your roof and hands you 20 to 50 high-resolution photos, which makes a fine condition baseline but thin evidence for a disputed claim. Tier 2 adds thermal imaging, $250 to $400. Infrared finds trapped moisture under shingles that no eyeball catches, and that single capability is what turns a denied cosmetic claim into a documented water-intrusion claim, which is a category of damage adjusters have a much harder time dismissing. Tier 3 adds a 3D orthomosaic model with measurements, $350 to $500, built in software like DroneDeploy or Pix4D, with precise area and slope numbers an adjuster can plug straight into an estimate.

Stack that against the alternatives, because the alternatives are what you're actually choosing between. An independent certified inspector runs $450 to $800, a forensic engineering report with a sealed opinion starts at $1,500, and both cost more than the drone tiers while buying you nothing the timestamped imagery doesn't already deliver. Of every documentation option on the market, the drone tiers are the cheapest an adjuster can't dismiss as a contractor's sales pitch, because no ladder touched the roof, no commission rode on the verdict, and the imagery carries its own timestamps. One practical note: any commercial drone operation in the United States requires the pilot to hold an FAA Part 107 Remote Pilot Certificate, so ask to see it before the flight.

Break-Even Math Nobody Runs for You

Nobody in the inspection business runs this calculation for you, because it cuts both ways, so let's do it anyway.

Industry guides put the average wind and hail claim payment at $13,000, and once you subtract a typical $2,500 deductible, the value at stake in a denial is $10,500, while a Tier 2 or Tier 3 drone inspection runs about $400.

Four hundred divided by ten thousand five hundred is 3.8 percent, which is the break-even: at that ratio the inspection pays for itself if it reduces your chance of a denial by four percentage points. Against a baseline denial rate of 15 to 20 percent, the documentation doesn't have to be magic; it just has to move your odds a little, which is a far lower bar than the inspection industry's marketing implies. That's the whole pitch.

Two honest caveats ride along with that number, and neither is small, because nobody has published a study measuring how much drone documentation actually moves denial rates; the 240-impact reversal is a vendor anecdote, told by the company selling the drones about a customer who had already hired the company selling the drones. And since the $13,000 average comes from an industry guide rather than carrier filings, treat the math as a decision tool, not a promise, and re-run it with your own deductible and your roof's replacement cost before spending a dime.

What the Skeptics Get Right

Functional-damage tests exist for a reason, and the reason is not conspiracy. Roofs in hail corridors take repeated hits, policies exclude wear and tear, and a drone that counts 240 anomalies has not proven a covered peril. Bruised mat versus displaced granule is a judgment call that depends on shingle age, granule loss patterns, and whether the inspector bothers to chalk a test square, and no model currently on the market makes it reliably. Models count. Humans decide. Documentation doesn't create coverage you never had.

Then there is the vendor fog: EagleView's “indisputable evidence” line is marketing copy, and its much-quoted 98.77 percent accuracy figure is about measurements, not damage verdicts, and heavy tree cover degrades every aerial product on the market. And on an old roof in a hail corridor, carriers increasingly skip the denial dance entirely and just non-renew at the next term, a trend no inspection can outrun and no documentation can reverse.

Nobody Walks the Roof

Here is the argument the drone companies underplay, probably because fear doesn't sell as well as AI, even though the numbers are on their side. Fall protection has been OSHA's most-cited construction violation for 15 straight years, with 4,041 citations in fiscal 2026, ladders drew another 1,659, and in 2024, 844 American workers died in falls, still the leading cause of death in construction, which means every aerial survey quietly eliminates the single most dangerous task in residential roofing: putting a human on a pitched surface. Every drone flight is a roof nobody had to walk and a ladder nobody had to misjudge, which makes documentation the product and the un-walked roof the dividend.

What This Analysis Didn't Prove

A few honest boundaries apply here. We could not verify per-inspection pricing beyond published tier ranges, and regional markets vary. No third-party audit exists for AI hail-detection accuracy claims, so treat every vendor count as approximate. Both the denial-rate and average-payout figures come from a single 2026 industry guide, not from state insurance filings, and we didn't model state-by-state differences in matching statutes or cosmetic-damage exclusions, which means a homeowner in Texas hail alley and a homeowner in Oregon face completely different coverage math that this article does not pretend to capture. That break-even math also assumes the inspection is independent; a contractor's free drone flight, bundled with a repair bid, carries the same conflict of interest as the free ladder inspection.

Should You Buy One?

If a storm just hit and you are about to file, the answer is yes, and Tier 2 is the minimum worth buying, because imagery alone rarely survives a coverage fight. Thermal is what turns "cosmetic" into "water intrusion," and water intrusion is what adjusters can't hand-wave. Get the report as a timestamped PDF before the adjuster's visit, not after, and confirm the pilot's Part 107 certificate, because the fastest way to lose a documentation fight is to hand the carrier a reason to disqualify your documentation. Ask whether the inspection fee credits toward repairs if you hire that contractor.

If your roof is over 15 years old in a hail corridor, skip the inspection and spend the money researching Class 4 impact-resistant shingles instead, because your carrier is pricing your non-renewal either way. IBHS data on 320,000 homes found impact-resistant roofs had 40 percent fewer claims and 55 percent lower losses. A drone documents the roof you have; the shingles decide whether you keep your policy.
