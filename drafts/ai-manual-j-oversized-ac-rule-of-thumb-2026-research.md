## Key findings

1. **The rule of thumb:** "500 square feet per ton" (sometimes 400-600) sizes ACs from floor area alone. For a 2,000 sq ft home that means a 4-ton (48,000 BTU/hr) unit, no math required. Source: furnaceprices.ca survey of contractor practice; HVAC School podcast (Jan 27, 2026) notes it was sensible for leaky pre-1980 houses.

2. **What Manual J actually finds:** Building scientist Allison Bailes (Energy Vanguard) ran 40 Manual J load calculations on real homes in hot and mixed climates. Average: **1,431 sq ft per ton** (range 624-3,325). Newer homes run 800+ sq ft/ton; high-performance homes reach 2,500. A contractor using 500 sf/ton installs a system **2 to 5 times larger than needed**. Source: https://www.energyvanguard.com/hvac-design/hvac-load-calculations/

3. **The code already requires the math:** A 2009 IRC amendment to Section M1401.3 reads: "Heating and cooling equipment shall be sized in accordance with ACCA Manual S based on building loads calculated in accordance with ACCA Manual J." ENERGY STAR new homes requires cooling oversize of no more than 15%. Source: https://www.contractingbusiness.com/archive/article/20863204/why-acca-manual-s-means-superior-equipment-sizing

4. **Enforcement is spotty:** On the Building Code Forum, a code official reports requiring Manual J on changeouts and finding "most of the older units were greatly oversized"; contractors "screaming"; most neighboring jurisdictions do not require calcs on changeouts at all. Source: https://www.thebuildingcodeforum.com/forum/threads/manual-j-on-change-out-on-existing-sytems.1439/

5. **Consequences of oversizing:** Rutgers NJ Green Building Manual: HVAC is 48% of typical US home energy; oversized equipment short-cycles, causing poor humidity control, moisture/mold issues, and excess wear. NYSERDA: energy/comfort impacts begin at 120% of building load; **severe impacts above 150%**. Sources: https://greenmanual.rutgers.edu/nr-properly-sized-hvac-equipment/ and https://da.nyserda.ny.gov/-/media/Project/Nyserda/Files/Publications/Fact-Sheets/CHC-CON-ashp-oversize-fs.pdf

6. **Bigger equipment worsens airflow, which is already broken:** DOE's 2018 review "Residential HVAC Installation Practices" found fewer than one in three residential systems move enough airflow; National Comfort Institute data shows average total external static pressure of 0.82" w.c. vs. 0.50" fan ratings. Static pressure rises with the square of airflow, so upsizing tonnage on a bad duct system makes the airflow problem worse. Source: https://www.contractingbusiness.com/residential-hvac/article/55339954/the-top-3-reasons-you-shouldnt-oversize-inverter-based-equipment

7. **Measured cost of the mistake:** An OSTI-indexed 1996 study notes units "100% larger than needed" are not uncommon; modeling a 30%-smaller compressor retrofit showed +30% EER at 95F, +34% SEER, and 39% lower power demand (reduced cycling losses). Source: https://www.osti.gov/biblio/238524

8. **Upfront price of oversizing:** PickHVAC installed-cost data: 2.5-ton $4,250-$8,600; 4-ton $5,950-$11,300. Midpoints: ~$6,425 vs ~$8,625, a **~$2,200 delta**. AllBetter 2026 guide: typical replacement $3,500-$7,500, national average ~$5,500; "tonnage is the biggest lever." Sources: https://www.pickhvac.com/central-air-conditioner/cost/by-tonnage/ and https://allbetterapp.com/ac-replacement-cost/

9. **The AI fix is real and consolidating:** Conduit Tech (founded 2022 by Marisa Reddy and Shelby Breger) does iPad LiDAR home scans generating 2D floorplans/3D models and ACCA-certified Manual J load calculations in under 15 minutes, with permit-ready reports. ServiceTitan announced its acquisition (Thoma Bravo press release; expected close ServiceTitan fiscal Q3 2026). Sources: https://getconduit.com/ and https://www.thomabravo.com/press-releases/servicetitan-to-empower-hvac-contractors-with-acquisition-of-conduit-tech

10. **Why the rule persists:** HVAC School (Jan 2026): the rule fit leaky old houses with big sensible loads; modern tight homes have smaller sensible loads but higher moisture risk, so the rule now overshoots latent capacity specifically, the capacity that controls humidity. Source: https://hvacschool.libsyn.com/why-square-footage-tonnage-short-276

## Original contribution
The assembled oversize-tax math: for a 2,000 sq ft newer home, rule-of-thumb (4 tons) vs. Manual J-typical (2.5 tons) = ~$2,200 extra upfront at cost-table midpoints, and 4/2.5 = 160% of load, which lands past NYSERDA's 150% "severe impacts" threshold. Plus the enforcement-gap cross-reference: code has mandated Manual J/S since the 2009 IRC, yet changeout enforcement is discretionary by jurisdiction. Plus Bailes' kicker that even a correctly executed Manual J overshoots 15-40% (McFarland 20-40%, Butler 15%), meaning the "right" answer ships with its own fudge factor.

## Strongest counterargument
Oversizing has honest defenders. Nobody ever got a callback because the house cooled too fast; Manual J designs to the 1% summer condition, and heat waves now blow past old design temps; additions and ADUs raise future load; infiltration inputs are educated guesses, so engineers pad; and modern inverter equipment ramps down, softening the cycling penalty. The rebuttal: the inverter defense fails on airflow (static pressure still squares with airflow, and most ducts are already at 0.82" vs. 0.50" rated), and padding for a hypothetical addition taxes every summer until the addition exists.

## Limitations
- Cost ranges are wide national averages; local bids vary by thousands.
- Bailes' 40-home sample is hot/mixed climates, not a national survey.
- Conduit Tech's accuracy and "ACCA-certified" claims are vendor claims; no independent audit exists.
- Manual J enforcement varies by jurisdiction; the forum thread is anecdotal.
- OSTI 1996 efficiency figures are modeled on one 15-year-old unit, not measured fleet data.
- NYSERDA's fact sheet addresses air-source heat pumps; the 120%/150% thresholds are applied here to cooling sizing generally.
