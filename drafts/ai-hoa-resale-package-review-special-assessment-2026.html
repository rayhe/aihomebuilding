Nobody Reads the 400-Page HOA Package. The AI Did, and Found the $9,200 Assessment.

By Catherine Chen · September 7, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Eleven days into escrow, the resale package lands in your inbox: four hundred twelve pages of CC&Rs, bylaws, the budget, two years of meeting minutes, the master insurance policy, and a reserve study last updated when your agent was in college, with six days left on your inspection contingency, which is why nobody reads it.

That is the system working as designed rather than as intended. Every state with a serious disclosure regime assumes a careful buyer sitting down with the documents, and every state wrote that assumption for a buyer who does not have a full-time job, a 17-day close, and a toddler. Into this gap, which is roughly the size of the American housing market, has walked the document-reading AI.

The Law Requires the Package, but It Does Not Require You to Survive Reading It

Start with what the seller actually owes you, because the AI is only useful once you know what should be in the pile. In California, Civil Code §4525 requires the owner of a home in a common interest development to hand the buyer the association's governing documents, financials, and disclosures. Illinois is more specific and more honest about the economics: under 765 ILCS 605/22.1, the association must produce the declaration, bylaws, rules, lien statements, anticipated capital expenditures for the current and next two fiscal years, reserve fund status, pending lawsuits, and insurance coverage within ten business days, for a fee capped at $375 plus $100 for 72-hour rush, and if you miss the lien statement you can buy a unit whose assessment lien survives your mortgage being current, because liens foreclose and the statute is not being poetic about it.

Florida, rearranged by the Surfside collapse, now runs the strictest regime in the country. HB 913, effective July 2025, requires milestone structural inspections and Structural Integrity Reserve Studies for buildings with three or more habitable stories, and reserves for structural items can no longer be waived by owner vote, which for decades was the mechanism by which every Florida board kept dues low and left the future to someone else. As of January 1, 2026, HB 1021 requires associations with 25 or more units to post governing documents, budgets, and reserve studies on a website or app, which is sunlight, finally, with a URL attached.

The Math of Not Reading Four Hundred Pages

The Foundation for Community Association Research, the research arm of the Community Associations Institute, published its 2025 Statistical Review this spring. Those numbers are difficult to look away from: 373,000 community associations in the United States, 78.1 million residents, 29.6 million housing units, $124.2 billion in annual assessments, of which $31.1 billion is reserved for long-term capital work. Homes in associations carry a combined market value of $13.1 trillion. And 81.0 percent of homes sold in 2025 sat inside a community association. Four out of five buyers sign up for a private government with taxing authority and a 400-page constitution, and most of them skim it.

Here is what the skimming costs, measured in the Venice and Sarasota County market: in that market, median annual condo fees hit $7,380 per unit, up 19 percent year over year, and buildings that completed their reserve studies early averaged 27 percent fee increases, while buildings that did not face projected one-time assessments averaging $9,200 per unit. In older coastal buildings where salt air has been chewing the concrete for decades, surprise assessments run $10,000 to over $100,000 per unit, and many owners have watched monthly housing costs double in under two years. Meeting minutes always discuss the assessment months before it appears in the budget, on page 217, which nobody reads.

Screening the full resale package with AI costs under $400. That is a 23-to-1 asymmetry, and it is the entire argument for doing the reading.

Run the asymmetry yourself. Illinois caps the package at $375, a capable AI subscription runs about $20 a month, and the total screening cost lands under $400, against a risk that averages $9,200 a unit in one Florida market and reaches six figures on the coast. I will also offer, with the caveat attached below, a piece of arithmetic nobody at CAI published: $31.1 billion reserved nationally against $13.1 trillion in association home values is 0.24 percent, a ratio that mixes an annual contribution figure with a stock of market value and is therefore an intuition pump rather than a funding adequacy measure, labeled as mine rather than theirs. Still, stare at 0.24 percent for a moment, because that is the national posture toward the roofs, pipes, and concrete holding up $13.1 trillion in housing.

The Machines Read Page 217 in Four Minutes

This is the precise step where AI document tools have set up shop, and unlike most construction-tech pitches, the value proposition is almost insultingly simple: the machine reads all of it, fast, and remembers what it read. In September 2026, property management platform ManageCasa expanded its agentic AI, Minii, which finds requirements inside governing documents, processes architectural review requests against the actual CC&Rs, and, notably, keeps each community's documents isolated instead of pooling them into a general chatbot. That privacy architecture is the product as much as the reading is. Your HOA's financials should not become training data.

Meanwhile the cruder version is already in buyers' hands. Inman reported last fall on agents feeding entire disclosure packages to general AI tools with prompts like: act as a real estate agent, analyze this package thoroughly, identify all material issues and seller admissions, cross-reference details across documents, flag risks and inconsistencies. What the AI is genuinely good at is extraction at scale, and the cost of that extraction is $20 a month. It will find every mention of "special assessment" across two years of minutes, which matters because assessments are debated in minutes long before they reach the budget. It will compare the reserve study's recommended funding against actual reserves and tell you which components sit under 70 percent funded. It will surface the pending litigation the seller's summary somehow forgot. It does in four minutes what a careful human does in four evenings, assuming the careful human exists, which the contingency calendar says they do not.

Six Prompts Worth More Than the Subscription

If you are buying into an association this year, here is the actionable core of this article. Feed the resale package to a document-capable AI and ask for these six things, in this order:

  1. Every mention of special assessments, whether proposed, approved, or merely discussed. Search the minutes, not just the financials, because the money is always debated before it is billed.
  2. Reserve funding versus the reserve study. What percentage of the recommended level is actually funded, and which components (roof, plumbing, elevators, concrete) are under 70 percent?
  3. Litigation and claims: pending suits, threatened suits, and insurance claims history. Active litigation can also kill your financing, since lenders decline projects with certain suits open.
  4. Use restrictions that collide with your life. Rental caps, pet limits, ADU prohibitions, home-business rules, parking assignments. Your plans mean nothing to the CC&Rs.
  5. Insurance, both sides: what the master policy covers, the deductible size (post-2024 Florida deductibles are the silent budget killer), and what you must carry yourself on an HO-6.
  6. Cross-reference the story: do the minutes discuss projects the budget does not fund? That gap has a name, and it is called your future special assessment.

Redfin's closing checklist covers the same ground for the manual reader: resale certificate, budget and reserve levels, minutes, CC&Rs, master policy. With AI doing the reading, it might actually get done before the contingency expires.

The Objection Against AI Review, Stated at Full Strength

Now the strongest case against all of this, stated without the usual hedging. An AI summary of a legal instrument is not legal advice, and a confident hallucination about an assessment clause that does not exist is worse than no summary at all. Inman, the same publication teaching agents these prompts, warns in the same breath that AI fabricates and that complex issues must go to the broker. Worst of all, the documents where precision matters most are often the worst inputs: decades-old CC&Rs run through a bad scanner, OCR chewing through the defined terms. And the deepest flaw is not in the reading but in the delivery. For most buyers the bottleneck is not reading speed but access. California management companies routinely take a week or more to assemble the package. Illinois law gives the association ten business days. Many contingency periods are shorter than the document pipeline, which means the AI can read instantly and still receive the package on day twelve of a ten-day contingency. A fast reader does not fix a slow bureaucracy.

There is also the privacy problem the vendors would prefer you not price in. Uploading your community's governing documents and financials to a general-purpose chatbot means your data goes where its data goes. ManageCasa's per-community isolation is marketed as the answer, which is exactly what you would expect the vendor to say, and no independent accuracy or security audit of these tools exists that I could find, though I looked.

What This Article Did Not Prove and Cannot Prove Yet

The limitations, stated plainly. I did not test any AI tool against a real resale package, so every capability claim above is vendor assertion or trade-press reporting, not measurement. Note that the Florida cost figures are regional reporting from the Venice, Sarasota, and Miami markets, not national data; assessment sizes vary enormously with building age, coastal proximity, and state law. My 0.24 percent reserve ratio is arithmetic on CAI's published totals, not a CAI metric, and it should not be quoted as one. This article covers three states' disclosure regimes in depth; the other forty-seven have their own, and nothing here is fifty-state advice. And the $9,200 figure that anchors the headline is an average of projections for non-compliant buildings in one market, which means your building's number could be zero or could be a second mortgage.

What to Do With This Before Your Contingency Expires

If you are buying into an association: order the resale package on day one of escrow, not day nine. Run the six prompts above the day it arrives. Then verify every material flag against the source page yourself, because the AI is a metal detector, not an appraiser. In California, match the document delivery date to your purchase agreement's review clock, since §4525 does not hand you a universal cancellation period. In Illinois, know the $375 cap and the $100 rush option, and use the rush option; $100 to protect a six-figure purchase is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy. In Florida, pull the milestone inspection report and the reserve study before you go firm, and if the building has 25 or more units, check the association's new mandatory website first, because as of this year, it is the law.

If you are selling: assemble the package before listing. Buyers with AI assistance will find the assessment in your minutes whether you disclose it gracefully or they discover it adversarially, and only one of those paths closes on time.

Your schedule means nothing to the code, and it never has. For the first time, something that reads faster than the bureaucracy moves is sitting on the buyer's side of the table, and the rational response is to let it read, then read the flagged pages yourself, because page 217 is waiting and it has always been waiting.
