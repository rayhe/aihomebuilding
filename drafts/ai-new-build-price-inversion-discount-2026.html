New Homes Just Sold for $25,000 Less Than Used Ones. Most of the Discount Is a Mortgage Trick.

By Marcus Washington · September 10, 2026

Saturday morning in a model home outside Phoenix, and the sales counselor is doing something she would not have done three years ago. She is apologizing for the price, not explaining it, and then sliding a sheet across the granite island that shows a monthly payment lower than the resale down the street, even though the sticker on the wall says otherwise.

That sheet is the whole story of American housing in 2026.

For the first time in the modern record, new homes are selling at a discount to used ones. In the second quarter of this year, the median new single-family home sold for $410,700, while the median existing home sold for $435,700. Read that again: the new house cost $25,000 less than the used one, the largest gap ever recorded in either direction, according to NAHB analysis of Census and National Association of Realtors data. Existing homes have now outsold new ones in six of the last nine quarters, a reversal that began in mid-2024 and has not let up.

Autopsy of a dead premium

Buyers over forty remember the old rule the way they remember landlines: new construction cost more, usually 10 to 17 percent more, because it was new, and new meant modern floor plans, current energy code, a builder warranty, and nobody else’s carpet. The premium was so reliable that appraisers, lenders, and every automated valuation model in the country baked it into their math as a near-constant of the market.

Seven years of premium, gone in eighteen months, and the speed of the collapse is the part the industry still has not priced in. New-home prices are down 4.7 percent year over year while existing prices keep grinding upward on lean supply. Builders are sitting on 8.5 months of inventory, more than double the existing-home market’s 4.4 months, and they are behaving exactly the way you would expect a business with warehouses full of depreciating product to behave, which is why May brought base-price cuts from nearly a third of builders and incentives from three in five, the industry’s standard playbook for moving product that gets cheaper the longer it sits unsold. In May, 32 percent of builders cut base prices by an average of 6 percent, and 61 percent offered incentives, according to NAHB survey data.

Follow the money into the earnings reports and the discount gets a dollar figure. Lennar, the country’s second-largest builder, watched its average selling price fall from $430,000 to $386,000 in a single year while its homebuilding gross margin compressed from 22.1 percent to 17.0 percent. Five hundred basis points of margin, handed back to buyers to keep the starts moving. D.R. Horton, the largest builder in America, gave back 130 basis points to land at 22.1 percent and told investors to expect worse. These are not charities. When a builder surrenders five points of margin, it is because the alternative is worse.

Three discounts wearing a trench coat

That $25,000 gap is real money, and it is also three different things pretending to be one. Pull them apart and the bargain changes shape.

First, composition. Builders did not just cut prices; they changed the product. Smaller homes, smaller lots, and a geographic drift toward cheaper Southern and exurban markets where land and labor cost less, which NAHB’s own economists flag as the primary driver of the median shift. A 1,700-square-foot box in a far suburb was never going to price like a 2,400-square-foot resale near the job center. Part of the “discount” is a different house. Not a cheaper one.

Second, the mortgage trick, which is the part the sales counselor slides across the island. Most builder incentives are not price cuts. They are rate buydowns: the builder pays to shave a point or more off your mortgage rate for the first years of the loan, or for its full term. Run the math on a $400,000 loan at today’s roughly 6.23 percent thirty-year rate versus a bought-down 5.23 percent. Principal and interest runs about $2,456 a month at the market rate and about $2,207 at the buydown rate, a difference of roughly $249 a month, or just under $3,000 a year. Capitalize that over a typical seven-year hold at a 6 percent discount rate and the buydown is worth about $16,700 in present value, which is real savings while you live there.

It is also savings you cannot sell, because when you list that house in five or seven years, the buydown does not transfer. The next buyer gets a market rate and a house that is no longer new, appraised against resale comps in a market where the historical premium may well have reasserted itself. Somewhere between a third and two-thirds of the apparent discount evaporates the day you become the seller instead of the buyer.

Third, genuine price cuts, the smallest of the three. About a third of builders actually lowered base prices, by about 6 percent on average. On a $410,000 home that is roughly $24,600, except it applies to a shrinking share of communities, and it lands on the smaller, farther-out product described above.

The machines learned the old market

Now consider who else is doing math on your house. Automated valuation models, the Zestimates and their institutional cousins, ingest hundreds of millions of transactions and learn the relationships that held for decades. One of those relationships was that newer construction commands a premium. The models learned it the way a child learns that stoves are hot: from overwhelming, repeated evidence.

Evidence that no longer describes the market. When transaction medians say new sells at a 5 percent discount while a model’s training data whispers that new should carry a premium, the model and the market disagree, and there is no clean way to referee the fight, because appraisers are stuck in the same trap from the opposite side. A human appraiser valuing your discounted new build cannot find comparable new-build sales at the discounted prices, since builders guard transaction data and incentives rarely appear in public records. So the appraisal leans on resale comps, which reflect the old premium the market just abandoned, and deals die in that gap. Buyers who did everything right watch their financing wobble because the valuation stack, human and machine alike, was trained on a world that ended in 2024.

No published study has yet measured AVM error specifically on new construction during this inversion, and this article does not pretend otherwise. The argument is structural: models dominated by resale training data will lag a regime change in new-build pricing, in the same way they lagged every other regime change, and the lag is the story.

The uncomfortable part

Here is what the decomposition is really telling you, and it cuts both ways. If you buy the new build at the discounted median, you are not buying a $25,000 cheaper house. You are buying a smaller house, in a cheaper location, with a subsidized loan attached. Strip out the buydown and the composition shift and the true apples-to-apples discount is modest, possibly zero in markets where builders are not cutting base prices at all.

But the reverse is also true, and any honest accounting has to say it: the discount spends the same. A buyer who pays less per month, gets a builder warranty, builds to the current energy code, and skips the bidding war has gotten a genuinely good deal, whatever the statisticians say about medians. If you hold for ten years, the buydown amortizes into real money and the newness decay washes out in general appreciation. If builders cut starts far enough, and they will, inventory normalizes and the historical premium comes back, which means today’s buyers bought the dip. Talking a buyer out of that deal with a clever decomposition would be malpractice dressed as analysis, and no statistician gets to spend your mortgage payment for you.

The sharper question is who this market is built for. Public builders with 600,000 controlled lots and 17 percent margins can subsidize your mortgage all day, because a buydown worth roughly $17,000 in present value keeps a $400,000 closing on this quarter’s books, while the framing crew on that site cannot subsidize anything, which is why the discount flows in one direction only, from shareholder margin to buyer payment, never to wages. The inversion is a transfer, from builder margin to buyer monthly payment, engineered to keep production lines moving. It works, right up until the starts get cut and the next cycle’s buyers discover the premium is back. Somebody always pays.

Buyer’s checklist: separating price cuts from theater

  • Demand two sheets. Ask for the base-price sheet and the incentive sheet separately. If the “discount” lives entirely on the incentive sheet, it is financing, not price.
  • Price the buydown yourself. Take the bought-down payment, subtract it from the market-rate payment, multiply by the months you will actually stay. That is the real value. Anything beyond year seven is optimism.
  • Compare dollars per square foot against resale comps in the same zip code, not against the metro median. The national inversion is a compositional artifact; your zip code is the market.
  • Ask what the incentives did to the appraisal. If the builder’s price only works with the buydown, confirm with your lender that the appraisal will support the contract price without it.
  • Check the builder’s inventory age. Standing inventory older than six months means deeper cuts are coming. In May, nearly a third of builders were already cutting.

What this article did not prove

Median-to-median comparisons are not apples-to-apples, and NAHB says so explicitly: the national inversion is a regional and compositional phenomenon, and your market may look nothing like it. Realtor.com’s March 2026 data shows urban infill new construction still commanding triple-digit premiums over existing homes in several Florida and Midwest metros, which means the inversion is a suburban Sun Belt story wearing national clothes. The buydown present-value math assumes a seven-year hold, a 6 percent discount rate, and a full one-point buydown; actual structures vary from 2-1 temporary buydowns to permanent ones, and the value moves with them. Builder margin figures come from public companies and may not describe private builders. And the valuation-model argument is structural reasoning about training-data composition, not a measured error rate, though somebody should measure it.
