Construction Workers Die of Heat at Six Times the National Rate. A Wearable Predicts It. Your Builder Won’t Buy One.

By Marcus Washington · July 27, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Last July, a framing crew in Phoenix started a shift at 6 a.m. to beat the afternoon heat. By 10:30, the heat index had crossed 105°F. One worker, 23 years old and in his second week on the job, sat down on a stack of plywood and did not stand up again. He had been on the site for four hours. His supervisor was inside the model home, on a phone call. Nobody had checked on the crew since the morning toolbox talk, which lasted two minutes and did not mention hydration.

He survived, barely, and only because someone on the adjacent lot saw him slumping and called 911 before the seizures started. Forty-five minutes before he collapsed, his core body temperature had risen past the threshold where the brain begins losing its ability to regulate itself, a trajectory that a $300 armband strapped to his forearm would have detected, flagged, and interrupted by vibrating on his wrist and pushing an alert to his supervisor’s phone telling him to stop the crew, get the kid into shade, and wait for the device to clear him back to work.

He was not wearing one, and almost nobody on a residential jobsite in America is, which is the point of this story and the reason 559 workers a year never come home from a shift that started before dawn.

Six Percent of the Workforce, Thirty-Six Percent of the Deaths

Construction workers make up roughly 6% of the American labor force. They account for 36% of all heat-related workplace deaths, a disproportion so extreme that if any other demographic group died at that rate from a single cause, there would be a federal task force and a congressional hearing by now. Instead, there is a proposed OSHA rule that has been sitting on a desk for two years, a National Emphasis Program on heat that quietly expired in April 2026, and a construction industry that treats hydration as a personal responsibility rather than a systems problem.

OSHA’s own analysis, submitted during the rulemaking process, estimates 559 workers die from heat every year and 24,656 suffer heat-related illness, and the proposed standard, OSHA calculated, could prevent 531 of those 559 deaths, a 96% reduction that remains theoretical because the rule that would produce it has not moved forward.

Half of heat-related deaths occur on a worker’s first day, and over 70% happen within the first week of employment, which means these are not experienced workers pushing through a bad afternoon but new hires whose bodies have not acclimatized, on jobsites that do not have acclimatization protocols, supervised by foremen who may not be trained to recognize heat exhaustion before it becomes heat stroke.

What the Armbands Actually Do

Two companies dominate the commercial wearable heat-monitoring space: Kenzen and SlateSafety. Both sell arm-worn devices that continuously measure skin temperature, heart rate, and ambient conditions, then fuse those signals into a real-time heat strain index.

Kenzen’s system uses photoplethysmography (PPG), infrared skin temperature, and ambient temperature sensors, and when the device’s algorithm detects that core body temperature is rising toward danger, it vibrates on the worker’s arm and pushes an alert to a supervisor dashboard. A second alert fires when the worker has cooled enough to resume work safely, a closed-loop intervention that requires no judgment call from the supervisor about whether the worker “looks okay.” “Heat-related injuries are 100% preventable,” said Heidi Lehmann, Kenzen’s chief commercialization officer, and the device is sold as a subscription, per worker, per month.

SlateSafety takes a similar approach but indexes against a metric called Heart Rate Limit Value, or HRLV, which compares a worker’s current heart rate against their maximum sustainable rate. If HRLV exceeds 100% for more than five minutes, the system triggers an intervention. At the U.S. Department of Energy’s Savannah River Site in South Carolina, supervisors pull workers out of the field when that threshold is crossed, no argument, no negotiation.

That is the Department of Energy, a federal agency running a nuclear waste facility that decided the $200-to-$800-per-device cost was worth it to keep its workers alive and functional through a South Carolina summer, where heat indices routinely exceed 110°F from June through September. The device works, it is deployed on federal worksites, and the people wearing it go home at the end of the day because of it.

Where They Are Not

Residential construction, almost entirely.

Adoption patterns for heat-monitoring wearables mirror every other construction safety technology: heavy industrial first, commercial second, residential never. Oil and gas companies adopted Kenzen, mining operations piloted SlateSafety, and DOE facilities mandated deployment as a condition of field work. Large commercial general contractors are evaluating it through pilot programs tied to insurance incentives.

Homebuilders are not. A construction wearable technology market projected to reach $7.3 billion by 2030 is growing fast, but growth concentrates in exactly the segments where heat deaths are already declining. Residential construction, where the problem is worst, is where adoption is lowest.

There are structural reasons for this gap, and they explain why residential construction consistently lags every other segment in safety technology adoption. A Turner or Skanska commercial jobsite has a safety director, a site superintendent, and a compliance infrastructure that can absorb a new monitoring tool. A residential subdivision has a project manager overseeing six to twelve subcontractor crews across multiple lots, and the subcontractors themselves are often small operations with five to fifteen workers, no safety officer, no dedicated compliance staff, and a margin structure that treats every dollar not spent on materials or labor as a dollar lost.

The math is not complicated. A crew of eight roofers working a residential subdivision in July. Eight armbands at $200 each is $1,600 in hardware, plus the subscription. A mid-range single-family home in the same subdivision sells for $350,000. That $1,600 is 0.05% of the sale price. Less than the builder spends on the sales office cookies.

The Dirty Dozen

D.R. Horton built over 90,000 homes in fiscal 2025, more than any other homebuilder in America. In April 2026, the National Council for Occupational Safety and Health put them on its 2026 Dirty Dozen list, an annual compilation of companies that, in COSH’s assessment, “put workers’ lives at risk through unsafe practices, inadequate protections, and systemic neglect.”

OSHA’s own inspection database tells a bleaker story than the Dirty Dozen designation alone. D.R. Horton has accumulated repeat citations for fall protection violations across Pennsylvania, Georgia, Texas, Florida, Virginia, and California. The pattern repeats across states: subcontractors performing roofing or sheeting work at heights of 20 to 29 feet, without guardrails, without personal fall arrest systems, without safety nets. In one Florida inspection, workers were found without hard hats and operating powered nail guns without eye protection, and the associated fines ranged from $4,050 to $14,502 per violation.

To put those numbers in context: a single workers’ compensation claim for a serious heat illness runs $10,000 to $50,000. A wrongful death settlement in construction averages well over $1 million. OSHA’s maximum penalty for a serious violation, as of 2026, is $16,131. For a company that generated $36.8 billion in revenue last fiscal year, these fines are not enforcement. They are an invoice.

The Federal Rule That Stalled

On August 30, 2024, OSHA published a proposed rule titled “Heat Injury and Illness Prevention in Outdoor and Indoor Work Settings.” It would have required employers to monitor heat conditions, provide mandatory rest breaks at a heat index of 80°F, and implement acclimatization protocols for new and returning workers. Public comment closed in January 2025. As of June 2026, no further steps toward finalization have been taken. OSHA’s most recent unified regulatory agenda did not include a target date for the rule. Legal analysts at Ogletree Deakins assessed its path to finalization under the current administration as “unlikely in the near term.”

Meanwhile, the Heat National Emphasis Program, which gave OSHA the authority to proactively inspect workplaces when the heat index exceeded 80°F without waiting for a complaint or an injury, expired on April 8, 2026. Whether the current administration will extend, replace, or simply let it lapse has not been publicly addressed.

Without a federal standard, heat protection is a patchwork. California, Washington, Oregon, Minnesota, and Colorado have state-level heat rules. Texas passed a law in 2023 that preempted local heat ordinances, effectively overriding a Dallas ordinance and an Austin ordinance that required water and rest breaks for outdoor workers. In states without protections, the only enforcement mechanism is the OSH Act’s General Duty Clause, a broad provision that requires employers to provide a workplace “free from recognized hazards.” Between 2022 and 2026, OSHA issued 60 citations under the General Duty Clause for heat-related violations across the entire country, a number so small relative to the scale of the problem that it functions less as enforcement and more as an acknowledgment that the enforcement apparatus technically exists.

What the Research Says Is Coming

The next generation of heat-monitoring wearables is smaller, cheaper, and does not require a cloud connection. A 2026 study published in MDPI’s Future Internet journal demonstrated a proof-of-concept ear-level wearable running on a Raspberry Pi Pico microcontroller, roughly the size of a hearing aid, that combines PPG, non-contact infrared thermometry, and nine-axis inertial sensing. It classifies three states directly relevant to worker safety: elevated PPG variability, drowsiness, and fatigue. In controlled testing on unseen subjects, it achieved an average F1-score of 97.80% with sub-second inference latency, all processed locally on the device without any cloud dependency.

Components for that device cost under $25 at hobbyist quantities. At production volumes, the bill of materials would be lower. The authors noted that field validation in operational construction environments has not yet been conducted, a limitation they acknowledged directly, but what matters is not whether the research device itself is ready for deployment. What matters is that the engineering problem of detecting heat strain on a worker’s body, in real time, with high accuracy, at low cost, has been solved, and the barrier to adoption has not been technological for at least three years.

The Insurance Angle Nobody Mentions

Insurance carriers are beginning to offer premium reductions for contractors that deploy wearable safety systems, driven by actuarial logic rather than altruism: fewer heat injuries mean fewer claims, lower reserves, and better loss ratios. For large commercial contractors carrying $10 million or more in annual workers’ comp premiums, even a 5% reduction pays for the entire wearable deployment several times over.

For residential subcontractors, the economics should be even more compelling. A roofing crew of six to eight workers generates disproportionate heat claims relative to its size because the work is inherently exposed, physically demanding, and often performed without shade structures or scheduled rest. A single heat stroke claim that results in an ER visit, lost-time injury, and elevated experience modification rate can raise a small contractor’s workers’ comp premiums by 15% to 25% for three years. That premium increase almost certainly exceeds the cost of eight armbands for a season.

Nobody is making this case to residential subcontractors because nobody is marketing heat wearables to residential subcontractors. Kenzen’s customer list includes large industrial and energy companies, SlateSafety is deployed at federal facilities, and the construction wearable market is growing at double digits annually in the direction of companies that already have safety infrastructure, not toward the jobsites where workers are actually dying.

What This Means for You

If you are a general contractor running residential subdivisions: ask your insurer whether deploying heat-monitoring wearables on your roofing and framing subs would reduce your experience modification rate. Run the math on one heat stroke ER visit against the cost of eight SlateSafety armbands for a summer. If the wearables cost less than one claim, which they will, the decision is arithmetic, not policy.

If you are a homebuyer closing on a new-build in a market where summer temperatures routinely exceed 90°F: you are benefiting from the labor of workers who are not protected by a federal heat standard, not monitored by available technology, and not supervised under acclimatization protocols that OSHA itself has said could prevent 96% of heat deaths. Changing that costs less per home than what the builder spent on your granite countertop edge upgrade.

And if you are one of the 559, you already know what a 23-year-old roofer on his second week discovers when the heat builds slowly and then all at once: by the time you realize it has gotten you, you are sitting on a stack of plywood with shaking hands and white sky and someone yelling your name from very far away, and a device the size of a watch that costs less than lunch would have stopped it forty-five minutes earlier.

Limitations

This analysis cites OSHA’s own estimate of 559 annual heat fatalities, which includes all industries, not construction alone. Construction’s 36% share implies roughly 201 construction heat deaths per year, but that number is derived from BLS Census of Fatal Occupational Injuries data that almost certainly undercounts heat-related deaths, particularly in residential construction where many workers are classified as independent contractors and deaths may be attributed to cardiac arrest rather than heat. Kenzen and SlateSafety’s per-worker subscription pricing is not publicly disclosed; our cost estimates use the $200-to-$800 per-device range reported by ConstructionPlacements and confirmed by industry sources, but actual total cost of ownership including subscriptions may be higher. That 97.80% F1-score for the MDPI ear-level device was achieved under controlled laboratory conditions on a small sample of the WESAD dataset, not on construction workers in operational heat environments, and field performance may differ substantially.
