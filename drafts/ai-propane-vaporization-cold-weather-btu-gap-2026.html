Your Propane Gauge Reads 20%. At Zero Degrees, Your Furnace Is Already Starving.

By Jake Kowalski · October 6, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

It is 2 a.m. in northern Vermont and eight below zero. Your furnace fires, runs ninety seconds, and locks out. You reset it. It fires, runs ninety seconds, locks out again. By morning the house is 48 degrees and the service tech is standing in your driveway, shrugging. He checks the furnace. Clean flame sensor, good ignitor, no fault codes that mean anything. Then he walks out to the tank, raps it with his knuckles, and reads the gauge.

Twenty percent: eighty gallons of propane sitting right there.

"Furnace is fine," he says. "Call your propane guy."

Your propane guy already knows what happened before you finish the sentence. Your tank did not run out of fuel. It ran out of boil.

Propane Does Not Flow. It Boils.

Everybody pictures the tank like a gas can: tip it and fuel pours out. Wrong machine, because a propane tank is a kettle. Liquid propane sits in the bottom, and your house drinks only the vapor boiling off the top. Your furnace flame feeds on evaporation, which has a speed limit set by two things: how much liquid surface is exposed, and how warm the steel is.

Drain the tank and the liquid surface shrinks. Drop the temperature and the boil slows. Do both at once, a quarter-full tank on a zero-degree night, and the boil rate falls off a cliff. That cliff exists in print: the industry publishes it as a table, and I have never seen a propane dealer hand it to a homeowner, which is strange, because it is the single most important spec of the entire system.

For a standard 500-gallon ASME tank, the vaporization rate in BTU per hour looks like this, from the industry-standard vaporization tables:

The Crossover Nobody Calculates

Now put a house on the other end of the pipe. A typical rural setup: 100,000 BTU furnace, 40,000 BTU storage water heater, 65,000 BTU range. Nameplate total: 205,000 BTU an hour if everything fires at once. A normal winter evening with the furnace and the water heater running together: 140,000.

Cross those loads against the table and the failure points draw themselves:

0°F at 20% A 500-gallon tank with 80 gallons left vaporizes 113,750 BTU/hr against a furnace plus water heater wanting 140,000, so the tank loses, and every gallon below 40% fill is unusable for full load at zero degrees.

At 10 degrees and 20% fill, the tank makes 182,000 BTU an hour, which covers the furnace and water heater but folds the moment somebody lights the range. At zero and 20%, even the furnace-plus-water-heater combo outruns it. At minus ten and 20%, the tank produces 59,150 BTU an hour against a 100,000 BTU furnace running alone, which is the 2 a.m. lockout from the cold open: eighty gallons of fuel in the yard, a furnace that cannot stay lit, and a service invoice for a machine that was never broken.

Flip it around and the table becomes a sizing tool. At zero degrees you need the tank at 40% or better to carry a normal evening load. Forty percent of a 500-gallon tank, filled to its legal 80% maximum, is 160 gallons. So here is the sentence your dealer will never put in the brochure: in a real cold snap, your 500-gallon tank is a 160-gallon tank. The other 240 gallons are decoration until April.

Five Percent of America Lives on This Math

This is not a niche curiosity. About 5 percent of US households heat primarily with propane, per the EIA's Winter Fuels Outlook, and the share tops 14 percent across Vermont, New Hampshire, South Dakota, North Dakota, and Montana. A typical propane household spends roughly $1,300 a winter. January 2025 pushed US propane consumption to 1.48 million barrels a day, an 18-year record and the coldest January since 2014. That month is exactly when quarter-full tanks across the upper Midwest started losing the boil race.

Dealer sizing guidance has not caught up. Ask any dealer and you will hear the standard advice, repeated across the industry: a 500-gallon tank is the minimum for whole-home heating and a 1,000-gallon tank suits large homes. Both statements are about gallons and delivery convenience. Neither mentions that the 1,000-gallon tank roughly doubles every number in the vaporization table: 477,350 BTU an hour at 20 degrees and 20% fill. You are not buying fewer deliveries. You are buying vaporization headroom, which is the thing that actually keeps the furnace lit in February. Size for January boil, not for delivery-day convenience.

The $150 Sensor That Sees It Coming

Here is the good news: the information problem is solved and cheap. That gauge on your tank is a float and a dial, 1950s technology you have to walk outside to read. Its replacement is an ultrasonic sensor the size of a hockey puck that sticks to the tank bottom with magnets and reads the liquid level to a hundredth of an inch.

Mopeka's Pro line does exactly this at 99.5% claimed accuracy, with an integrated temperature sensor, configurable low-level alarms, a five-year battery, and a WiFi bridge for remote checking, covering tanks up to 2,000 gallons. Generac's Tank Utility monitor runs $199.99 MSRP with a phone app, consumption tracking, and low-fuel alerts, and Generac's 4G LTE monitors now feed tank levels straight to ecobee smart thermostats, so the fuel gauge sits on the thermostat screen next to the room temperature. Dealers have built autofill programs on cellular monitors: they watch your level, model your burn against degree days, and roll the truck before you run dry.

All of that is pointed at the wrong finish line. Every one of these systems answers "when will I run out." None of them answers "when will I starve with fuel left," which is the failure this article is about, and it is a strictly easier computation: live fill percentage from the sensor, the seven-day forecast low from any weather API, and the vaporization table as a lookup. What comes out is a push notification reading something like: "At 24% with a forecast low of 4°F Friday, your tank sustains your furnace and water heater down to −2°F. Schedule a fill."

Sensor companies sell hardware and dealers sell gallons, and the gap between them is a lookup table plus a weather API, which means the whole product is about a weekend of work for whoever gets there first. Until then, you can run the math yourself with the table above and a thermometer.

Objections, at Full Strength

Fair critique first: most propane homes never touch this failure zone. A 500-gallon tank kept above 40% through winter covers the design temperatures of nearly every propane-heavy state, and the disaster needs three simultaneous ingredients, single-digit cold plus low fill plus heavy simultaneous draw. This is a tail-risk story. Treat it like one, not like your tank is a time bomb.

Second, the tables are nominal: real vaporization moves with wind exposure across the tank shell, frost jacketing that insulates the steel from the air, and the condition of the regulators. A furnace starving at 25% fill on a cold night is as likely to be an iced-up regulator or an undersized second-stage gas line as it is to be vaporization physics. Use the table as a planning tool, not a diagnostic oracle, and check the regulator for ice before you blame thermodynamics.

Third, underground tanks rewrite the numbers. Below the frost line, soil sits near 50°F year-round in much of the country, so a buried 500-gallon tank can out-boil an aboveground one on a zero-degree night. Buried tanks recover slower and cannot be cheaply upsized, so the tradeoff is real, but do not universalize the aboveground table.

Fourth, a sensor does not add a single BTU. A $150 puck tells you sooner; it does not change the physics. Real fixes are behavioral (keep-fill discipline) or capital (a bigger tank, or manifolded twin 500s, which double the wetted surface area and are easier to deliver to than a single 1,000). Do not buy the gadget thinking you bought headroom.

Finally, the audience is specific. This means nothing in the South, nothing on gas mains, nothing on heat pumps. It matters in propane country, rural, cold, the Vermont-to-the-Dakotas-to-the-mountain-West belt where the tank in the yard is the heating system. If that is not you, enjoy your gas bill and move along.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

That crossover table is my calculation, not a field study. I crossed published nominal vaporization rates against typical nameplate inputs (100K furnace, 40K water heater, 65K range), not a Manual J load calculation for any specific house, and nobody has published measured furnace-starvation events mapped to fill percentage and temperature. Vaporization figures come from a secondary reproduction of the industry-standard tables; the warm-weather rows in that reproduction show obvious OCR corruption, so I used only the internally consistent 20°F-to-minus-20°F rows, and the values are nominal for a 41-by-104-inch 500-gallon ASME tank, and your tank's geometry shifts them. EIA household-share figures come from the Winter Fuels Outlook cycle and describe direction, not this month. I did not verify current installed costs for tank upsizes or emergency delivery fees, so treat any dollar figure attached to those as hedged.

The Keep-Fill Rules

If you heat with propane, here is the operating manual the dealer should have stapled to your lease:

One. Never drop below 30% after November, and make it 40% if your January lows hit single digits. Set the monitor alert at 35%, not 20%, because at zero degrees 20% is already the danger zone, as the table shows.

Two. Size the next tank for vaporization, not gallons. If your design temperature is under 10°F, run your connected load against the table at 30% fill before the concrete pad is poured, and if it fails, spec the 1,000-gallon tank. The trench, the pad, and the gas line labor barely change; the tank delta is the cheapest insurance in the build.

Three. Buy the ultrasonic monitor and join the dealer's autofill program. One avoided emergency delivery, or one avoided frozen-pipe night at 2 a.m., pays for the sensor several times over.

Four. Ask about manifolded twin 500s, since two tanks double the wetted surface area, which doubles the boil rate, and a delivery driver would rather fill two 500s than wrestle one 1,000 in a tight yard.

Five. When the furnace short-cycles on a bitter night with fuel in the tank, check two numbers before you call for service: the fill percentage and the overnight low, run them against the vaporization curve, then check the regulator for ice. Then check the gas line sizing. The furnace is fourth on the list, which is why the tech in the cold open found nothing wrong with it. Nothing was wrong with the machine. The kettle just could not boil fast enough.
