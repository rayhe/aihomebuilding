One Saw Cut, One Cable, $56,000. The Scan Your Remodeler Never Ordered.

By Jake Kowalski · September 10, 2026

Picture this. Your contractor is adding a powder room off the garage, which means the plumber needs a single 4-inch core through the slab for the drain, a ten-minute job that has gone fine a thousand times before, and nobody in the chain pauses to ask what is inside the concrete because the plans are twenty years old and the original PT drawings vanished with the builder's filing cabinet. Once the saw fires up and bites in, it hits something that is not concrete. It is a steel cable holding roughly 30,000 pounds of tension, and it just let go inside your foundation.

That cable is called a post-tension tendon, and since the 1980s it has been the standard way to build slab-on-grade homes in expansive-soil country: Texas, Arizona, Nevada, California, Florida. Instead of a forest of rebar, the slab gets draped cables that are tensioned after the concrete cures, squeezing the whole foundation into compression, and it works beautifully until someone drills into it without looking first, which happens constantly.

Sever one tendon and the repair runs $20,000 to $30,000, according to the scanning firm GPRS, which handles these jobs commercially. Add delays and secondary damage and a trade guide citing 2026 industry data puts the all-in average at $56,000 per strike. GPRS also notes, with refreshing bluntness, that a severed cable can cause "structural failure that endangers the lives of not only those completing the work, but anyone in the immediate area," because thirty thousand pounds of steel does not release gently, a point a 1998 OSHA fatality report makes in grim detail, describing a worker struck and killed when a tensioned cable released during stressing work on a commercial parking ramp.

Now the punchline: what prevents all of this is a ground-penetrating radar scan that takes about an hour and costs between $250 and $1,500 per visit, according to Concrete Insight, a scanning outfit with no reason to lowball its own industry. A Portland scanning company charges $200 an hour with a two-hour minimum, a Maryland company runs $168.75 an hour with a four-hour minimum, so call it $400 to $675 for a typical residential visit, which is roughly the cost of the plumber's half-day.

On commercial jobs, scanning before coring is close to standard practice, because the owners carry the kind of liability budgets that make a $1,500 scan a rounding error. On residential remodels, it barely happens: no plumber's bid includes it, no GC asks for it, and most homeowners have never heard of it. Everybody's plan is hope.

Now the radar has a brain

Ground-penetrating radar has been around for decades, and reading a radargram used to be a specialist skill closer to interpreting an ultrasound, which is one reason residential contractors never bothered, and that is changing fast.

GSSI, the company that built the first commercial GPR system 45 years ago, just shipped an "Auto Drill" feature on its StructureScan Mini XT handheld scanner. You tap the planned core location and size on the touchscreen, and a specialized algorithm hunts for obstructions in that column of concrete automatically. That same update pushes the depth range to 24 inches while sharpening the image enough to resolve bundled cables that older units smeared into a single dot, which matters because PT tendons frequently travel in pairs.

IDS GeoRadar went further: its C-thrue system offers automatic rebar and void detection with augmented-reality visualization, so the operator watches the hidden steel overlay the real slab in real time instead of squinting at waveforms on a cart. And a 2025 Cairo University study published in Frontiers of Structural and Civil Engineering paired GPR with a YOLOv8 deep-learning model that classified rebar diameters at 97.2% accuracy on real building data, which is the difference between "there is steel here" and "there is a 16-millimeter bar here," computed automatically.

Why point-and-shoot scanning misses anyway

Here is the part nobody tells homeowners, and it is the most important paragraph in this article, so read it twice.

Post-tension cables do not run straight; they drape, riding high near beams and column lines and sagging low through the middle of every span, because that curve is what makes the physics work. A scan of the exact drill point therefore tells you nothing about what is happening twelve inches away, where the cable has dipped or climbed right into your planned cut.

Contractor guides already know this. Their number one listed scanning mistake is "scanning only the exact drill point instead of a buffer zone," with a recommended buffer of at least 12 inches in every direction around every cut. But homeowner-facing guidance never explains the why, and the why is the drape. If your scanning company only wants to scan the bullseye, hire a different scanning company. Hope is not a scanning protocol.

Do the math

Run the expected value, because it is almost embarrassing. Take a $400 scan against the vendor's $56,000 average strike cost: the scan pays for itself if it prevents one strike in 140 scans, a 0.7% base rate. Use the lower $20,000 repair figure and a $400 scan, and the break-even strike probability is 2%.

Nobody publishes the true residential strike rate, which is itself a scandal worth a paragraph, because the vendors who know the numbers sell scans and the contractors who skip them have no incentive to count their luck. With draped cables in unmarked residential slabs and a bullseye-only scanning culture, anyone who thinks their crew's strike probability sits below 2% is doing wishful thinking with a diamond blade.

One caveat before the GCs start quoting me to customers: the $56,000 figure comes from industry cost data via a scanning vendor, and vendors benefit from scary numbers. More conservative is the $20,000 to $30,000 replacement range. Either way, the conclusion survives.

What the vendors leave out of the ad

Now the skepticism, because a radar is not a magic X-ray and this publication is not a brochure.

A Maryland scanning company's liability waiver, which sits on their own website in plain view, states the quiet part at full volume: "GPR scanning can not distinguish the difference between a single rebar, conduit or post tension cable 100% of the time. It can only detect the center and approximate depth of targets." Interpretations, the waiver continues, "are opinions based on judgements," and the company accepts no liability if you rely on their data and hit something anyway. Read that twice.

GPRS's answer to this is 320 hours of field training and 80 hours of classroom instruction before a technician works solo, which tells you the operator is the instrument at least as much as the hardware, while wet or freshly poured concrete degrades the signal and patch repairs create irregular layouts that confuse the models. The AI research, impressive as it is, is trained largely on lab and bridge data and classifies rebar diameter, not the exact PT-versus-rebar discrimination problem a residential scan lives or dies on. A cheap scan by a green operator can mark a tendon as conduit and hand you false confidence with a signed report.

False positives cost money too: every anomaly flagged that turns out to be nothing still burns crew time and repositioning. The scan is the cheapest insurance in construction, but it is insurance, not certainty.

What to actually do

If your home was built after roughly 1980 in a Sun Belt market, assume you are standing on a post-tension slab until someone proves otherwise. Often the original PT drawings are sitting in your county's permit file, and they are free to pull.

Before anyone cores, cuts, or anchors into that slab, budget $250 to $1,500 for a GPR scan with a 12-inch buffer around every cut, photographed and kept in your project file. Ask the scanning company specifically how they distinguish tendons from rebar and conduit, what happens when their readings are ambiguous, and whether their written report carries any warranty at all. If a core drilling company tells you they do not require scans and do not want one, treat that as a disqualifier rather than a discount. Experienced drillers require it precisely because they have seen what the blade finds when nobody looked first.

Limitations

This analysis uses vendor-published cost data (GPRS via a 2026 trade guide) for the $56,000 average strike cost; no independent, non-vendor audit of residential PT strike repair costs exists in the sources reviewed. No published data was found on the frequency of residential post-tension cable strikes, so the expected-value argument uses break-even threshold analysis rather than a claimed base rate. GSSI Auto Drill, IDS C-thrue auto-detection, and the YOLOv8 97.2% figure are real shipping features and peer-reviewed research, but their accuracy on messy residential PT slabs with patch repairs and irregular layouts is not independently verified. What the OSHA fatality establishes is what released tendon tension does to a human body; it was a commercial tensioning accident, not a severed-cable strike. No national percentage for PT slab prevalence in residential construction was found, so none is claimed here, and any article that cites one is citing a guess dressed up as a statistic.
