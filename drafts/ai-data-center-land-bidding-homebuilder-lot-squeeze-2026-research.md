## Angle
Not the labor-drain story (covered 10+ times already: ai-data-center-labor-drain, data-center-electrician-drain, etc.). This is the LAND story: data center operators are the second-largest buyers of US development land in H1 2026, paying multiples no homebuilder can match, and the result is not more expensive homes — it is no homes at all (NAHB's framing).

## Original contribution (the math nobody published)
**Homes-not-built arithmetic.** Take the Loudoun County numbers at face value:
- Data center bid: $4,400,000/acre. County median land: $125,000/acre. Ratio: 35.2x.
- Typical suburban greenfield density: 4 homes/acre. Per-potential-home land cost at the data center bid: $1,100,000 per home site.
- A builder targeting a $600,000 home with land at 20% of delivered price ($120,000/lot) needs entitled land at ~$480,000/acre at 4 units/acre. The $4.4M bid is 9.2x the maximum a production builder can pay. The parcel's residential yield is therefore zero — NAHB's "no homes at all" is arithmetic, not rhetoric.
- North Las Vegas: 205 acres × 5 homes/acre ≈ 1,025 homes not built on that parcel alone.
**Second-order effect (appraisal contamination):** the $500,000/year option payments and $360K/acre outlier sales enter county comp databases. Appraisers valuing nearby residentially-zoned parcels see data-center-distorted comps; the appraisal gap then kills residential deals even on parcels no data center wants. Nobody in the trade press has connected the option-payment practice to residential appraisal failure.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Data centers are the best commercial neighbors a county fiscal office could ask for: enormous property-tax payments, near-zero service burden (no schoolchildren, minimal traffic, few emergency calls). Much of the land was farmland, not entitled residential — it was never going to be starter homes without rezoning, water, and sewer the county could not afford. The housing shortage is primarily an entitlement and zoning failure, not a bidding failure; blaming server farms lets cities off the hook for decades of exclusionary zoning. And moratoriums are performative at the state line: pause Loudoun and the campus goes to Frederick County. Several studies (Rochester) find no measurable home-price harm nearby.

## Limitations (honest accounting)
- The $4.4M/acre Loudoun figure is a single reported offer ("reportedly," per NAHB via Inman) — not a recorded sale; treat as directional.
- Avison Young's $6B H1 2026 figure covers all data center land buys; the share that was residentially-zoned or residentially-suitable is unknown and unknowable from public data.
- Density assumptions (4–5 homes/acre) are typical suburban greenfield; actual entitled density varies by jurisdiction.
- The appraisal-contamination mechanism is reasoned from the HousingWire option-payment anecdote, not from a statistical study of comp databases — flagged as analysis, not finding.
- Price-effect studies conflict (Rochester: no effect; George Mason: slowed growth) — reported both, picked neither.
