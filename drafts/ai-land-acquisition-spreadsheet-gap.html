D.R. Horton Deployed AI Across 30 States to Buy Land. Your Builder Is Still Using Spreadsheets.

By Frank DeLuca · July 28, 2026

A regional builder bought 25 acres and underwrote the project at a $475,000 price point, hired the engineers, opened the model. Three months later, a competitor launched an identical product less than a mile away, priced $50,000 lower. Assumptions collapsed overnight: slash prices and bleed margin, or hold the dirt while carrying costs compound at $8,000 a month and the sales office sits empty. An NAHB-hosted case study sponsored by Acres.com described the scenario and drew the obvious conclusion: the acquisition team had no visibility into competitor land strategies, no way to see the quiet purchases happening behind LLCs and shell entities on adjacent parcels, no systematic method for connecting the dots that would have flagged the collision before the first stake went into the ground.

That builder lost six figures, not because of the land itself but because of the process that led to it.

Who Moved First

In November 2025, D.R. Horton selected Prophetic, a Portland-based AI-native land acquisition platform, for an organization-wide deployment spanning more than 30 states. D.R. Horton is not a company that experiments casually. It is the largest homebuilder in the United States by volume, and its land pipeline is the engine that sustains every division, every community, every closing. When D.R. Horton changes how it buys dirt, the signal carries weight far beyond one press release.

By June 2026, M/I Homes followed with its own deployment, another publicly traded national builder, Columbus-based, rolling out the same platform across its single-family operations. Ron Frissora, M/I's chief information officer, stated the rationale plainly: "Being able to evaluate more sites, eliminate dead ends earlier, and focus our teams on the right opportunities has a direct impact on how we grow."

Meanwhile, Fayetteville-based Acres.com launched Acres Intelligence in May 2026, which it describes as the first AI agent built to work alongside land acquisition teams. In a vendor demonstration, founder Carter Malloy showed the system searching for vacant infill parcels suitable for luxury housing within a five-minute drive of an H-E-B grocery store in Texas, scoring each parcel by zoning, flood exposure, slope, and development suitability in real time, then assembling a rough investment committee packet with underwriting assumptions, development constraints, and risk flags. The kind of packet that takes an analyst team three days and a conference call nobody enjoys, finished in minutes.

What the Old Process Actually Costs

I have run land acquisition on residential projects for twenty years, and the process has not changed in any fundamental way during that time. A land opportunity surfaces through a broker call, a municipal meeting, a tip from an engineer who noticed a rezoning petition, or a county records search that someone ran on a Tuesday afternoon because they had a hunch about a corridor. What follows is a sequence so familiar every acquisition director can recite it from memory: drive the site, walk the dirt, talk to the neighbors, pull the tax records, check the flood maps, call the utility district, call the school district, call the planning department, then spend three days assembling a preliminary feasibility memo that gets presented to the investment committee, which asks six questions that require three more days of research, after which the committee either kills the deal or authorizes a letter of intent that begins a 90-to-120-day due diligence period during which the real surprises emerge. A Phase I environmental report flags a recognized environmental condition requiring a Phase II at $25,000. A geotech report reveals expansive clays that add $4,000 per lot to foundation costs. A traffic impact study triggers a road improvement obligation that the county neglected to mention during the pre-application conference.

Each dead deal costs real money, not just the staff time, which is substantial but difficult to isolate in most builders' accounting systems, but the hard costs that land teams spend investigating sites they ultimately walk away from: Phase I environmental assessments run $2,500 to $5,000 each, preliminary engineering studies cost $10,000 to $30,000, title searches and legal reviews add another $5,000 to $15,000, and traffic or utility studies can exceed $20,000 for complex sites. A regional builder evaluating 40 to 60 parcels per month and closing on five to eight per year is spending $150,000 to $400,000 annually on deals that never close. That number does not appear on any line item in the budget because it is distributed across consultant invoices that get coded to "land development" regardless of whether the land was ever developed, and nobody tracks the kill ratio because nobody wants to see it.

What the Platforms Actually Do

Prophetic's system unifies five workflows that traditionally involve separate tools, separate consultants, and separate timelines: ZoneAI interprets zoning regulations across U.S. cities by translating thousands of pages of municipal code into structured data about what can be built on a specific parcel; SiteAI runs environmental and regulatory screening; DevMap generates yield estimates and site plans; DealDesk manages the acquisition pipeline; and SearchAI handles parcel discovery and off-market sourcing. Because the platform operates as a single system rather than a collection of point solutions, the zoning output feeds directly into the yield estimate, which feeds directly into the financial model, which feeds directly into the go/no-go recommendation, all within a single interface rather than across four spreadsheets, two consultant reports, and an email chain that nobody can find three weeks later.

Acres Intelligence operates on a different model but attacks the same bottleneck, layering Acres' proprietary parcel, ownership, zoning, permitting, environmental, and geospatial datasets with natural-language AI workflows that can execute tasks previously requiring days of analyst work. Its Home Builder Index tracks land transactions, lot sales, inventory positioning, and competitive activity in real time, giving acquisition directors visibility into where competitors are buying, where lot-price corrections are occurring, and where pricing power persists at the submarket level. Malloy described the value proposition with a line that any acquisition director would recognize: "I'm not going to make my decision to invest millions of dollars based on this. But I can make plenty of decisions not to waste my time based on this."

That is the real economics: the primary value of AI in land acquisition is not finding better sites but killing bad sites faster.

Every parcel eliminated in 15 minutes instead of 15 days is $5,000 to $50,000 in due diligence costs that never get spent, plus the opportunity cost of an acquisition team tied up on a dead end instead of pursuing the next viable tract.

The Math Nobody Has Published

Neither Prophetic nor Acres has disclosed pricing. Neither D.R. Horton nor M/I Homes has published cost savings or deal-velocity improvements from AI adoption. No independent researcher has compared AI-evaluated sites against manually evaluated sites on deal success rates, margin outcomes, or time-to-close metrics. Throughput numbers are impressive on their surface, but throughput and accuracy are different measurements, and the construction industry has a long history of adopting tools that speed up the wrong part of the process.

Here is what we can calculate from public information. Prophetic's customers have analyzed 183,000 parcels across 20 states and identified 3,000 high-potential sites. That is a screen-to-viable ratio of roughly 1.6 percent, meaning 98.4 percent of the parcels evaluated were rejected. If those 183,000 parcels had been evaluated manually at even a cursory first-pass level, at perhaps two hours per parcel for an analyst earning $45 per hour with benefits, the labor cost alone would exceed $16.4 million.

The Counterargument at Full Strength

Land acquisition is a relationship business, and relationships do not scale through software. Off-market deals, the best ones, come from a landowner who calls the acquisition director she has known for 15 years, not from a parcel search engine. Political dynamics of a planning commission hearing, unwritten rules about which variances a city will grant and which it will deny, local knowledge that comes from decades of building in a specific submarket: none of that lives in a dataset. AI can tell you that a parcel is zoned R-3 with a 1.2 FAR and no FEMA flood exposure. It cannot tell you that the planning commissioner who represents that district lost her reelection campaign on an anti-density platform and that every multifamily application in her territory will face a six-month delay that your financial model does not account for.

Oliver Alexander, Prophetic's CEO, put it plainly in his interview with Commercial Observer: "Unless you are the most well-resourced company that can just throw six-figure headcount at this problem, everyone else fights over the scraps on the market." What he is really saying is that the largest builders already have the analysts, the relationships, and the institutional knowledge to compete without AI. Prophetic's pitch is aimed at the builders who lack that infrastructure, and the question for those builders is whether a software platform can substitute for the human capital they never had, or whether it merely gives them a faster way to identify parcels they still lack the local expertise to close.

A convergence risk compounds the problem. If every builder in a market screens the same publicly available parcel data through the same AI platforms using similar scoring criteria, the platforms will identify the same parcels as high-potential, driving competition to the same tracts and inflating land prices in exactly the corridors the AI flagged as undervalued. I have watched this happen in specific corridors where every builder chased the same school district, the same interchange, the same annexation play, and when six letters of intent hit the same landowner in the same month, the price stopped making sense for any of them. AI just accelerates the convergence.

What This Means If You Are Building Five Communities

A regional builder running four to six active communities with a three-year lot supply needs to close two to four new land deals per year to sustain operations. At current evaluation rates, that means screening 200 to 400 parcels annually, spending $150,000 to $400,000 on due diligence for deals that fail, tying up one to two full-time acquisition staff on a process that produces a close rate below 4 percent, and making go/no-go decisions with competitive intelligence that is weeks or months stale because the public records your team relies on lag behind actual transactions by 60 to 90 days in most jurisdictions.

Builders deploying AI are not operating in that world anymore. They are screening thousands of parcels per month instead of dozens, killing nonviable sites in minutes instead of weeks, tracking competitor activity in near-real time instead of from county recorder archives, and generating preliminary underwriting packets that would take an analyst three days to assemble. Already the gap between the two operating models is large, and it is widening with every quarterly improvement cycle these platforms ship.

Jason Jones, D.R. Horton's VP of data analytics, framed the adoption in terms of housing supply: "One of the largest challenges to providing affordable housing is the identification, acquisition, and entitlement of land suitable for development. We are confident the insights provided by Prophetic are going to help us expand homeownership opportunities for hard-working American individuals and families." Strip the corporate language, and the point is simple: more sites evaluated faster means more homes in the pipeline, and in a market short 4.7 million units, speed at the front end of the development cycle is not a competitive luxury but the bottleneck that limits how many homes actually get built.

What nobody in any of these announcements addresses is what happens to the acquisition analysts, the due diligence coordinators, the site evaluators who currently do this work. If a platform can screen 183,000 parcels that would have required "$16.4 million in labor," those labor hours are not being redirected to higher-value tasks in most organizations. They are being eliminated. Whether that is an acceptable trade depends on your position in the org chart.

What This Analysis Cannot Tell You

Prophetic and Acres are the primary commercial sources for the claims in this article, and neither company has submitted to an independent audit of screening accuracy, deal success rates, or time savings. D.R. Horton and M/I Homes have not publicly disclosed financial results attributable to AI adoption in their land operations, and their 10-K filings do not break out technology spending at the level needed to calculate ROI on these platforms. That 183,000-parcel figure represents total screenings across all Prophetic customers, not a single builder's experience, and we do not know how many of the 3,000 identified sites actually closed or produced profitable communities. Platform pricing is undisclosed, which means the cost-benefit calculation for a mid-size builder is still a black box. Small and custom builders under $10 million in annual revenue may find these platforms priced beyond their reach, and the pitch that AI levels the playing field for smaller builders has not been proven by anyone yet. Convergence risk as described above is theoretical, drawn from patterns I have seen in corridor-level land rushes, not from documented outcomes with AI-driven screening specifically.

What we know for certain is that the two largest categories of builder, the nationals with 30-state footprints and the publicly traded regionals, are deploying AI in their land operations at enterprise scale, and the rest of the industry is watching from the parking lot. How long that gap persists, and who pays for it, depends on whether the platforms deliver what the press releases promise. A spreadsheet, meanwhile, is still open on someone's desktop, waiting for the next broker call.
