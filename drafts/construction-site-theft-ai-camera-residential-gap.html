Your Builder Loses $8,400 Per Home to Theft. Four Cameras Would Cost $410. He Bought Zero.

Bobby Whitten runs Wayne Homes in North Canton, Ohio, a company that does $50 million a year in residential construction and that he figures loses roughly $25,000 of that each year to on-site theft. "As sophisticated as we are," Whitten told NAHB, "we see it happen every day." His loss ratio works out to 0.05% of revenue, which would make him one of the luckiest builders in the country if the industry average weren't forty times worse.

Richard Rowerdink's research on construction site material controls put the standard residential shrinkage rate at 2% of gross revenue, a figure that, applied to the current Census Bureau median of $420,000 for new single-family construction, means $8,400 walking off a typical job site in stolen lumber, copper wire, power tools, and fixtures over the course of a build. According to the National Equipment Register, total residential construction theft losses reach $300 million per year, and the National Insurance Crime Bureau's broader estimate, including commercial, runs to $1 billion.

FBI data shows more than 11,000 construction site theft incidents in 2021, a number that exceeded convenience store robberies for the same year, and BauWatch's 2024 Construction Crime Index, built from a survey of 500 workers, found that 70% of construction workers have witnessed theft on sites they've worked. Eighty-five percent of those thefts are never reported to anyone, because the losses fall below insurance deductibles or because nobody has evidence worth filing.

What does not happen next, on the vast majority of residential sites, is anything at all about it.

What Gets Taken

BauWatch's data upends the assumption that construction theft means someone hot-wiring an excavator at 3 a.m., because heavy machinery accounts for just 12% of stolen items while the rest is mundane, portable, and profitable: bricks and roofing tiles at 26%, timber and pipework at 22% each, personal effects like phones and PPE at 20%, and household appliances including boilers and fixtures at 19%. In 64% of incidents the total value of stolen goods fell between $630 and $5,040, with only 6% exceeding $12,600, which means the typical construction thief is not running an Ocean's Eleven operation but loading a truck bed with framing nailers and rolls of Romex wire that will surface at a flea market three counties over by the weekend.

A Maryland builder named Martin Poretsky told NAHB that thousands of dollars in carpeting was stolen "right after it was laid," three nights in a row, while a northeastern Ohio developer had eleven newly planted trees ripped out of a subdivision within hours of landscaping, and a Northern Virginia remodeler lost $1,500 in brass hardware from a locked garage, none of which qualifies as a sophisticated heist and all of which would be preventable with a single camera that sends a phone notification when someone walks onto the site after 6 p.m.

The Security Market That Skipped Residential

AI-powered construction site security exists, it works, and it is not priced for the people who need it most.

ECAM, a UL-listed provider with 140 field technicians nationwide, offers live video monitoring backed by AI detection, where a monitoring specialist watches the feed, issues voice warnings through on-site speakers, and escalates verified threats to law enforcement in real time. ECAM claims 99% detection accuracy and 99% system uptime, and it costs $1,000 to $2,000 per month, a figure that represents a rounding error for a commercial developer building a $40 million mixed-use project and an impossibility for a custom home builder managing two $500,000 houses simultaneously.

Ring launched its Jobsite Security line with a Mobile Security Trailer starting at $4,999, featuring a 12-foot collapsible mast, 360-degree 4K camera, solar power, LED lights, two-way audio, and AI-triggered warnings, all of which is impressive hardware at a price tag that exceeds most residential builders' entire site overhead budget. Milesight sells construction CCTV mobile surveillance units with 4G connectivity and virtual perimeters at enterprise pricing. Avigilon, now owned by Motorola, markets AI-powered analytics for construction monitoring at commercial scale.

LVT deployed mobile security units for Aspect Homes, a residential developer in Nevada, and the results were stark: 50% reduction in trespassing and zero thefts on monitored sites. "I've had no theft," said founder Mickey Stratton, "and I know most of the buildings in the area are experiencing theft." But LVT's pricing model serves volume builders with enough scale to justify the contract, and the custom builder, the small GC, the remodeler running three crews, none of them are part of that calculus.

The $80 Camera Nobody Deploys

A Wyze Solar Cam Pan costs $80, runs on solar power, connects over WiFi or can be paired with a mobile hotspot, delivers 2K video with color night vision, pans 360 degrees, and uses AI to distinguish people from raccoons, sending push notifications to a phone when it detects a human on site, with cloud storage at $15 per month and no subscription required for local recording to a microSD card.

A Gianteye 4G LTE Solar Camera costs roughly $60 and requires no WiFi at all because it uses a cellular SIM card, operating on solar power with battery backup, AI human detection, and built-in 64GB storage, in a product whose manufacturer specifically lists construction sites as a use case.

Four of either camera, positioned at the material laydown area, the tool trailer, the front entrance, and the back of the lot, would cost $240 to $320, and adding six months of cloud storage at $15 per month brings the total to $330 to $410 for the duration of a typical single-family build, a number so small it would not survive a single line-item review on any project budget in the country if anyone bothered to put it there.

At 2% shrinkage on a $420,000 home, the theft exposure is $8,400 against a camera investment of $410, and even if the cameras prevent just 25% of theft, the builder saves $2,100 for a 5:1 return on investment, while preventing half the theft yields 10:1 and eliminating shrinkage entirely, which LVT's Aspect Homes case study suggests is achievable, delivers 20:1.

Why It Doesn't Happen

Three forces keep residential construction sites unmonitored, and none of them have anything to do with the technology being unavailable or expensive.

Insurance structure is the first, because a typical builder's risk policy carries a $2,500 to $5,000 deductible, and when two-thirds of theft incidents fall under $5,000, most losses never generate a claim, so the builder absorbs the cost, categorizes it as "materials variance" in the project budget, and moves on, turning shrinkage into an invisible line item buried in the gap between estimated and actual material costs that nobody tracks because nobody bills for it.

Project management culture is the second, because residential construction operates on trust and relationships where the GC knows his framing crew and the framing crew knows the plumber's guys, and deploying cameras feels like an accusation, though the BauWatch data found that in 49% of cases, workers had been approached by individuals or groups offering "protection" for the site, with 27% actually paying, which suggests the threat is not always internal and the crews who feel accused by a camera are the same crews who lose their Sawzall on Tuesday and don't mention it until Friday.

Market structure is the third, because security companies sell to procurement departments rather than to owner-operators pulling up in a Ford F-250, and the sales cycle for a $1,500-per-month ECAM contract involves site surveys, proposals, and multi-year agreements that have no residential analog, while neither Wyze nor eufy nor any other consumer camera manufacturer has a "builder starter kit" page on their website. Consumer camera companies market to homeowners watching their front porch, commercial security companies market to construction firms with six-figure security budgets, and the residential builder sits in the gap between them, served by neither.

What a Builder Should Do Monday Morning

Buy four solar-powered AI cameras with person detection for $240 to $320 total, mount them on T-posts or existing structures at the four corners of the site covering the material staging area, tool storage, primary access point, and the most exposed elevation, enable push notifications for after-hours human detection, and tell every sub and every crew member the cameras are there, because deterrence works when people know they're being watched and collapses the moment they suspect otherwise.

Add a $10-per-month cellular hotspot if the site lacks WiFi, or use a 4G LTE camera that doesn't require it, bringing the total annual monitoring cost to $120 to $180 for cloud storage.

Track every shrinkage event by comparing estimated material quantities to actual deliveries and installed quantities, because most builders don't know their shrinkage rate and you cannot reduce what you do not count.

Limitations

Rowerdink's 2% shrinkage estimate is not recent, and current industry-wide residential shrinkage data is thin because builders rarely track material losses separately from cost overruns. NER's $300 million figure specifically covers equipment, not materials or tools, and may undercount residential because much residential theft goes unreported. BauWatch's survey polled UK workers, and US patterns may differ in specifics, though the structural dynamics of open sites, portable goods, and low security investment are functionally identical across both markets.

Consumer cameras are not commercial security systems: they lack monitoring services, they require WiFi or cellular connectivity that may be spotty on rural lots, and their AI detection generates false positives from deer, dogs, and delivery drivers. They represent a $400 solution rather than a $40,000 solution and should be evaluated accordingly.

Culturally, the strongest counterargument is that builders have operated without cameras for decades and the industry functions, with shrinkage priced into every bid. But so was hand-drawn takeoff before software replaced it, and so was faxing change orders before Procore, and the fact that a cost has always existed is not evidence that it should continue to when eliminating it costs less than the fuel surcharge on a single lumber delivery.
