A Cloned Voice Confirmed the Wire Instructions. The Buyer Lost $273,000. The Industry's Best Defense Is a Code Word.

By Catherine Chen / July 30, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Jennifer Hupke, a top agent in Milwaukee, described the anatomy of the scam to Inman in plain terms: "Right before closing, you'll get an email that looks like it's from your title company or lender with 'updated wire instructions.' It looks legit: same logos, similar email, correct names. But it's not them. And once that money is wired, it's gone."

Gone, in this context, means unrecoverable. The FBI's 2025 Internet Crime Complaint Center report logged 12,368 real estate fraud complaints and $275.1 million in losses last year, a 58% increase from 2024's $173.6 million. Business email compromise, the category that captures most closing-table wire fraud, accounted for $3.046 billion in total losses across 24,768 complaints nationwide. It was the second-highest loss category in the entire report.

Those numbers are bad. What makes them worse is that they are about to become obsolete, because AI voice cloning has introduced a weapon that email-trained defenses were never designed to stop.

442 Percent

Vishing, the industry term for voice-based phishing conducted over phone calls or VoIP, jumped 442% between the first and second halves of 2024, according to CrowdStrike's 2025 Global Threat Report. By the first quarter of 2025, voice phishing had become the single most common phishing type, accounting for more than 60% of all phishing-related incident response engagements tracked by Cisco Talos.

442% Increase in vishing (voice phishing) attacks between H1 and H2 2024. Voice phishing became the dominant phishing type by Q1 2025, surpassing email for the first time.

Why the spike? Voice cloning software can now generate a usable synthetic voice from just a few minutes of recorded audio. Not hours. A loan officer's YouTube explainer, an agent's podcast appearance, a title company rep's recorded webinar: enough raw material for a criminal to produce a voice that sounds exactly like the person a buyer has been trusting for three months of negotiations, inspections, and paperwork.

Cody Schuiteboer, president and CEO of Best Interest Financial, told Inman that the timing is what makes it lethal: "Fraudsters know that borrowers are anxious about closing, want to make it happen, and are used to following their loan officer's directions implicitly. The cloned voice makes that process extremely efficient since it comes at the right time and is virtually impossible to detect."

Your Agent's Personal Brand Is the Attack Surface

The professionals most at risk are the most successful ones. Agents and loan officers who built strong personal brands through video content, podcast appearances, and webinars have generated the largest libraries of publicly available audio. Every minute of that content is training data for a voice clone.

Michael Benoit, founder of ContractorBond.org and president of Pacific United Insurance Services, put it this way: "The top agents who created the strongest personal brands are the ones who are in the most danger of impersonation because their customers are familiar with this voice and take it on faith."

NAR counts roughly 1.5 million licensed real estate agents in the U.S., and the top 20% handle approximately 80% of transactions by dollar volume, meaning those top-producing agents are simultaneously the richest targets for voice cloning and the most trusted by buyers handling the largest wire transfers. A perverse incentive: the more public-facing and successful an agent becomes, the more vulnerable their clients are.

And it gets worse, because real-time deepfakes now allow fraudsters to use live face-swapping software during video conferences, bypassing identity checks with remote notaries and title companies, which means that voice verification was the last reliable identity check for many closings, and that check is now broken.

What the Numbers Everyone Quotes Get Wrong

That $275.1 million figure gets cited in every industry newsletter, but it is almost certainly a fraction of actual losses.

CertifID's third annual State of Wire Fraud report found that roughly one in four consumers are targeted by fraud during a real estate transaction, with approximately one in twenty becoming victims, which against roughly 6.08 million total home sales in 2025 (5.4 million existing plus 680,000 new) implies about 304,000 victims per year.

96% Estimated share of wire fraud victims who never file an FBI IC3 complaint. The $275 million headline figure may represent as little as 4% of actual losses.

Only 12,368 complaints reached the FBI, and if CertifID's survey is even directionally correct, 96% of victims never reported. Multiply the average loss per complaint ($22,237) by 304,000 estimated victims and you get $6.76 billion in potential annual losses. Cut CertifID's numbers in half to be conservative and the figure is still $3.38 billion, which means the $275 million headline captures somewhere between 4% and 8% of the actual damage.

Average loss per reported complaint: $22,237. But that average skews upward because small losses are less likely to be reported, and even generous adjustment for survey bias and measurement error yields a true cost that is an order of magnitude above the FBI's published number.

The Defense That Exists

CertifID, the company that effectively created the wire fraud protection category in real estate, has protected over 1.4 million transactions and blocked $283 million in attempted fraud. Working with the U.S. Secret Service, the company has recovered $118 million in stolen funds. Its Fraud Engine now safeguards more than $300 billion in annual transaction volume.

In June 2026, CertifID acquired CloseSimple, a closing communication platform, and launched a unified Client Experience that pulls identity verification, document signing, payment, and wire instruction delivery into one authenticated session where sensitive information never travels by email, fraud education surfaces at the exact moment a buyer is about to take a high-risk action, and every interaction feeds the detection engine.

Other platforms are emerging alongside it. Realtor.com integrated automated payment platforms like Payload that handle earnest money deposits without email-based wire instructions at all, removing the primary interception vector. Microsoft's Agent 365 governs agentic workflows in mortgage processing. CrowdStrike's Falcon AIDR treats AI prompts as the new malware.

These tools work, but coverage remains the problem.

Where 86% of Closings Stand

CertifID safeguards roughly $300 billion in annual transaction volume, which sounds large until you put it against total U.S. residential real estate transaction volume of approximately $2.1 trillion, based on 6 million sales at a median price near $350,000. That means the market leader covers approximately 14% of all transaction value. Eighty-six percent of American home closings still depend on email threads, phone calls to unverified numbers, and the assumption that a familiar voice is a trustworthy one.

86% Estimated share of U.S. residential transaction value that still relies on email-based wire instructions and phone verification, outside any dedicated fraud prevention platform.

Meanwhile, AI attack capabilities are compounding at a rate that makes those numbers feel quaint: vishing grew 442% in a single year, CertifID reported that wire fraud losses have grown fifty-fold in under a decade from $9 million to $446 million in annual reported losses, and defense adoption is growing in single-digit percentages.

Fifty-two percent of consumers report being "not aware" or only "somewhat aware" of wire fraud risks when they enter a transaction, and only 47% were warned by their real estate professionals at the start, a gap that becomes more dangerous when you learn that consumers 55 and older are twice as likely to be unaware of the threat as those 44 and younger.

How the Industry Actually Responds

Right now, the dominant defense remains a code word: buyer, agent, and closing team agree on a verification phrase at the start of the relationship, and no phrase means no wire movement. Benoit and others recommend dual verification: confirm by phone and by a known email address simultaneously.

Code words work until they don't. A criminal who has infiltrated email long enough to learn transaction details, timing, and participant names, which is how business email compromise operates by definition, may also learn the code word. Dual verification helps, but it assumes the "known number" hasn't been spoofed and the "known email" hasn't been compromised, precisely the assumptions BEC attacks exploit.

Robert Siciliano, a security awareness expert, proposed a structured framework he calls the Triple-A Protocol: Analyze (recognize manufactured urgency), Authenticate (identify the digital mask), Act (execute out-of-band verification through a pre-validated number). It is a reasonable behavioral checklist for a problem that has outgrown behavioral checklists.

"Whatever public audio you created is available to someone working on a clone, which isn't a paranoid idea because it's true," Schuiteboer told Inman. His conclusion: "The only surefire defense in the long run is a culture that treats voice alone as an insufficient means of confirming identity for any transfer of funds."

What to Do Before You Wire Anything

If you are buying a home, three steps reduce your exposure materially.

First, ask your title company whether they use a dedicated wire fraud prevention platform like CertifID, the current market leader. If the answer involves the words "we call to verify," that is 2019-era security applied to a 2026-era threat. Closing teams that don't use protected payment infrastructure are asking buyers to bet their down payment on phone verification that AI has already defeated.

Second, if you receive a call that sounds like your agent, loan officer, or title rep asking you to act on wire instructions immediately, establish a verification protocol on the day you sign the purchase agreement, not the week of closing: insist that no wire instructions will be accepted or changed without a separate, in-person or video confirmation initiated by you, not by whoever calls first, and agree on a code word that serves as the final gate before any money moves.

Third, hang up. If someone calls about wire instructions, end the call and dial them back on a number you saved independently, not from the call itself, not from a related email. If the real person picks up confused, you just prevented a loss averaging $22,237. If nobody picks up, you have your answer.

The FBI's Recovery Asset Team can freeze fraudulent transfers if notified quickly through IC3 or local field offices. Speed matters: the Financial Fraud Kill Chain protocol works only when the report arrives before the funds are moved offshore, typically within 48 to 72 hours.

Limitations

The underreporting calculation relies on CertifID's consumer survey data, which has incentive bias. CertifID sells wire fraud protection; its survey estimates are higher than independent sources. The one-in-four targeting rate and one-in-twenty victimization rate have not been independently replicated. The $6.76 billion extrapolation should be read as an upper bound, not a midpoint. Similarly, the 86% adoption gap is derived from CertifID's self-reported transaction coverage against total market volume, and competing platforms' coverage is not included, meaning the true unprotected share is likely somewhat lower. The FBI's IC3 report captures only reported, quantified losses and explicitly acknowledges undercounting.

Voice cloning quality varies, and not every cloned voice is indistinguishable from the original. The 442% vishing increase reflects all voice phishing, not only AI-cloned attacks; many vishing incidents still use human impersonation without AI. What has changed is the floor: the minimum skill required to produce a convincing fake voice dropped from "recording studio and voice actor" to "laptop and three minutes of YouTube audio."
