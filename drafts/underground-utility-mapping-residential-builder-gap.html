Your Contractor Called 811. The Locator Painted Lines Where the Records Said. The Gas Main Was on the Other Side of the Road.

By Jake Kowalski July 25, 2026

In early 2026, the National Utility Locating Contractors Association published an open letter that should have made every general contractor in America reconsider how much trust they place in painted lines on dirt. Their locators, the professionals whose entire career consists of finding buried pipes and cables before someone else's backhoe finds them the hard way, described a pattern so persistent it had become background noise in the industry: marks placed exactly where municipal records indicated, only for excavation to reveal infrastructure in a completely different location, not a few feet off but on the wrong side of the road, crossing at the wrong block, or missing from the records entirely, which means the people whose job it is to stand between your excavator and a pressurized gas main are working from maps they no longer trust. When professionals who locate utilities for a living tell you the records they depend on are unreliable, the question shifts from whether the system has a problem to how deep the problem runs and why residential construction, where excavation volume dwarfs every other sector, gets nothing better than spray paint and hope.

Last year, underground utility strikes across the United States hit a record. Common Ground Alliance documented 221,717 unique damage reports through its national Damage Information Reporting Tool, more than any year since tracking began. CGA's year-over-year Index, benchmarked from a 2022 baseline of 100, rose to 102, climbing eight points from 2023 in lockstep with construction activity that shows no sign of slowing. CGA President Sarah K. Magruder Lyle and the Damage Prevention Action Center put the total annual economic cost at more than $30 billion when you account for direct repair, project delay, litigation, service disruption, and the twenty fatalities that occur each year because a bucket hit a pressurized line nobody knew was there.

During active construction hours, a crew somewhere in this country strikes something buried every three minutes.

A 99.47% System With a $30 Billion Residual

Give the 811 One Call system its due: in 2021 it fielded 39.5 million locate requests and achieved a 99.47% accuracy rate on processed calls, which sounds near-perfect until you multiply 39.5 million by the remaining 0.53% and land on roughly 209,000 expected strikes, a number that lines up almost exactly with what CGA measured four years later despite sustained investment in damage prevention programs, upgraded locate equipment, and federal legislative attention that produced the bipartisan PIPES Act and several rounds of PHMSA rulemaking. Root-cause analysis from CGA's 2024 DIRT data partitions the damage sources into two nearly equal blocks: excavation practices at 24% and locating-plus-mapping accuracy failures at 25%, meaning that in one quarter of all reported incidents the people who were supposed to find the lines either could not or found them in the wrong place because the underlying records were wrong, which is a fundamentally different failure mode than a careless excavator and requires a fundamentally different solution.

Failure to call 811 at all remains the single largest contributor and has barely budged in years, but the mapping failures are what matter here because they represent a technology problem that is actively being solved for commercial construction and completely ignored for residential.

Commercial Gets Centimeters. Residential Gets Spray Paint.

On a commercial project with an eight-figure budget, the owner hires a subsurface utility engineering firm that deploys ground-penetrating radar, electromagnetic locators, and sometimes vacuum excavation to produce ASCE 38 Quality Level A data, meaning verified horizontal and vertical positions with centimeter accuracy that get integrated into the project's BIM model before a single shovel breaks ground. Highway departments order these surveys before every interchange reconstruction, data center campuses map every conduit and lateral beneath their footprint because hitting a fiber trunk carrying a hyperscaler's traffic is the kind of mistake that ends contracts, and municipal water authorities require them for any pipe replacement deeper than four feet in congested utility corridors, all of which means the technology to know exactly what is buried beneath any piece of dirt already exists, works reliably, and is commercially available to anyone willing to pay $5,000 to $25,000 for a survey that on a $50 million project is a rounding error with enormous downside protection.

A residential GC building a $400,000 custom home calls 811, watches painted lines go down on the dirt, and digs. For a $2 million infill project on a lot where utilities have been added, rerouted, spliced, and abandoned since the Eisenhower administration, the locating standard is identical.

Screening Eagle's GS1000, standard equipment on large commercial sites, produces real-time 2D subsurface scans overlaid on a satellite map with adjustable depth layers so a superintendent can see exactly what is under the next ten feet of trench before the excavator moves an inch of soil. Haskell's innovation arm, Dysruptek, takes GPR output further by feeding it into a 3D model displayed through a Trimble XR10 augmented reality headset, which lets someone stand on bare dirt and see pipes, conduits, and cables rendered in three-dimensional space exactly where they sit underground, as if six feet of soil and clay had turned transparent. SubVysion, a Y Combinator S2026 startup in San Francisco, is building centimeter-accurate 3D maps of underground utilities and pitching them as a direct solution to the $30 billion in annual damages and twenty annual deaths that utility strikes cause, and the broader underground mapping market is projected to reach $3.3 billion by 2030 according to ResearchAndMarkets, which tells you how much money is flowing toward solving this problem for people who build highways and data centers.

None of these companies have a residential product page. None price for a half-acre lot. Not one markets to the contractor pouring your foundation.

Economics Nobody Runs

A gas line strike on a residential site costs $3,000 to $10,000 in direct repair plus the insurance claim, the premium increase that follows it, and one to four weeks of project delay that cascades through every subcontractor schedule downstream of excavation, turning a single incident into a compounding cost that can eat the builder's entire profit margin on a mid-range custom home. A fiber optic cable hit can generate $10,000 to $50,000 in business interruption claims from the telecom provider who will pursue recovery aggressively because their SLA clock started ticking the moment service dropped. A water main break runs $2,000 to $8,000 with a minimum one-day shutdown while the utility company repairs, pressure-tests, disinfects, and reinspects before clearing you to backfill. In every scenario, the contractor bears liability if the utility was not properly located, even when 811 was called and the marks were placed exactly where records indicated, because the prevailing legal standard of reasonable diligence will prompt a judge or jury to ask whether a prudent contractor should have used additional locating methods given visible risk factors on the lot, and "I followed the painted lines" is an explanation, not a defense.

A commercial-grade GPR survey that would have prevented every one of those strikes runs $5,000 to $25,000 depending on lot size and subsurface complexity, which represents 1.25% to 6.25% of a $400,000 build and sits squarely in the range that residential contractors reject because their margins are already thin enough that any line item without immediate visible benefit gets cut. But a single-pass GPR survey of a residential lot with a handheld unit and basic automated software interpretation could be priced at $500 to $1,500 if someone built the business model around residential volume rather than commercial project fees, because the scanning hardware already exists and the interpretation software already exists and neither has been packaged for the market that digs more holes per year than any other segment of construction.

Run the numbers at residential scale and the gap becomes absurd. Approximately 1.1 million housing starts per year, each involving excavation for foundations, utility trenches for water, sewer, electric, and gas, and frequently additional trenching for irrigation, landscape lighting, and data connections. CGA does not publish a residential-specific strike rate, which is itself revealing about how much attention the damage-prevention industry pays to homebuilding, but applying even a conservative 2% incident rate at an average direct cost of $7,500 yields 22,000 strikes and $165 million in preventable damage per year, concentrated entirely in the segment of the construction industry with the least access to the technology that prevents it.

Infill Lots and ADUs Sit in the Blast Radius

New subdivision construction has one structural advantage: the developer who installed the utilities usually has decent records because the infrastructure is recent and the as-built drawings were filed digitally rather than sketched by a crew foreman on graph paper fifty years ago. Risk concentrates in infill development and ADU construction within established neighborhoods, where the original sewer lateral may run at a different angle than the as-built drawing shows because the original clay pipe was replaced with PVC in the 1990s without anyone updating the municipal GIS layer, and the gas company's records may show a main on the north side of the street that was actually relocated to the south side during a road widening in 1987, and a cable TV line might not appear in any database at all because the installer who direct-buried it in 1998 never filed a locate sheet with the jurisdiction, which means every trench on a mature lot is an act of faith in records that may predate the widespread adoption of email.

California alone permitted 22,000 ADUs in 2023, each requiring trenching for water, sewer, and electrical connections across lots that have accumulated sixty years of additions, repairs, reroutes, and improvisation by homeowners who never pulled a permit. For every one of those 22,000 projects, the subsurface locating standard was an 811 call and painted lines derived from records that nobody has verified against physical reality since the lot was originally platted, which in much of the Bay Area and Los Angeles basin means the 1950s and 1960s.

What Would Shift This

A handheld GPR device priced under $2,000 and designed for a one-person residential crew rather than a three-person subsurface engineering team would turn a pre-dig scan into a standard line item on every residential excavation bid, and the closest the commercial market has gotten is Screening Eagle's Proceq GP8000 at roughly $8,000, which still requires interpretation expertise that most residential contractors do not have, so the gap between what exists and what residential needs is real but not wide and not expensive to close for a manufacturer willing to trade margin per unit for volume across a million-start-per-year market. Machine learning applied to GPR signal classification is an active research area with strong results in laboratory settings, but practical residential deployment trails commercial deployment by roughly five years because all the training data comes from commercial sites and nobody has yet built the residential-specific dataset that would let an algorithm distinguish a 2-inch PVC water line at 30 inches from a 4-inch cast iron sewer at 36 inches in the kind of shallow, cluttered, poorly documented subsurface that defines a 1960s residential lot.

Municipal as-built records digitized and accessible through a national subsurface registry would give 811 locators dramatically better maps to work from before they ever step onto a lot. Britain's National Underground Asset Register is building exactly this, creating a comprehensive digital twin of the country's subsurface infrastructure that will be available to any authorized user. No American equivalent exists, and the NULCA open letter makes clear that locating professionals have run out of patience waiting for one.

Limitations

CGA's DIRT data is self-reported and almost certainly undercounts total damages, particularly in residential construction where minor strikes get repaired on-site without anyone filing a report. My residential-specific strike estimate of 22,000 per year rests on housing-start data and an assumed 2% incident rate; CGA publishes no residential breakdown, making the number a reasonable extrapolation rather than a measured fact. GPR equipment costs cited are approximate retail prices that vary by region, vendor, and lease-versus-purchase terms. Alexander van Noort's statistic that 90% of construction projects experience delays and 50% trace to subsurface uncertainties comes from an industry context that skews heavily toward commercial and infrastructure work and may not translate directly to residential, where projects are smaller and subsurface complexity is generally lower.

Strongest Counterargument

Most residential utility strikes do not cause serious injury, and the direct repair cost on any single project falls well below what a comprehensive subsurface survey would have cost, which means the economically rational response for a builder operating on 8% margins is to accept the residual risk rather than add $1,500 to every excavation bid in a market where buyers are already stretching to afford the house. Apply a $1,500 GPR survey to all 1.1 million housing starts and you spend $1.65 billion annually to prevent $165 million in damage, a ten-to-one cost ratio that makes no financial sense at the aggregate level and probably never will.

But utility strikes are not normally distributed across all lots; they cluster on the properties where old records are wrong, where multiple utilities cross in patterns nobody documented, and where prior unpermitted work created subsurface conditions that no database reflects, which is precisely the infill and ADU construction that constitutes the fastest-growing segment of residential building and carries the highest per-incident consequences because these lots sit in dense neighborhoods where a gas main strike can evacuate a block and a fiber cut can drop service for hundreds of downstream customers. Targeting just the riskiest 10% of residential excavations, roughly 110,000 projects on mature infill and ADU lots, with a $500 to $1,500 survey would cost $55 to $165 million per year, a market large enough to sustain a real business and small enough that the underground mapping industry has not yet noticed the opportunity sitting six inches beneath every backyard in America.
