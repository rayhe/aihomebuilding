Your House Isn't Bolted to Its Foundation. California Will Pay You $3,000 to Fix That.

By Catherine Chen · September 6, 2026

Picture the ground under your house moving sideways, hard, for what feels like a minute. Now picture the connection between your house and that ground: a wooden sill plate resting on concrete, held by gravity and the optimism of whoever built the place in 1962. For most California homes built before 1980 with raised foundations, that connection is approximately nothing.

When the lateral load arrives, the house slides, or drops. Or the short cripple walls around the crawl space fold over. California's disaster-preparedness office puts it plainly: houses without adequate bolting and bracing can slide or topple off their foundations during an earthquake, and the repairs afterward are "potentially very expensive," which is bureaucratese for a number that can erase a homeowner's equity in a single afternoon.

The fix is disarmingly simple: bolt the house to the foundation and brace the cripple walls with plywood. The California Residential Mitigation Program, a joint powers authority few have heard of, will pay for a substantial share of it.

$3,000
Maximum Earthquake Brace + Bolt grant per house, against a published typical retrofit cost of $3,000 to $7,000, per CRMP's program FAQ. Do the subtraction: the grant covers 43 to 100 percent of a standard contractor job.

Where the Money Goes

Earthquake Brace + Bolt offers up to $3,000 per house toward a retrofit to California Existing Building Code Chapter A3, under program rules effective February 1, 2026. Funding comes from the California Earthquake Authority's Loss Mitigation Fund and FEMA's Hazard Mitigation Grant Program, two institutions that ran the actuarial math and chose bolts over rebuilds, and the ZIP codes where the money flows are picked by a formula that weights the USGS earthquake hazard map and the share of pre-1940 homes equally.

Buried in section 1.2 of the rules is a targeting algorithm that sends public money to the oldest houses on the riskiest ground, exactly where private retrofit demand is weakest: older homes, older owners, fixed incomes.

Earn $94,480 or less and a supplemental grant can cover up to 100 percent of the cost. Maximums are regional: up to $7,000 more in Northern California for a full brace-and-bolt, $2,650 in Southern California, per the January 2026 grant rules. A qualifying Bay Area homeowner facing a $7,000 quote can pay nothing; in San Diego the same quote leaves a $1,350 check.

A Sibling Program

This year the program got a sibling that matters enormously if your house has a tuck-under garage, because the soft-story archetype fails in a completely different and far more dangerous way. New rules effective February 1, 2026 launched Earthquake Soft-Story for houses with big ground-floor openings. Those get retrofitted to FEMA P-1100 plan sets, a prescriptive document that tells the contractor exactly where to brace and how to bolt, which is convenient, because EBB's cripple-wall math does not apply to a soft-story house and registering for the wrong program wastes a registration window.

Ground floor mostly air and garage doors? You want Earthquake Soft-Story. Raised foundation over a crawl space? EBB.

What the AI Sees From the Street

Meanwhile, a different inventory is being built. Researchers at Berkeley, UCLA, and Stanford developed a deep-learning system that identifies soft-story buildings from Google Street View images at regional scale, because conventional identification is labor-intensive and time-consuming, and BRAILS++, the NSF-funded NHERI SimCenter tool, took the same idea further, extracting building characteristics from satellite and street-level imagery to feed regional earthquake loss models and turning a clipboard survey that took engineers years into a compute job.

No app photographs your crawl space and quotes the retrofit, and the street-view models are trained on the multi-story soft-story archetype, not the single-family houses EBB targets. Less exciting and more important: detection exists at the city scale, money exists at the house scale, and nobody has built the bridge between them yet.

Insurance Math Nobody Runs

Finish the retrofit and the California Earthquake Authority discounts your earthquake premium by up to 25 percent, which sounds generous until the payback period stretches toward your children's college graduations. CEA's average homeowner premium runs about $800 a year, making the discount roughly $200 annually, which means a $4,000 out-of-pocket retrofit carries a twenty-year payback. If you are doing this for the insurance discount, you are doing it wrong.

You are doing it because the alternative is the foundation bill. Foundation work starts above $10,000 and structural damage runs into the hundreds of thousands. In PEER and CEA laboratory tests, retrofitted cripple walls gained 125 percent to over 1,800 percent in strength depending on wall finish, and held peak load at nearly triple the drift of unretrofitted walls. The house stays on the foundation.

Fine Print That Forfeits the Grant

Two traps, both in writing. First: do not start the work before you submit preliminary documentation and receive clearance from EBB and FEMA, because the rules are explicit and they are not kidding, and the experienced do-it-yourselfer who bolts the house on a Saturday and applies on Monday gets nothing. Second: registration opens in windows by ZIP code, and eligible is not the same as funded. When your ZIP opens, register first and comparison-shop contractors second.

What You Should Actually Do

Buying a pre-1980 California home with a raised foundation? Check your ZIP at earthquakebracebolt.com before closing, since grants follow the house's location, not the owner, so the retrofit economics belong in your offer math, and make your contractor quote against the Chapter A3 standard plan, not against vibes. Household income under $94,480: apply for the supplemental grant in the same registration. Ground floor a garage with a house balanced on top: you want the soft-story program, not EBB.

Why You Might Reasonably Do Nothing

Strongest argument for doing nothing: with no earthquake insurance, $3,000 to $7,000 buys mitigation for an event that may never hit your ZIP during your ownership, and the same money spent on fire hardening or a new roof has more certain returns, which is exactly the calculation most California homeowners are quietly making right now. Fair objection, but it runs into two facts: standard homeowners policies exclude earthquake damage entirely, and FEMA aid after a major quake is slow, partial, and means-tested. A house that slides off its foundation is frequently a total loss, and no premium discount math captures that. The retrofit is cheap catastrophe insurance you bolt to the ground.

What This Piece Did Not Prove

CRMP's $3,000 to $7,000 cost band is the program's own published range, not an independent audit, and I could not find a third-party dataset of completed retrofit invoices to check it against, which means the most important number in this article is also the least verified. Real quotes move with cripple-wall height, rot discovered mid-job, and foundation condition, and that twenty-year payback uses CEA's average premium, so yours will vary with fault proximity, construction type, and deductible. AI detection research targets soft-story buildings, and its accuracy on single-family cripple-wall stock is untested. And the PEER-CEA data is laboratory component testing, not whole-house field performance, which is the number you actually care about and the number nobody has.
