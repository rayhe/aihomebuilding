Your House Passed Its Blower Door Test. It Still Changes All Its Air Every 20 Minutes.

By Priya Greenwood · September 25, 2026
← Back to articles

On a Tuesday morning in October, an energy rater wedges a calibrated fan into the front door of a brand-new house, a nylon shroud sealing the frame. Once the blower spins up, the house is pulled to a negative pressure of 50 Pascals, roughly the squeeze of a 20-mile-an-hour wind pressing on every wall, every window, every square inch of siding, all at once, a condition that never occurs in nature, which is exactly the point.

Under that artificial storm, the rater measures how much air the fan must move to hold the pressure steady. That reading gets normalized against the volume of the house and reported as ACH50: air changes per hour at 50 Pascals. Across most of America, a new home must score 3.0 or better, so read that number slowly. In effect, the law allows your house to replace its entire volume of air three times an hour. Under test pressure, that is a complete air change every 20 minutes.

Almost no buyer ever sees the number, because it travels from the rater's laptop to the code official in a signed report, gets filed with the permit paperwork, and disappears. This is a shame, because ACH50 is the most honest figure in residential construction. R-values are lab promises and SEER ratings assume perfect installs, but the blower door number is a measurement of the actual house, taken on the actual lot, on the day it was finished.

What 3.0 costs in Chicago

Take a 2,400-square-foot two-story outside Chicago: about 20,400 cubic feet of interior volume, a 92-percent gas furnace at $1.20 a therm, cooling at 17 cents a kilowatt-hour, and 6,500 heating degree days a year. At 3.0 ACH50, the arithmetic runs like this.

CFM50 equals 3.0 times 20,400 divided by 60: 1,020 cubic feet per minute at test pressure. Researchers at Lawrence Berkeley Laboratory spent years correlating test-pressure leakage with real-world infiltration, and their rule of thumb divides by 20. Call it 51 cubic feet per minute of natural leakage on an average day. Annual heating penalty follows the standard degree-day formula, 1.08 times CFM times 24 hours times heating degree days: 8.6 million BTU of sensible heat a year, which the furnace turns into 93 therms of gas, about $112, while cooling adds 368 kilowatt-hours, roughly $63. Total: around $175 a year, every year, for a house that passed code.

Now tighten that house to 1.5 ACH50, a number good builders hit routinely. Savings run about half the total, $87 a year, or roughly $2,600 over a 30-year mortgage. Not nothing, but not the headline either.

What should worry you is the existing housing stock. A 1960s ranch testing at 10 ACH50 leaks about 119 cubic feet per minute under natural conditions. Against the same Chicago math, that is 218 therms a year, around $262, just to heat air that escapes through the cracks. If you are buying an older home, the blower door number matters more than the furnace brand.

The code is a patchwork, and the patches have holes

The 2021 International Energy Conservation Code, the model code most states work from, demands 5.0 ACH50 in climate zones 1 and 2 and 3.0 everywhere else, tested to ASTM E779 or E1827 with a written report delivered to the code official. That is the model; the adoptions, as always, are another story. Louisiana took the 2021 code and amended the limit up to 7.0 ACH50 statewide. Indiana lets builders substitute a visual inspection for the test entirely. Meanwhile the 2024 IECC tightens the model to between 2.0 and 2.5, but only a handful of states enforce it so far.

So the number on your report depends on your ZIP code twice over: once for the climate zone, once for how seriously your state takes the model code. A house that passes in Baton Rouge would fail in Denver, yet both get certificates of occupancy.

A $2,900 fog

Sealing a house by hand means a caulk gun, a can of foam, and a crew willing to crawl the attic twice. AeroBarrier, the aerosol envelope-sealing system, does it with a fog. In that process the blower door stays in the doorway while the house is pressurized, and atomized sealant rides the escaping air straight into the gaps, pinholes to half-inch cracks, sealing them from the inside out while the software watches the number fall in real time.

A builder's guide to the process puts typical results for a 1,800-to-2,600-square-foot home at 4.0 to 6.5 ACH50 before sealing and 1.5 to 2.0 after, for $2,400 to $3,400 and two to three and a half hours of active sealing. A Connecticut retrofit documented by Steven Winter Associates went from 3.32 to 1.63 in about two hours using less than four gallons of sealant, a 51 percent cut. Beazer Homes uses the system nationally as its answer to air-leakage targets.

Run the payback honestly and it is not a triumph. Taking our Chicago archetype from 5.0 to 1.8 ACH50 saves roughly $186 a year in heating and cooling. Against a $2,900 mid-range sealing cost, that is a 16-year simple payback on energy alone. But the real case for the fog is elsewhere: rooms that stop feeling drafty, attics that stop growing mold, and HVAC equipment sized from a real number instead of a guess. A Manual J calculation fed with 1.5 ACH50 instead of 5.0 can trim several thousand BTU per hour off the design load, which is real money on equipment day.

The number is an index, not a leak rate

Here is the part the industry mumbles. Fifty Pascals never happens in a real house. It approximates a 20-mph wind on all four sides at once, sustained, which meteorology declines to provide. ACH50 is a comparative index, brilliant for ranking houses, useless as a description of any given Tuesday, and that divide-by-20 conversion to natural infiltration is a correlation carrying roughly plus-or-minus 50 percent of slop, depending on climate, exposure, and how the wind actually hits the lot.

Credit where due: the instrument itself is precise, with a European review of repeatability studies putting the measurement standard deviation at 1 to 2 percent in calm conditions with experienced testers, under 4 percent across testers who follow verified procedures. Wobble enters when anyone extrapolates from 50 Pascals down to the 2-to-4 Pascals of natural infiltration, because the pressure exponent in that extrapolation averages 0.65 across American houses with a standard deviation of 0.057, putting roughly ten to fifteen percent uncertainty into the natural-leakage estimate. Precise instrument, approximate translation, and both things are true.

Then there is preparation, which moves the number more than most buyers realize. A standard blower door test is supposed to be "as-used": nothing taped, dampers as found. AeroBarrier's own technical bulletin acknowledges that its sealing runs happen with windows, ducts, and fireplaces masked off, and that a later blower door test reads differently once the prep comes down. Preparation choices move the number, and the code requires third-party testing only "where required by the code official," which means the builder usually hires the rater who delivers the passing grade.

Drones are learning to see heat

The clipboard era may be ending. Lamarr.AI, a spinout from MIT's Building Technology Program, flies drones carrying infrared and visible-light cameras around buildings, then runs the imagery through computer vision models that classify thermal anomalies: missing insulation, air leaks, water intrusion. Large language models draft the report, including the cost to fix each defect and the expected return. Lamarr's team began by studying how heat waves punish low-income neighborhoods, won a $1.8 million Department of Energy award, and now sells to portfolio owners across the US, Canada, the UK, and the UAE.

In the UK, Kestrix is building city-scale heat-loss atlases the same way, stitching drone thermography into 3D models that quantify watts lost per square meter of facade, so landlords and policymakers can aim retrofit money at the worst buildings first. America's General Services Administration, through its emerging-tech arm, cites vendor claims of 50 percent more defects detected than manual inspection, at 80 percent lower cost and 80 percent faster delivery.

Temper the excitement, though, because those are vendor claims and pilot projects, aimed at commercial portfolios rather than your 2,400-square-foot colonial. Nobody is flying a thermal drone over single-family resales yet, and the models still need a thermographer's judgment for the ambiguous cases. If a home auditor tries to charge you an "AI premium" this year, the premium is for marketing.

The honest objections

The strongest case against chasing the number goes like this. Below about 2 ACH50, a house needs deliberate mechanical ventilation under ASHRAE 62.2, and a tight house with a neglected or missing ERV has worse indoor air than a leaky one with fresh air pouring through the cracks, which means tightness without ventilation is not an upgrade.

Second, the dollars-per-BTU case for air sealing usually loses to competing upgrades. In many homes, the same $2,900 buys attic insulation or a heat-pump water heater with a better return. Air sealing earns its keep through comfort, durability, and moisture control, which are real but harder to put in a spreadsheet.

Third, sealing the envelope changes combustion safety, because a tighter house can backdraft a naturally vented water heater or fireplace, spilling carbon monoxide indoors. Any serious air-sealing job includes combustion-appliance-zone testing, and any contractor who skips it should be shown the door.

What to do with all this
  • If you are buying new, write the number into the contract: 2.0 ACH50 or better, tested by a third party to ASTM E779, report delivered at closing. Expect about $325 for the test on its own, $350 to $450 with thermal imaging.
  • If you are building, budget $2,400 to $3,400 for aerosol sealing on a typical home, and get tested before drywall and at final, not just at final.
  • If you own an older home, test first and seal second. A good audit finds the big holes, attic top plates, rim joists, recessed can lights, the chimney chase, and those get sealed before anything else.
  • If you are going below 2 ACH50, price the ERV or HRV in the same breath. ASHRAE 62.2 is not optional at that tightness.
  • Get combustion safety testing with any serious air-sealing work. Tight houses and atmospherically vented appliances are a combination that demands verification.

What this article did not prove

The dollar figures rest on the Lawrence Berkeley divide-by-20 rule of thumb, which this article states plainly is approximate, plus-or-minus 50 percent depending on climate and exposure. Chicago's 6,500 heating degree days, $1.20 gas, and 17-cent electricity are assumptions; the formulas are shown so you can substitute your own. AeroBarrier's cost table comes from a single builder's guide and varies by market. Drone-thermography figures are vendor claims and pilot results, not peer-reviewed studies, and none of them cover single-family resales. Cooling math counts sensible load only; the latent load of humid infiltration air is excluded, which understates cooling penalties in humid climates. No house was measured for this article, so what it proves is narrower and, I would argue, more useful: the test exists, the number has a dollar value you can compute, and the gap between a code-minimum house and a tight one is worth knowing before you sign.
