Your 900-CFM Range Hood Passed Inspection. Nobody Read the Code Section That Should Have Stopped It.

By Catherine Chen, September 13, 2026, Policy & Regulation

Seventeen years ago, the International Residential Code grew a new sentence. Section M1503.4, in the 2009 edition, said that any kitchen exhaust hood capable of moving more than 400 cubic feet per minute must be supplied with makeup air at roughly the exhaust rate, through a damper that opens automatically when the hood runs. (IRC M1503.4) Homeowners hated it, contractors ignored it, and plan reviewers skimmed past it. In the code book it sat like a speed limit sign on an empty highway.

It turns out the speed limit was physics.

A 900 CFM pro-style hood is not a kitchen appliance. It is a small commercial ventilation system wearing a kitchen costume, and when you switch it on in a modern tight house, it has to find 900 cubic feet of air per minute from somewhere. If the house envelope does not leak enough, the hood creates that air by pulling the interior pressure negative, and in a 2,400-square-foot home built to today's airtightness levels, the number gets alarming fast. I ran the power-law infiltration model, Q equals C times dP to the 0.65, against three levels of house tightness. In a tight new home (2 ACH50), a 900 CFM hood pulls the interior about 70 Pascals negative. In a typical existing home (5 ACH50), about 32 Pascals. Even in a drafty 10 ACH50 farmhouse, about 6 Pascals.

Now meet the other side of the ledger. Stacked against those numbers are the worst-case depressurization limits for natural-draft, atmospherically vented appliances, the kind that rely on a warm flue instead of a fan: minus 2 Pascals for an orphan water heater, minus 3 for a furnace commonly vented with a water heater, and minus 5 for a single natural-draft appliance. (BPI/RESNET combustion safety protocols) When the house goes more negative than those limits, the flue stops drafting and the combustion gases, including carbon monoxide, start spilling into the room. Compare 70 against minus 2: the arithmetic is not a close call.

And here is the number nobody in the industry seems to have written down: even the code's 400 CFM threshold, the point at which makeup air becomes mandatory, exceeds the minus 5 Pascal limit by roughly 4 times in a tight new home (20 Pa at 400 CFM against 2 ACH50). A 900 CFM hood exceeds the orphan-water-heater limit in every house in my table, leaky farmhouse included, which means the 400 CFM line in the code is not conservative. If anything, it is generous to the hood.

In 2018 the IRC rewrote the requirement to name the actual hazard, hinging the makeup-air mandate on whether natural-draft fuel-burning appliances that are neither direct-vent nor mechanical-draft sit inside the dwelling's air barrier. (GreenBuildingAdvisor) That is the code drafters admitting, quietly, what the physics always said: the rule was never about fresh air, it was about not reversing your water heater's flue while you sear a steak.

So why does almost nobody comply? Because the code lives on the wrong pages: the hood's CFM rating sits on the appliance schedule, buried in the kitchen sheets, while the mechanical notes about makeup air live three pages away. A human plan reviewer, staring at a residential set on a Friday afternoon with a deadline, checks the pages, not the physics. Nobody cross-references the appliance sheet against the mechanical notes against the location of the water heater.

A machine does, because that is the actual AI story here, and it is not a press release. UpCodes launched AI-native plan review in June 2026, running discipline-specific analyses including MEP against more than 11 million locally adopted code sections across 6,000 jurisdictions, with each flagged issue linked to the drawing page and the governing section. (UpCodes announcement) When a jurisdiction adopts it, the M1503.4 check happens automatically, on every set, before the permit is issued. Your 2024 inspector read some of the pages; the algorithm reads all of them at once.

NREL's field work backs up the hazard model. In a combustion safety test protocol field study, kitchen fan airflow rates were found to be on the same order of magnitude as clothes dryer airflow, and it was the combustion-appliance-zone depressurization, not which fan was running, that statistically predicted flue spillage. Backdrafting was observed in real homes. (NREL field study) For context on the stakes: CDC surveillance counted roughly 430 unintentional non-fire carbon monoxide deaths a year from 2005 to 2018, and provisional 2022 data reached 624 accidental deaths, an 85.7 percent increase over a decade. (CDC/USAFacts) Those are not hood-caused deaths, and no one should claim they are. They are the size of the pool in which the hazard swims.

Now the full-strength case against the alarm, because the counterargument is not weak. Millions of homes have run 600-plus CFM hoods for decades without makeup air and without mass casualties. Most older homes are leaky enough that the hood never reaches critical depressurization. Sealed-combustion and power-vented appliances are now the majority in new construction, which makes the hazard moot in exactly the houses where hoods are biggest. And worst-case CAZ test conditions, every exhaust fan on high, interior doors positioned for maximum depressurization, the dryer running, almost never occur simultaneously in real life. NREL's own researchers noted the standard test produces false positives. A 400 CFM mandate can look like a regressive renovation tax: a $1,000-plus retrofit on every kitchen remodel that crosses the threshold.

Nor is the fix free, but it is cheaper than the counterargument suggests. A Broan MD6TU, a 6-inch interlocked makeup-air damper with a pressure-sensor kit that meets M1503.4, retails for about $269. An 8-inch MD8TU runs $284 to $290; the 10-inch, around $400. Installed, with ductwork and a wall cap, the job typically lands between $800 and $1,500. (Wickford Appliance) That is a line item, not a hardship, on a kitchen remodel that justified a $3,000 range hood.

Here is the playbook for the homeowner or builder reading this. Remodeling with a hood over 400 CFM? Budget the damper and put it in the mechanical notes, because the plan reviewer, human or AI, will redline the permit without it. Buying a home with a pro-style range and an atmospherically vented water heater or furnace inside the same air barrier? Ask for a worst-case CAZ depressurization test from a BPI analyst: it costs $200 to $400, takes about 45 minutes, and tells you whether the house fails before you close. Building new means sealed-combustion, direct-vent appliances, which remove the hazard class entirely. And do not solve this by cracking a window, because the code requires automatic, interlocked makeup air for a reason, and the window solution depends on the cook remembering physics at 7 p.m. on a Tuesday.

What this analysis could not establish: the real-world incidence of hood-induced backdrafting events, because nobody measures it. My depressurization numbers come from a single-zone power-law model assuming whole-house depressurization with interior doors open, the worst case under BPI 1200, and they ignore the hood's own flow sag under duct static pressure (installed CFM typically runs 10 to 30 percent below rating, which softens the numbers modestly). UpCodes' claim that AI plan review catches missing makeup air is vendor marketing until independently audited; no third party has measured its miss rate on residential MEP sets. And the CDC death counts are contextual, not causal. Hood-induced backdrafting has not been isolated as a cause of death in any dataset I could find.

A sentence entered the code in 2009. The industry spent 17 years not reading it, and now the machines do. That is the entire story: a sentence in a code book, physics that never negotiated, and a market finally being forced to meet a requirement it treated as optional. At $269 for the damper, the physics cost nothing to check; the only expensive part was pretending neither existed.
