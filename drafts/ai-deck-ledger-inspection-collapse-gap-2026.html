90% of Deck Collapses Start at One Board. AI Can See Whether Yours Is Bolted or Just Nailed.

By Jake Kowalski · September 7, 2026

Crawl under almost any older deck and look at where it meets the house. That horizontal board, the ledger, is carrying half the deck's weight plus every person standing on it. In a surprising number of American backyards, the only thing holding that board to the house is a row of nails. Nails pull out. Bolts do not. That distinction is the entire ballgame, and almost nobody buying a house ever checks it.

Get the scale of this. The U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission counts roughly 6,000 injuries a year from the structural failure or collapse of decks, porches, railings, and staircases, according to data summarized by remodelers who track the agency's numbers. The International Association of Certified Home Inspectors estimates that about 60 percent of America's 45 million decks have safety issues, and that 90 percent of deck collapses happen at the ledger, the connection to the house, with the deck swinging away from the building as the ledger lets go. Not the railings. Not the stairs. It is the board you never look at.

NADRA, the North American Deck and Railing Association, used its 2026 Deck Safety Month to put a number on the aging fleet: roughly 30 million decks across North America have outlived their practical service life. Many were built before bolted ledger connections became standard, before corrosion-resistant hardware was widely required, and before anybody really understood what pressure-treated lumber does to metal fasteners over decades. More than 75 percent of people involved in a deck collapse sustain significant harm, and in the vast majority of cases the failure was visible and fixable long before anything fell.

What the code actually demands

Modern code is not shy about this connection. IRC Section R507 requires deck ledgers to be fastened to the house band joist with half-inch lag screws or through-bolts, on a spacing schedule that gets tighter as the joist span grows. At a 14-foot joist span, that is a half-inch lag screw every 9 inches, or a through-bolt every 15. Lag screw tips must fully penetrate the inside face of the band joist. Ledgers must be flashed per R703.4 so water never touches the house band joist. And the deck needs lateral-load resistance: hold-down tension devices at two or more locations, each rated at least 1,500 pounds, within two feet of each end of the deck, per the code language NADRA helped write.

Read that list again and picture a 1998 deck built by the previous owner's brother-in-law. Two rows of deck screws at 18 inches. No flashing. No hold-downs. That is not a hypothetical. A Pennsylvania PE who investigates collapses has documented decks attached with a single row of 10d common nails, and others with 2.5-inch deck screws spaced 18 inches apart, both with missing or botched flashing that let moisture rot the structural members for years, completely hidden from the owners. One forensic detail sticks with me: a two-story deck that collapsed at a Fourth of July party with 30 people dancing on it, failed partly because the ledger was fastened to the framing of a cantilevered wall section. Wrong substrate, wrong fasteners, thirty people. Every collapse this engineer has examined was preventable.

The connection nobody photographs

Walk a home inspection and watch what gets photographed. Shingles, gutters, the furnace data plate, the electrical panel. The ledger lives in the dark, six feet off the ground, behind lattice or shrubbery, and inspectors have historically given it a glance at best. Virginia Tech professor emeritus Frank Woeste, who has studied balcony and deck collapses for decades, keeps finding the same root cause: ledgers connected with nails instead of lag bolts or structural screws. His rule of thumb is blunt: 90 percent of deck collapses trace to nailed ledgers, and as few as two people leaning on the wrong rail can pull a nailed deck off the side of a house.

Moisture is the silent partner. Without flashing, water wicks into the ledger-to-house gap and the band joist rots from the inside. You cannot see it from the yard. You can barely see it from underneath. By the time the ledger shows visible decay, the connection is already living on borrowed time. This is the exact failure mode that photo-based AI is starting to catch early: staining patterns, efflorescence, missing flashing, and visible fastener types are all things a camera can see before a human decides to crawl under there.

What the phone can now see

Point your phone camera under the deck and the new inspection tools go to work on the pixels. Simple pitch: snap photos of the ledger, the flashing, and the fasteners, and let vision models flag what a human would need a ladder and a flashlight to notice. Missing flashing above the ledger. Nail heads where bolt heads with washers should be. Discoloration that reads as moisture intrusion. None of this requires new hardware. It requires a camera and a model trained on enough defect photos to know what wrong looks like.

Home inspection software is already moving this direction. In June 2026, Spectora launched AI tools that match an inspector's spoken observations and photos to pre-approved report narratives, with early-access inspectors cutting about 25 percent of their time per inspection and finishing reports on site instead of at the kitchen table that night. Colorado's Alpine Building Performance released a free AI inspection forecaster that predicts likely home issues before an inspector ever visits, aimed at buyers and agents moving fast in hot transactions, profiled by 5280 earlier this year. Loveland's IMGING platform flies drones and handheld phones through the same AI damage-detection pipeline. And the underlying tech keeps getting cheaper: one open-source project runs real-time defect detection with bounding boxes and severity scoring off a phone camera, proof that this is commodity computer vision now, not lab research.

Nobody has shipped a dedicated "ledger scanner" app yet, and that is fine. The general-purpose inspection AI already handles the visible half of the problem. A buyer who runs their listing photos through any competent defect detector before waiving inspection contingencies will catch the missing flashing and the suspicious ledger staining that the listing photographer artfully cropped out.

The case against, at full strength

Now the part the demo videos skip. A photo cannot see inside the ledger-to-band-joist sandwich, and concealed rot inside that sandwich is exactly what killed the decks in the forensic case studies. No vision model can confirm lag screw penetration depth, verify the 1,500-pound hold-downs, or tell you whether that ledger is bolted into solid band joist or the end grain of a cantilevered wall. The most dangerous failures are the ones no camera can reach. Treat any AI verdict of "looks fine" as a guess with good lighting, not a clearance.

Second, the famous 90 percent figure is an industry estimate from InterNACHI, repeated by law firms and remodelers, not a peer-reviewed epidemiological number. The CPSC's 6,000-injuries figure covers decks, porches, railings, and staircases together. The real ledger-specific numbers are murkier than the headlines suggest, which does not make the problem small, it makes the precision of the headline worth questioning. I would rather you read that as intellectual honesty than as doubt about the underlying danger.

Third, false positives have a cost. An AI that flags every weathered board as a structural hazard creates inspection theater: anxious buyers, canceled deals, and contractors upselling full ledger replacements to fix cosmetic aging. A tool that cries wolf trains everyone to ignore the wolf. The good implementations, Spectora's included, keep a human in the loop precisely because the model is an assistant, not an engineer of record.

What to do with this

If you are buying a house with an elevated deck, do the 30-second ledger test before you waive anything. Get under the deck with your phone. Look for bolt heads with washers, not nail heads or screw heads. Look for metal flashing tucked above the ledger where it meets the house. If you see nails, or no flashing, or staining that looks like water has been living in that joint, the deck predates modern code and you want a structural engineer's eyes on it before you close. Download NADRA's free deck evaluation guide and walk the checklist. It costs nothing and it is written for homeowners, not contractors.

Run the money before you panic. A structural engineer deck evaluation runs roughly $300 to $600. A ledger retrofit with proper through-bolts, flashing, and hold-downs runs in the low thousands on a typical deck. A collapse rebuild runs $15,000 to $40,000 or more, and that is before anyone gets hurt. The PE who investigates these failures keeps saying every one was preventable, and the math agrees with him: the retrofit is a rounding error next to the rebuild.

If you run an inspection shop, the lesson from Spectora's numbers is where to spend the saved time. Shaving 25 percent off report writing means nothing if you spend it at the kitchen table. Spend it under the deck with a flashlight and a camera. That is the one place on the property where a half hour of attention buys the most safety per minute, and no AI can do the crawling for you yet. Point the phone, let the model flag the obvious, then put your own eyes on the connection that holds the whole thing to the house. The board has been waiting 30 years for somebody to look at it. Go look.
