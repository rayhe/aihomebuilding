Your Experience Mod Is 1.28. Your Competitor's Is 0.89. That Gap Costs You $38,000 a Year.

By Marcus Washington · September 14, 2026

You lost the bid by two percent. Same lumber, same schedule, same crew size. His number was lower for one reason, and it was not that he works cheaper. His experience mod is 0.89. Yours is 1.28. On a job with real payroll, that decimal point is worth more than your profit.

Few numbers in residential construction are less understood, and none may be more expensive. Your experience modification rate is a multiplier your insurer applies to every workers' comp premium dollar, set by comparing your claims history against what actuaries expected from a contractor of your size doing your kind of work. Run a 1.0 and you pay the manual rate. Run 1.28 and you pay 28 percent more, on every hour, every job, all year. Run 0.89 and the carrier hands you an 11 percent discount your competitor has to beat with actual efficiency.

How the formula actually works

It is built from roughly three years of your loss history, with about a year of lag baked in, which means the number on this fall's renewal is mostly a report card on 2022 through 2024. Every claim gets split at what the rating bureaus call the split point, the dollar line that decides how much of your loss history the formula actually feels. Dollars below that line count as primary losses and land in the formula at full weight, which means every dollar you spend there counts exactly as a dollar against you. Dollars above the line count as excess, and the formula discounts them through credibility factors that shrink their influence as the claim grows. The old nationwide split point sat at $18,500; under the current methodology each state sets its own at roughly 40 percent of its average lost-time claim cost, according to the National Council on Compensation Insurance and a Gallagher summary of the change.

This is the part nobody tells small contractors, and it is the only part of the formula that reaches directly into your wallet. Five $12,000 medical-only claims put $60,000 of fully weighted primary loss into your mod. One $150,000 claim puts roughly one split-point of primary loss in; the rest gets discounted. Same dollars out the door. Wildly different mod. A big accident barely dents it next to a steady drip of small ones, because frequency is the tax and severity is the footnote, and the formula was designed that way on purpose, to punish the contractor whose jobsites keep producing claims more than the one who had a single terrible day.

California just made the base rate hurt more

California's Workers' Compensation Insurance Rating Bureau has the approved advisory pure premium for class 5403, carpentry, at $11.33 per $100 of payroll for policies effective September 2025, and the bureau has proposed $12.52 effective September 2026, a 10.5 percent jump, per the WCIRB base rate comparison. On May 1 the bureau filed for a 10.4 percent average increase across all classes, driven by more cumulative trauma claims and higher medical and legal costs, Insurance Journal reported. That followed an 8.7 percent increase approved for September 2025, the first meaningful upward adjustment in nearly a decade (Apex Risk), which means a contractor renewing this fall pays a base rate nearly a fifth higher than two years ago before the mod even touches the number.

Advisory pure premium is the loss-cost portion before the carrier adds expenses and profit, so your charged rate runs higher, and every point of mod applies to that higher loaded number rather than the pure premium shown here. But the mod applies to the manual premium either way, which means every point of mod swings real money and deserves the same attention you give your lumber quotes.

The bid gate nobody talks about

This is where the number leaves the insurance office and walks onto the job site. Electrical Contractor magazine describes the trend bluntly: owners and GCs increasingly require every contractor on site, down through the lower sub-tiers, to hold an EMR of 1.0 or less, and above 1.0 you may not be allowed to bid at all. Public owners put it in writing. The City of Durham's bid documents demand a written explanation of corrective actions from any bidder above 1.0, which turns your safety record into a writing assignment you complete in front of the procurement officer deciding whether you work next quarter. One energy contractor's prequalification grid requires 1.0 or below for standard approval and triggers a full safety review above 1.25, which in practice means your bid sits in a separate pile while the 0.89 contractors get the call back.

So the 1.28 does double damage: it inflates every bid you submit and locks you out of the rooms where the best work gets awarded, which makes the mod a revenue problem you carry into every estimate rather than an insurance problem you solve at renewal. At 0.89 you get the discount and the invitation, which is another way of saying the mod decides who even gets to compete.

The math, with the work shown

Take a hypothetical Central Valley framing contractor: twenty carpenters, $1.2 million in annual payroll, all in class 5403 at the proposed $12.52 advisory rate. Manual premium: 12,000 times $12.52, which works out to $150,240 a year on a pure-premium basis before any carrier loads a dime of expense on top. At a 1.0 mod, that is the number; at 1.25, it is $187,800. That gap is $37,560 a year before the carrier's expense load; loaded at a representative 60 percent for carrier expenses and profit, the annual swing approaches $60,000.

On a single bid: a $500,000 framing package at 35 percent labor means $175,000 of payroll and roughly $21,910 of manual premium. The 1.25 bidder carries about $5,478 more insurance cost on that one job than the 1.0 bidder, before either of them has bought a single stick of lumber. Framing margins live around 3 to 6 percent, which makes that $5,478 the whole ballgame: on a $500,000 package a 4 percent margin is $20,000, and handing $5,478 of it to your insurer before the first wall goes up is a handicap no estimator can value-engineer away.

What actually moves the number

Because the formula weights frequency, the highest-leverage work is unglamorous: the work that prevents the $8,000 staple-gun laceration and the $11,000 ladder ankle, which means housekeeping, ladder discipline, material handling, and a nurse triage line that keeps a cut finger a first-aid visit instead of an ER claim. Return-to-work programs matter because a lost-time claim costs the mod more than a medical-only one, since the formula reads days away from work as a severity signal, which makes getting an injured carpenter back on light duty arithmetic rather than charity.

This is the opening the AI safety vendors are walking through, because the mod's obsession with small frequent claims is a problem statement a camera system can plausibly claim to solve. CompScience, which raised a $27.6 million Series B in February 2025, runs computer vision over a contractor's existing site cameras to flag hazards before they become claims, and claims a 23 percent injury-rate reduction with partners including Swiss Re and Nationwide. All vendor numbers with no independent audit behind them, and the company's push into construction is new enough that contractor results do not exist yet. But notice what the product sells: fewer small incidents, which is to say fewer primary losses, the exact input the mod weights most heavily, so the vendor's pitch and the actuary's formula are aimed at the same pile of dollars. The percentages are unproven, but the targeting is actuarially literate.

The strongest case against all of this

Now the part the insurance industry will not put in its brochures. Experience rating rewards fewer reported claims, not fewer injuries. A contractor can hold a 0.89 two ways: by preventing injuries, or by making sure injuries never become claims, and the formula cannot tell the difference, which is the structural flaw no rate filing will ever fix because the bureau only sees the claims contractors choose to report. Discourage reporting, route the hurt worker to the clinic off the books, lean on the crew's loyalty to keep quiet, and the mod improves on paper while the job itself gets more dangerous for everyone on it. Every safety consultant has seen the version where the mod is a fiction and the workers pay for it in untreated backs and knees, which is why the most honest safety people in this business will tell you the mod is a useful lie: useful because it moves money, a lie because it measures paperwork as much as protection.

Layer AI cameras over that incentive and think about what changes. A worker who knows every lift is scored may hide the small strain, exactly the strain that, reported early, stays a medical-only claim and, hidden, becomes the surgery that blows up next year's mod. The number measures claims history, and claims history is partly, sometimes largely, a measure of reporting culture rather than of actual safety. A low EMR is evidence of a safe contractor the way a low crime rate is evidence of a safe neighborhood, sometimes true and sometimes just an uncooperative precinct. Treat the number as a signal, never as proof.

What this analysis does not prove

Dollar figures here use advisory pure premium rates, which exclude carrier expenses, profit, schedule credits, and dividends, so real premiums vary. Split-point mechanics come from NCCI materials; California runs its own WCIRB plan, so formula details differ though the frequency-first principle holds across the translation. CompScience's figures are company-reported, unverified by any third party I could find, and its construction results are not yet published. That worked example is illustrative, a single-class payroll few contractors actually have, and the 3 to 6 percent framing margin is an industry rule of thumb rather than a surveyed figure. And the three-year lookback means nothing you do this quarter moves the number before 2028 or so. Anyone selling a fast fix is selling something else.

What to do Monday morning

Pull your mod worksheet from your agent and read the actual loss runs, not just the number. Sort your last three years of claims by frequency, not size, and attack the top of that list. Price a 24/7 nurse triage line; keeping one ER visit a first-aid case can pay for the year. Build a real return-to-work program with light-duty tasks defined before anyone gets hurt. If you are shopping vision-AI safety tools, demand construction-specific results and a reference customer whose mod actually moved, not a percentage on a slide. And if you are a homeowner about to sign a $400,000 contract, ask the GC for the EMR, with the caveat above attached. A 1.4 with no explanation is a louder warning than any online review.
