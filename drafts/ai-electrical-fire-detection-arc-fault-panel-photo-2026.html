Electrical Safety

Your Panel’s Breakers Haven’t Tripped in 40 Years. An AI Can Tell You Why From One Photo.

By Jake Kowalski · September 7, 2026

Somewhere in a 1970s ranch house right now, electricity is jumping a gap it was never supposed to jump. A wire loosened in a backstabbed outlet in 1998. A breaker bus bar has been slowly oxidizing for three decades. Each time current crosses the gap, it superheats the air to a few thousand degrees for a fraction of a second. You cannot hear it. You cannot smell it. Your smoke detector will not notice until the wall cavity is already involved.

This is arcing, and it is the heat source in 73 percent of home fires involving electrical distribution and lighting equipment, according to the National Fire Protection Association. Your electrical system has exactly one job in this scenario: the breaker is supposed to see the fault and trip. Here is the part that should bother you. In millions of American homes, the breakers were built by companies that were later caught faking the safety tests, and nobody ever made anyone take them out.

46,652
Home electrical fires per year in the U.S. (2020–2024 average), per the NFPA. 527 civilian deaths. $2.234 billion in direct property damage. 77 percent traced to electrical failure or malfunction.

The Outlet That Listens

A company called Whisker Labs makes a gadget the size of a chunky smart plug. You stick it in any standard outlet, connect it to Wi-Fi, and give it about a week to learn the electrical personality of your house. Then it samples your home’s voltage 30 million times per second, hunting for the high-frequency noise signature of arcing, the electrical equivalent of a doctor listening for a heart murmur.

When it finds something ugly, it does not just push a notification and wish you luck. A human on Ting’s fire-safety team calls you, walks you through flipping breakers one at a time to isolate the fault, and the subscription includes $1,000 in labor credit toward a licensed electrician to fix whatever they find. PCWorld’s review found it could even identify which device or fixture was arcing. That is the difference between a sensor and a service.

The company’s 2023 whitepaper, released at the NFPA Conference, claims the system prevents 80 percent of home electrical fires, based on more than 4,100 documented hazard cases across 265,000 monitored homes. Its headline stat: 1 in 68 homes experiences an electrical fire hazard every year, from wiring infrastructure, devices, appliances, or utility-side faults. Homes between 40 and 60 years old are 25 percent of the monitored base but account for 33 percent of the wiring hazards.

1 in 68
Share of homes that experience an electrical fire hazard each year, per Whisker Labs’ 2023 analysis of 265,000 monitored homes. About 60 percent of detected hazards trace to faulty wiring; 40 percent to something plugged into an outlet.

Your insurer already knows about this. State Farm has offered more than a million free Ting sensors to policyholders, with three years of service fees covered, and says over 200,000 insured homes are now monitored. The company reports its system flags and mitigates roughly 250 fire hazards a month. When an insurance giant starts giving away hardware, it is because the actuaries ran the numbers and the hardware is cheaper than the claims.

The Panels With a Rap Sheet

Now the part about your breaker panel. If your house was built between the 1950s and the 1980s, there is a real chance the gray metal box in your garage says Federal Pacific Electric, Stab-Lok, Zinsco, Sylvania, or GTE-Sylvania on it. Electricians have a nickname for these. They call them fire starters, and they are not exaggerating for effect.

Federal Pacific’s Stab-Lok breakers have a documented history of failing to trip during overloads and short circuits, which is the one thing a breaker exists to do. The company’s own testing story is a scandal: after FPE was sold in 1979, the new owner told the Consumer Product Safety Commission the Stab-Lok breakers did not meet Underwriters Laboratories requirements despite carrying UL labels. CPSC testing confirmed the failures. A New Jersey court later found the company had knowingly distributed breakers that were not tested to UL standards as their labels claimed. The CPSC then closed its investigation, citing lack of budget, and the panels stayed in the walls.

Zinsco panels have their own failure mode. Their breakers are known to melt and fuse to the bus bar, which means they physically cannot trip no matter how much current flows. Both brands were installed in millions of homes. Neither was ever formally recalled. Production ended around 1980, but electricians kept installing leftover stock for years.

Home inspectors flag these panels on sight. Many insurers now refuse to write policies on homes that have them, or charge higher premiums until they are replaced. A licensed electrician will swap a residential panel for $3,500 to $5,000 in most markets, usually in a single day. Next to the cost of rebuilding a burned garage, it is the cheapest insurance you will ever buy.

Aluminum branch wiring deserves the same paragraph. Popular in the 1960s and 70s as a cheap copper substitute, aluminum connections deteriorate over time, resistance climbs, and the result is overheating at outlets and splices. The CPSC recommends aluminum wiring be replaced or repaired by a qualified electrician. If your inspection report mentions it and the seller shrugged, the shrug was the most expensive part of the transaction.

The Showing-Table Trick

Here is where AI enters the story for buyers. Real estate agents have started photographing breaker panels at showings, three shots, door closed, breakers visible with the dead front open, and the manufacturer label legible, then feeding the photos to a vision model with a structured prompt. The model reads the brand, flags FPE, Zinsco, or Sylvania hardware, and estimates replacement cost for the market. One agent’s published AI showing toolkit walks through exactly this workflow, built for the moment before a buyer writes an offer.

Used correctly, this is genuinely useful triage. A buyer looking at a 1968 split-level can learn, before spending $600 on a full inspection, that the panel alone is a $4,000 line item and a likely insurance headache. That changes the offer math. It also changes the negotiation, because “the panel is a documented fire hazard that three insurers will surcharge” is a stronger credit ask than “the electrical looks old.”

Used incorrectly, it is a liability machine. The toolkit’s own disclaimer says it plainly: identifying a panel brand does not confirm a defect, and none of this substitutes for a licensed electrician’s in-person inspection. Vision models misidentify hardware. A 2026 photo of a panel with a replacement interior can read as original equipment. And telling a seller their panel is dangerous based on a chatbot’s opinion is a fast way to blow up a transaction over something an electrician would have cleared in twenty minutes. Triage is triage. It tells you where to point the professional, not what the professional will find.

What the AI Can’t Do

Time for the cold water, because this story has two claims that deserve a hard stare.

First, the 80 percent prevention figure. It comes from a company whitepaper, not a peer-reviewed trial, and it is calculated over the company’s own monitored homes, which are self-selected early adopters who then receive human follow-up and free electrician credit. That is a bundled intervention, not a pure sensor effect, and the counterfactual, what would have burned without the sensor, is modeled, not observed. The underlying hazard data is still valuable. The headline percentage should be read as marketing with a dataset attached.

Second, one sensor in one outlet is not a whole-house CT scan. Arc signatures attenuate across branch circuits, and a fault on a distant circuit presents a fainter signal than one nearby. The system compensates with sensitive detection and human review, which is exactly why the human review exists. But a renter who plugs a Ting into a bedroom outlet should not assume the detached garage workshop is equally covered.

Third, arc-fault protection already exists in a dumber, cheaper form. AFCI breakers have been required by code in new residential construction for a quarter century, and they trip on arc signatures at the panel. The gap is not technology. The gap is the roughly 140 million existing homes, most of which will never get AFCI retrofits because nobody opens the panel until something goes wrong. Sensors and photo triage are patches for a housing stock that predates the fix.

None of this makes the tools useless. It makes them what they are: a $49-a-year early warning system that is dramatically better than the nothing most homes currently have, and a free pre-offer screening trick that is dramatically better than the nothing most buyers currently do. “Better than nothing” is not a slogan, but in a category where the baseline is literally nothing, it is the whole business case.

What to Do This Week

Find your panel and read the label. Open the panel door, do not remove the dead front, that part stays on unless you are an electrician, and photograph the manufacturer label and the breaker handles. If it says Federal Pacific, FPE, Stab-Lok, Zinsco, Sylvania, or GTE-Sylvania, you have a documented hazard. Budget $3,500 to $5,000 for a replacement and get two quotes from licensed electricians. Tell your insurer after the swap, not before; some carriers surcharge the old panel, others reward the new one.

Ask your insurer about a Ting. State Farm policyholders can get the sensor and three years of monitoring free, plus the $1,000 electrician credit. If you are with another carrier, the retail subscription runs about $49 a year, which is less than the deductible on any claim you will ever file. Plug it into a standard outlet, not a surge protector or power strip, and give it a week to learn your house.

Check for aluminum branch wiring while you are at it. If your home was built between 1965 and 1975 and the inspection mentioned aluminum wiring, get an electrician’s opinion on COPALUM crimp or AlumiConn pigtailing versus full rewire. This is not a DIY project and not a handyman project. Full stop.

Buying a house built before 1990? Make the panel photo part of the showing. Three photos, brand legible, run them through a vision model with a disciplined prompt, and treat the output as a question for your inspector and electrician, not an answer. If the model flags a Stab-Lok or Zinsco panel, your offer should already include the replacement number before the inspection contingency even starts.

Do not wait for the AFCI retrofit that is never coming. If your panel predates arc-fault requirements and a full panel swap is not in the budget this year, ask your electrician about AFCI breakers for bedroom circuits specifically. NFPA data shows bedrooms are the most common room of origin. Protect the rooms where people sleep first.

Limitations

Fire statistics come from NFPA’s 2026 “Home Electrical Fires” report covering 2020–2024, built on NFIRS incident data and the NFPA fire experience survey. Incident-based fire data undercounts unreported fires and misclassifies some causes; the figures are the best available national estimates, not a census. Whisker Labs’ 1-in-68 and 80-percent figures are company-published, derived from its monitored-home population, and have not been independently replicated in peer review. State Farm program details reflect the carrier’s published offer and may vary by state and eligibility. Panel replacement costs ($3,500–$5,000) reflect 2026 pricing from licensed electricians in major metro markets; costs vary with panel amperage, service upgrades, and local permit fees. Nothing in this article substitutes for an in-person evaluation by a licensed electrician. Do not remove a panel dead front yourself.
