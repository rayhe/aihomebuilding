40% of Homeowners Can’t Pick a Paint Color. The AI That Picks for Them Chose the Same One for Everyone.

By Elena Vasquez · July 30, 2026

Sherwin-Williams sells 1,700 paint colors, Behr sells 2,000, and Benjamin Moore catalogs 3,500. Combined, the three largest residential paint manufacturers in America offer homeowners roughly 7,200 ways to make a room their own, a number so vast it should guarantee that no two living rooms in the country look quite alike.

Increasingly, they look exactly alike. Agreeable Gray, Accessible Beige, Repose Gray, Edgecomb Gray. Scroll through any real estate listing site in any metro area, and the interiors blur into a single, warm-toned wash that designers have started calling "the greige consensus," a narrow band of the color spectrum where safety feels indistinguishable from taste.

That convergence used to be driven by Pinterest boards and HGTV, but now it has an engine.

40%
Share of homeowners who say the inability to choose a paint color prevents them from starting projects at all, according to a study commissioned by Behr Paint Company.

The Decision That Paralyzes

In August 2025, Behr and Google Cloud announced ChatHUE, a conversational AI agent trained on Behr's proprietary color data and built on Google's Gemini, Vertex AI, BigQuery, and Cloud Run infrastructure. The premise was rooted in a statistic that the paint industry has known for years but never quite solved: 40% of consumers identify color selection as the single most difficult step in a paint project, and 40% say that difficulty stops them from starting the project entirely. Behr's chief marketing officer, Andy Lopez, described the goal plainly: "turn a daunting decision into an exciting, personalized discovery."

ChatHUE is not alone. Sherwin-Williams launched Color Expert, an AI-powered app whose core feature reads a photograph of your room and recommends colors based on what it sees. Upload a picture of your kitchen with white cabinets and oak floors, and the algorithm scans the existing palette and serves you hues it calculates will complement those materials. Sue Wadden, Sherwin-Williams' Director of Color Marketing, told Homes and Gardens that homeowners consult "over ten touchpoints" before committing to a color, and the app is designed to collapse that process into a single session. Pantone released its own AI Palette Generator built on Microsoft Azure, letting users query things like "what colors evoke optimism in Gen Z" and receive palettes grounded in Pantone's trend forecasting articles.

Those tools are genuinely useful, and nobody disputes that 7,200 options is too many or that decision paralysis kills paint projects. But the tools are also, by design, consensus machines.

What "Complement" Actually Means

When Sherwin-Williams' Color Expert reads your room and recommends a wall color, it is not designing a space. It is solving an optimization problem: which color, from a library of 1,700, will generate the lowest visual friction against the objects already present? That calculation depends on what furniture, flooring, and fixtures Americans actually buy, which is to say it depends on what is mass-produced and mass-marketed: grey sectionals from West Elm, white quartz countertops, light oak engineered hardwood, matte black fixtures. Every upload feeds the algorithm.

A study published in January 2026 in Color Research & Application by researchers at Hanyang University in Seoul illuminated the mechanism. Semin Jin and Kyung Hoon Hyun built a vision-language model that predicts color compatibility for objects in interior design images, then tested it with 16 working designers. Eighty-one percent of them reported that the AI's predicted compatibility scores "aligned with aesthetic principles with which they were familiar." What the authors presented as validation is also a confession: the AI learned what designers already agree on, and now it is teaching it back to everyone else. Alignment with existing consensus is not the same thing as good design. It is the same thing as average design, delivered with mathematical confidence.

Behr's ChatHUE operates on a different mechanism but converges on the same endpoint. Trained on "extensive proprietary color data" and "data-driven market trends," it guides consumers through a conversational flow whose recommendations are grounded in what has worked before, where "worked" means "sold well" or "tested well in consumer research." The tool does not ask what the homeowner wants to feel in their living room at seven in the evening in November. It asks what project they are doing and what aesthetic they prefer, then maps those inputs to a curated subset of Behr's catalog that the data says performs best for that combination. Performance, in this context, means popularity, and popularity in a feedback loop means more popularity.

The Feedback Loop Nobody Is Measuring

Here is what none of these companies publish: the distribution of their AI recommendations. Of the 1,700 colors Sherwin-Williams sells, how many does Color Expert actually suggest in a given month? Of Behr's 2,000, how many does ChatHUE steer consumers toward? Without that data, the convergence thesis remains directional rather than proven, but the structural incentives all point the same way. An algorithm trained on popularity will recommend popular colors. Popular colors become more popular because the algorithm recommends them. The tail of the distribution, the 1,200 colors nobody asks about and the algorithm never suggests, becomes commercially invisible even while remaining technically available.

This is not merely a paint problem; it is the same dynamic Spotify's recommendation engine produces in music, where 30% of all streams go to the top 1% of tracks, and the same dynamic Amazon's "customers also bought" creates in retail, where algorithmic recommendation concentrates demand on a narrowing set of products. The mechanism is platform-agnostic: when an optimization engine mediates choice, it narrows choice, because narrowing is what optimization does.

For residential construction, the implications are specific. Paint is the cheapest design decision a homeowner makes, at roughly $50 per gallon for Behr Marquee and $85 for Sherwin-Williams Emerald. For the cost of a mid-range dinner, a homeowner can transform a room in ways that no other building material matches at that price point. Color is the one dimension of home design where individual expression costs almost nothing, where the gap between builder-grade anonymity and genuine personal statement is measured in a different shade rather than a different budget. When AI collapses that dimension into algorithmic consensus, it eliminates the one form of architectural individuality that was accessible to everyone.

The Counterargument Deserves Full Strength

The best case for AI color selection is that 40% of homeowners not starting projects is a real and measured problem, and that a tool which gets them off the couch and into a paint store creates value even if it recommends safe choices. A homeowner who paints their walls Accessible Beige because ChatHUE suggested it has a better-looking home than a homeowner who stares at 2,000 swatches for three months and then paints nothing. Decision paralysis is not a theoretical concern; it is quantified by the paint industry's own research and confirmed by the ten-plus touchpoints that Sherwin-Williams documented. If the alternative to algorithmic color selection is no color selection, the algorithm wins.

Furthermore, ChatHUE is conversational rather than dictatorial. Users can reject suggestions, ask for alternatives, and specify preferences that push the algorithm away from its default recommendations. Color Expert shows the user multiple options, not a single prescription. Pantone's tool explicitly lets users define their own aesthetic parameters. These are brainstorming tools, not prescriptions, and blaming them for homogeneity is like blaming a dictionary for clichés.

That argument is structurally sound and empirically incomplete. A conversational interface that presents five options is not neutral when those five options cluster in the same region of the color space. The user who rejects Agreeable Gray is offered Accessible Beige, not Hale Navy, because the algorithm's confidence interval does not extend to colors that risk visual conflict with the grey sofa it detected in the uploaded photo. Freedom to reject is real, but freedom to discover what the algorithm would never have suggested is not, because that discovery requires the user to know what they are missing, and the entire premise of the tool is that the user does not know what they want.

What to Do With This

If you are choosing a paint color and an AI tool gives you its first suggestion, treat that suggestion the way you would treat the first search result on Google: probably relevant, definitely not the only answer, and shaped by forces that have nothing to do with your taste. The algorithm does not know that you grew up in a house with teal kitchen walls and that color still makes you feel at home. It does not know that you hate beige because your first apartment was beige and you swore you would never live in beige again. It knows what photographs well, what sells well, and what clashes least with the mass-market furniture that populates the majority of uploaded room photos in its training set.

Use the tool to eliminate, not to select. Ask it what clashes with your flooring, not what complements it. The set of colors that will look wrong is smaller, more stable, and more useful than the set of colors the algorithm thinks will look right, because "wrong" is a property of physics and "right" is a property of the person standing in the room.

If you are building or renovating and your builder's design center offers AI-assisted color selection, ask whether the tool recommends from the full catalog or from a curated subset. If the answer is a curated subset, ask who curated it and when it was last updated. A subset chosen by a regional designer who knows your market is a resource. A subset chosen by a national algorithm optimized for sell-through is a filter that removes the colors you might have loved.

If you are a designer, the Hanyang University study should concern you, not because 81% alignment with aesthetic principles is bad, but because it suggests the tool you are evaluating will agree with you. Agreement feels like validation, and in a design process, agreement should feel like a warning that nobody in the room is pushing back.

Limitations

No paint manufacturer has published the distribution of colors recommended by its AI tool, so the convergence thesis is structural and inferred from the optimization architecture rather than directly measured from recommendation outputs. The Behr statistic that 40% of consumers find color selection the most difficult step comes from a third-party study commissioned by Behr, which has a commercial interest in positioning its AI tool as a solution to that difficulty. The Hanyang University study used 16 designers, a small sample whose generalizability to the broader design profession is uncertain. Color trend convergence in American residential interiors preceded AI tools by at least a decade, driven by Pinterest, HGTV, and real estate staging practices, and isolating the incremental effect of AI recommendation from that pre-existing trend is not possible with available data. We did not test any of these AI tools ourselves. The tools may recommend a wider range of colors than the structural analysis suggests, and we have no data to confirm or deny that possibility.
