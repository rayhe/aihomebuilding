The Clock That Stops Buddy-Punching Costs $1,000 Per Worker in Illinois. The Fix Is a Piece of Paper.

By Marcus Washington, September 16, 2026, Workforce & Labor

Five forty-seven in the morning, outside Naperville, and a framer named Luis holds his face up to a tablet bolted to the site trailer. A camera reads him, the screen flashes green, and he is on the clock. Two years ago the same ritual meant a paper sign-in sheet on a clipboard, half the names illegible, three of them written by the same hand. His contractor put up that tablet after one too many paydays covering men who were not there yet. What he did not know, and what most of the contractors buying these clocks still do not know, is that in Illinois each of those morning face scans is a regulated event under the toughest biometric privacy law in the country, and the penalty for getting the paperwork wrong runs $1,000 per worker for a careless mistake and $5,000 per worker if a court decides you knew better.

What makes this story uncomfortable is that the technology is genuinely good at the job it was hired for.

Theft that never makes the invoice

Buddy-punching, one worker clocking in for another, is the oldest payroll fraud in the book, and it survives because the old clocks made it easy. A 2017 survey of 1,000 employees found that 16 percent admitted to clocking in for a colleague, and extrapolating across 78 million hourly workers put the annual cost above $373 million. Worse, the American Payroll Association puts the drain at 2 to 7 percent of gross payroll, with three-quarters of small businesses affected. A 2024 peer-reviewed paper in the Human Resource and Leadership Journal put the average at $1,560 per employee per year, with workers stealing an average of 4.5 hours a week. And it is not fading: a 2026 survey of more than 800 hourly workers found one in four had buddy-punched in the previous twelve months, even though 71 percent knew it was against company policy.

Read that again. A quarter of the hourly workforce committed time fraud last year while knowing the rules. No clipboard ever stood a chance.

A camera that works with gloves on

Here is why the face-scan clock took off on jobsites instead of in offices. Fingerprints fail on construction crews, because workers wear gloves and their hands are covered in concrete dust and sawdust that blinds the sensor, which is why ExakTime built its FaceFront biometrics, in the company's own words, "with contractors in mind," turning a foreman's phone into a photo-verification device that snaps a face, matches it to the file photo, and stamps the GPS coordinates. ClockShark added facial recognition to its construction time-tracking app in 2018, announcing that the feature "uses artificial intelligence to verify the identity of employees" and that unfamiliar faces would alert administrators to possible buddy-punching. You cannot lend somebody your face. That was the entire pitch.

Beyond theft prevention, the appeal runs deeper. ENR noted that federal contractors need exact timekeeping records for their own employees and their subcontractors' crews to comply with the Davis-Bacon Act, and that contractors have had "limited guidance" on the legal implications of collecting biometric data to do it. A rugged tablet at the trailer door gives a GC a defensible audit trail for wage claims, safety headcounts, and certified payroll, and for an industry where the office manager used to spend Monday mornings decoding timesheets, it looked like progress. It was progress, right up until Illinois sent the bill.

In Illinois, your face is worth $1,000

Passed in 2008, the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act remains the only state law that lets workers sue directly over biometric collection, and its demands are simple and absolute. Before collecting a face scan, a company must inform the worker in writing that the biometric is being collected, state the purpose and how long it will be kept, and get a written release. It must publish a retention schedule and destroy the data when the purpose is done. And it may not hand the biometric to a third party, like a payroll vendor, without consent. Break the rules and the statute sets the price: $1,000 per negligent violation, $5,000 per intentional or reckless one, plus the plaintiff's attorney's fees.

For years the terrifying question was how many violations one clock creates. In 2023 the Illinois Supreme Court answered: every scan is a separate claim. One worker scanning in and out twice a day, five days a week, generates hundreds of violations a year, and each one carries its own $1,000 to $5,000 ticket, which is how White Castle's exposure got estimated in the billions and every Illinois employer with a fingerprint or face-scan clock suddenly owned a liability shaped like a lottery jackpot, payable to whoever filed first. Plaintiff firms noticed, and time-clock cases became a cottage industry.

Then the legislature blinked, and in August 2024 Governor Pritzker signed SB 2979, which rewrote the damages math so that collecting the same biometric from the same person by the same method is now a single violation no matter how many times it happens, and disclosing it to the same recipient the same way is likewise one violation. The amendment also made electronic signatures count as a written release, which matters enormously on a jobsite where nobody carries a pen. As one law firm's analysis put it, a single time clock now "typically create[s] two violations per employee" instead of sixty per week, one for the collection and one for the transmission to the payroll vendor. Reed Smith's summary was blunter: the amendment kills the "annihilative liability" that followed the per-scan ruling.

Good news on paper, except the contractors never read the memo, and 2026 is suing them anyway.

This year's lawsuits, already filed

What is arriving in court now is not about office workers. It is about hourly people and the clocks they were told to use.

In September, a proposed federal class action accused Anviz Global of collecting and storing workers' face scans through its biometric timekeeping system without the notice or written consent BIPA requires. According to the complaint, the named plaintiff scanned her face to clock in and out, and nobody ever told her what was collected, why, or for how long. Every scan worked exactly as designed, and the paperwork behind them did not exist.

In April, an Illinois appellate court ruled that holding a government contract does not shield an employer from BIPA claims. Cornerstone Services, the defendant in that case, had tracked employees with a fingerprint-scanning time clock since around 2008. Workers signed a consent form, but the form never mentioned that their fingerprints were being shared with ADP, the payroll vendor. Disclosing biometric data to a third party without telling the worker is its own violation, and no amount of government funding buys an exemption.

And a Crowell & Moring alert this year flagged the pairing that should scare every contractor: a suit against TI Communities that the complaint itself describes as "a wage theft and privacy case." Workers scanned their faces on a system called Worksite to clock in, then were allegedly called back to work after hours and told not to clock back in. Off-the-clock work, overtime miscalculations, and BIPA violations, all in one complaint. A face-scan clock bought to end wage disputes became the evidence locker for one.

A question for the union hall

There is a wrinkle my father would have appreciated, because he carried a union card and read every contract cover to cover. For unionized workers, BIPA claims may not belong in court at all. On exactly this question, the Illinois Supreme Court took up Walton v. Roosevelt University, while the federal courts have been consistent: where a collective bargaining agreement gives management the right to direct the workforce, disputes over biometric timekeeping go through the grievance procedure, not a class action. The Seventh Circuit held as much for airline and food-plant workers whose unions had bargained over the clocks.

This is how it is supposed to work: across the table, the union reviews the device, negotiates the data rules, and the workers get a voice in how their faces are handled instead of a settlement check three years later. Meanwhile the non-union residential crew gets neither the bargaining nor, usually, the notice. Something to think about the next time someone tells you the open shop is the free market at work.

Now the math, because arithmetic is the whole story

Take a 25-person residential crew in the Chicago suburbs, and picture a face-scan kiosk at the trailer: two scans per worker per day across 250 workdays, which comes to 12,500 scans a year.

Under the old per-scan rule, with sloppy consent paperwork, the exposure was 12,500 scans times $1,000: $12.5 million for negligence, $62.5 million if a court found the violations reckless. A number like that ends a residential GC. It ends the company, the trucks, the name on the trailer.

Apply the 2024 amendment and the same year collapses to per-person math: 25 workers times two violations each, one for collecting the scan and one for sending it to the payroll vendor, times $1,000. That is $50,000 for negligence, $250,000 at the reckless tier. Springfield cut the exposure by a factor of 250, and the contractors still have not read the memo.

Now set that against the theft the clock stops. Twenty-five workers at $34 an hour, 2,000 hours a year, is a $1.7 million payroll. Take the Payroll Association's range, which says buddy-punching drains 2 to 7 percent of gross payroll, and use the conservative end: the clock saves $34,000 a year. Run those numbers and the fix literally costs more than the disease, because with botched onboarding the $50,000 privacy exposure is half again the theft it prevents.

But with a proper written release, signed before the first scan, purpose and retention period disclosed, e-signature now legally sufficient, the exposure drops to roughly zero and the clock wins cleanly. All of it, a $50,000 swing on a 25-person crew, comes down to a form handed out at orientation. Nobody at the supply house mentions this when they sell you the kiosk.

In defense of the clock

Fairness requires the other side, and the other side is strong, because most of it comes from the workers themselves.

Start with the honest framer who shows up at 5:47 and watches the late guy get credited for the same hours, because buddy-punching is theft from the crew before it is theft from the boss, and over a year that quiet subsidy comes out of raises, bonuses, and overtime that might have existed. Accurate clocks protect the workers who actually work. On Davis-Bacon jobs the law demands accurate records, and a GC who cannot produce them faces fines that make BIPA look polite. Most contractors who buy these clocks are not harvesting faces for fun; they are trying to run an honest payroll, and for the ones who collect proper consent, BIPA is a paperwork exercise, not a technology problem.

There is also the awkward fact that BIPA's private right of action mostly enriches the plaintiffs' bar. Workers in time-clock settlements typically receive a few hundred dollars apiece while the firms collect millions in fees, which means the law's loudest beneficiaries are not the scanned. And the statute punishes notice failures, not the technology; a face-scan clock with a proper release is perfectly legal, which is exactly why the compliance fix is a form instead of a bonfire.

None of that changes the math; it only changes who gets blamed when the math goes wrong.

Four steps, one afternoon

If you run a crew in Illinois and there is a biometric clock on your trailer, or you are thinking about buying one, here is the checklist, and it costs less than one hour of a lawyer's time.

First, publish the policy: BIPA requires a written retention schedule and destruction plan, public and specific, with biometric data destroyed when the employment relationship ends or the purpose is fulfilled, whichever comes first, because "we keep it in the app" is not a policy.

Second, get the release before the first scan: written, informed, and signed before the camera ever sees the worker, stating what is collected, why, and how long you keep it, and since 2024 an electronic signature counts, so there is no excuse involving clipboards.

Third, name the vendor: if face data flows to a payroll company, name that company in the consent, because the Cornerstone case exists as a precedent precisely where a form mentioned the clock and never mentioned ADP, and each undisclosed disclosure is its own violation.

Fourth, check the contract: in a union shop, read the collective bargaining agreement before the lawyers read it for you, since the grievance procedure may be the exclusive remedy and the union may have already bargained the clock's terms, while in a non-union shop you have no such shield, which is one more thing the open shop does not tell you.

What I could not prove

I am a reporter, not a lawyer, and nothing here is legal advice. Whether the 2024 amendment applies retroactively to scans taken before August 2024 is still being litigated, with strong arguments on both sides, so old clocks carry old questions. Nobody publishes buddy-punching rates specifically for residential construction crews, so the theft math uses economy-wide figures from the Payroll Association and the 2026 worker survey; your framing crew may be more honest than the average warehouse. Treat the $200 kiosk price and the $34 wage as illustrative rather than quotes, since device costs vary by vendor and volume. Other states' biometric laws, Texas, Washington, California's treatment of biometrics as sensitive personal information, are real but narrower than Illinois, and I did not analyze them in depth. Illinois is where the exposure lives, which is where I did the math.

No one disputes that the clock is honest. Nobody ever questioned whether the technology works; the question is whether the company that bought it did the paperwork, because in Illinois the most expensive words on a jobsite are the ones nobody said at orientation.
