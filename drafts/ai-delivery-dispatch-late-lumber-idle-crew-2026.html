Your Framing Crew Stood Around for Four Hours Waiting on Lumber. The Delivery App Knew It Would Be Late.

By Frank DeLuca · September 4, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Lumber was promised for 7 AM. At 7:40 the lead carpenter called the yard and got the particular silence of a man looking at a screen that says nothing useful. At 9:15 the yard said the truck was "loading," and at 11:30 it was "ten minutes out," which is what dispatchers say when the truck is not ten minutes out. It arrived at 2 PM. Four framers had been on the clock since 7, at full wages, producing exactly nothing for seven hours, and when the invoice for that week arrived, the idle time was nowhere on it, because it never is. Nobody bills for standing around. The money just quietly leaves through a hole nobody measures.

Twenty years of running residential projects has taught me that the most expensive problems are the ones with no line item. A change order gets argued about, with redlines and meetings and bad feelings. A late delivery gets shrugged about, and the shrug is worth more than the argument.

Fifteen percent of the day, gone

Researchers have been measuring this hole for decades. Work-sampling studies find that construction workers spend roughly 15 percent of their time waiting for materials and equipment, a figure the authors attribute to poor supply chain management (Khanh et al., via an MDPI Buildings review). A meta-analysis put the share of construction operative time devoted to outright wasteful activity at 49.6 percent (Horman and Kenley, 2005, via Virginia Tech), and observers with clipboards on seventeen building projects in Chile found 53 percent of total working time going to non-productive activity, with waiting at 9 percent and lack of materials accounting for 12 percent of the waiting (Serpell et al., 1997). A Brazilian delay-factor study found late material delivery loading at 0.866 on the supply-management factor, which explained more than a fifth of all schedule variance (SciELO).

None of these studies were conducted on American single-family jobsites, which is worth saying plainly. Residential work is less professionalized than the commercial projects researchers prefer to study, with thinner supervision and more informal ordering, so if anything the residential numbers are worse. But "worse" is my inference, not their finding, and I will not dress it up as data.

Run the arithmetic anyway, because even the conservative version stings. Take a four-person framing crew at a fully loaded cost of about $65 an hour per worker, wages plus workers comp plus the truck and the small tools, which comes to $260 an hour for the crew. A delivery that slips four hours burns $1,040 in wages for zero production. Stretch the 15 percent waiting figure across a six-month build and you get something like 150 dead hours per worker, or roughly $10,000 per worker per project, spent waiting. Your GC absorbs it, buries it in the next bid, and charges you for it anyway, without either of you ever naming it.

The software finally noticed

For years the logistics side of residential construction ran on phone calls and a whiteboard at the lumberyard. That is changing, and the new tools are attacking the problem from three directions at once.

First, the order itself. Toolbx launched an AI order-entry system this month that reads a contractor's material list, quote, or takeoff and converts it into a draft dealer order in under a minute, extracting the customer, the job site, the requested date, and the delivery-or-will-call instructions, then matching line items against the dealer's SKU catalog. The company expects match rates above 95 percent and integrates with the ERP systems lumberyards actually run, including Epicor BisTrack and ECI Spruce, and a sixth-generation Alabama and Florida dealer called Swift Supply is testing it in early access. Mis-keyed orders are a genuine root cause of wrong deliveries, and this is the first product I have seen that treats the lumberyard's order desk as the bottleneck it is.

Second, the truck. Dispatch's DispatchOne is an AI orchestration layer for building-product distributors that routes on live traffic, driver location, delivery windows, and order priority, matches the vehicle to the load, and gives the contractor real-time tracking with live ETAs and photo proof of delivery. It also reports what delivery actually costs, on-time rates and cost per stop, which is the kind of data most dealers have never seen about their own operation. DispatchTrack does the same job specifically for non-bulk construction materials, lumber and drywall, with GPS tracking, automated customer alerts, and a "Driver AI" that feeds the driver jobsite access instructions per stop, because half of late deliveries are really lost deliveries wearing a different excuse.

Third, the jobsite. Veyor coordinates deliveries at the site itself: booking windows for suppliers and subs, gate coordination, real-time updates on what is arriving when. This is the unglamorous half of the problem, and it may be the more important half. An optimized route means nothing if the truck arrives to a locked gate, no forklift, and nobody who ordered the material within shouting distance.

What the software cannot fix

Now the part where I argue against the brochure, because twenty years will do that to you.

AI order entry fixes the dealer's keystroke problem. It does not fix the driver's traffic problem, the yard being out of stock on the specified LVLs, or the wrong SKU getting picked because two products share a bin. Toolbx and Dispatch solve different halves of the chain, and a dealer running one without the other has fixed the paperwork while the truck still wanders. Vendor claims about match rates and on-time improvements are vendor claims. I found no independent audit of any of these platforms' field results, and until a third party rides along with the trucks, the numbers are marketing.

There is also a structural truth the software cannot touch: residential jobsites are the worst customers in the delivery business. Small drops, tight streets, no receiving dock, no forklift half the time, and a super who is on the roof when the truck arrives. The routing algorithm can be perfect and still lose to a cul-de-sac with cars parked on both sides. Veyor's jobsite-coordination angle may matter more for residential than all the route optimization in the world, because the last fifty feet are where residential deliveries go to die.

And the economics cut both ways. These are dealer-side SaaS products: the dealer pays, the dealer benefits, and the benefit accrues partly as the dealer's margin, not yours. Nothing about AI dispatch obligates your lumberyard to pass the savings to your bid. Which brings us to the part you can actually control.

Put the delivery in the contract

If you are building or managing a build, three demands cost you nothing and change the dynamic completely.

First, delivery windows in writing, with a start and an end time. "Tuesday" is not a window, it is a rumor. "Tuesday 7 to 9 AM" is a commitment you can plan a crew around, and a commitment creates accountability when it breaks. Second, a live tracking link shared with the superintendent before the truck rolls, the same way you would track a pizza, because the technology exists and there is no longer any excuse for the 11:30 "ten minutes out." Third, a missed-window remedy in the supply agreement: a discount on the load, or priority re-dispatch at the dealer's expense. Dealers will agree to this more readily than you expect, because the dealers buying this software finally have the data to know which of their own trucks are the problem.

Ask your GC what the delivery terms are on the current job. If the answer is a shrug, you have just found the hole the money leaves through. It is worth about $1,040 every time a truck runs four hours late, and it has been leaving through that hole on every project you have ever built.

Limitations

The 15 percent waiting figure and the 49.6 percent waste figure come from commercial and international studies, not US residential jobsites, so applying them to a single-family build is an extrapolation, and I have labeled it as one. The loaded labor rate of $65 an hour is a blended national-ish assumption: union coastal markets run far higher and rural markets lower, which moves the $1,040 figure in both directions. The Brazilian delay study's variance figure describes its own sample of projects, not yours.

Vendor capabilities for Toolbx, Dispatch, DispatchTrack, and Veyor are described from company materials and trade-press coverage. No independent field audit of their on-time or accuracy claims was found, and no per-delivery or per-dealer pricing was publicly available, so this article makes no claim about what the software costs or what ROI a dealer actually earns. The 150-hour waiting extrapolation assumes the 15 percent rate holds steady across a whole build, which it will not; framing and MEP rough-in are more delivery-sensitive than finishes.
