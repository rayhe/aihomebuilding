Your AI Drew a Beautiful Floor Plan in Nine Minutes. It Still Costs $6,500 to Build From.

Coffee was still brewing when the plan arrived, three bedrooms and two baths across eighteen hundred square feet, the kitchen pulled tight against the south wall so the morning light would land on the island exactly where it should. Nine minutes earlier it had been a paragraph typed into a browser tab; now it was a drawing, dimensioned and plausible and almost beautiful, priced at twenty dollars a month.

Somewhere inside that drawing there is a version of this story in which the architect is finished. A machine produced the plan in nine minutes, the subscription costs less than a pizza, and the design fee, that ancient percentage skimmed off everything you build, finally cracks open, so sit with that version a moment, because it is the version the pricing pages want you to inhabit, and it fails the way a badly framed wall fails, not at the surface but at the connections, where the drawing meets the ground.

Start with the numbers nobody argues about, since a licensed architect designing a custom home typically charges five to fifteen percent of construction cost, and the National Association of Home Builders puts average new-home construction at $162 per square foot. For a 2,000-square-foot house, that is $324,000 of construction and, at a middle-of-the-road eight percent fee, $25,920 for the design. Sources: Coohom's fee breakdown citing NAHB, and The House Plan Company's stock-versus-custom analysis citing the NAHB 2024 Construction Cost Survey.

Now the machine: Maket, the most polished of the current AI floor-plan generators, charges $20 a month for its Homeowner plan: 300 credits, with each floor plan generation costing 20 credits. Fifteen plans a month, about $1.33 per layout. A competing tool, Floor Plan AI, prices its starter tier at $9.99 a month for 200 credits, with basic generations costing a single credit each, roughly five cents a plan. Per-drawing cost has fallen by something like 99 percent in two years, which is the kind of number that makes people stop reading before they reach the catch.

Here is the catch, and it is structural: an architectural fee is not one fee but roughly five phases, and schematic design, the phase the AI has conquered, is about fifteen percent of the total under the standard industry phase breakdown. Fifteen percent of $25,920 is $3,888, and AI does that slice for $60, three months of subscription, which is genuinely remarkable and also the entire extent of the disruption. Everything after, design development, construction documents, structural coordination, code compliance, the licensed stamp, costs exactly the same whoever sketched the first plan, because none of it is sketching. Maket says this outright in its own FAQ: the AI handles "roughly 70 to 75 percent of the schematic floor plan phase," and "your architect still stamps the permit set." Credit the company for honesty its marketing department keeps trying to bury.

Run the full comparison and the price war looks different. For that same 2,000-square-foot house, four paths from idea to permit-ready drawings:

Read the table twice, because the surprise is not where the ads point. Against the full architect fee, the AI path saves about seventy-four percent, which is real money. Against a stock plan, the AI path costs $2,800 MORE. Stock plans ship with construction documents included, the unglamorous sheets the plan checker actually reads: sections, details, structural notes, energy calculations. AI plans ship with none of that. As one industry reviewer put it, the paid tier's human-validated uploads exist precisely because "the automated output is not a drawing you build from." You are comparing a sketch to a set of drawings and calling it a price war.

Then there is the ownership question, which no pricing page mentions. In March 2023 the U.S. Copyright Office issued guidance reiterating that copyright requires human authorship: if a work's traditional elements of authorship were produced by a machine, the Office will not register it (88 Fed. Reg. 16190, summarized in the Office's 2024 update letter). Architectural plans are copyrightable when a human designs them. A purely AI-generated plan, prompted into existence in nine minutes, sits outside that protection. Buy a $1,800 stock plan and you receive a license to build from it. Generate a plan for $1.33 and you receive pixels with no registered rights attached. Whether that matters in a dispute has never been tested in court, which is itself the warning.

What this means for your project

Use the AI for what it is: a schematic engine. Two or three months of subscription, iterate ruthlessly, and arrive at your architect with a plan you already love instead of paying $200 an hour to discover what you hate. That is the Nashville model from the Coohom reporting: a homeowner drafted digitally, then hired an architect for hourly consult plus permit plans, landing at $5,600 total, about half what her neighbor paid for full-service design on a similar house.

Budget the permit phase separately and first, because the stamp, the structural engineering, and the energy calculations run $5,000 to $8,000 depending on your market, owed no matter who drew the first line. If that number surprises you, the AI has not saved you anything yet.

Price the stock plan before the AI subscription when your design is close to standard. $3,800 all-in with documents beats $6,660 without them, every time, unless the customization is the point. HUD research cited by The House Plan Company finds modified stock plans deliver roughly 70 percent of the customization at 25 to 30 percent of custom cost. Know which side of that line your project sits on.

Read the license before you pay: AI generators grant usage rights under their terms of service; none can grant copyright in the output, because under current guidance there is no copyright to grant. And cap the iteration: every regeneration burns credits, and as one reviewer noted, "generative work is iteration by definition, so the useful question is not the monthly rate but how many attempts a real project takes." Fifty free credits evaporate in an afternoon.

The case for the machine, stated properly

That comparison is rigged, because a stock plan is someone else's house. AI draws your house, on your lot, for your family, and if the alternative is a $26,000 architect fee or not building at all, $6,660 is liberation. Architects have charged percentage fees since long before anyone itemized what the phases cost, and the guild has never once volunteered to break them down. Machines force the itemization. Maybe the permit phase is expensive because it has never had to compete.

Fair, genuinely fair: percentage fees are a cartel with good stationery, and anything that drags their components into daylight does the homeowner a favor. But notice what the objection concedes: the expensive phase survives. Nobody disrupted the design fee. What got disrupted was the sketch, which was never the expensive part. That is worth $60. It is not worth believing you have escaped the other $6,600.

Limitations

Maket's "70 to 75 percent of the schematic phase" is the company's own claim, unverified by any third party, and your mileage depends on how many credit-burning iterations your indecision requires. That $5,600 Nashville hybrid is a single reported case; architect consult and stamp fees range from $1,500 to well over $5,000 by market. This four-path table assumes a simple lot in a standard code jurisdiction; seismic, wind, and snow zones add structural engineering to every path equally. The copyright analysis applies the 2023 guidance to architectural plans by inference; no court has ruled on AI-generated residential plans specifically, and a plan with substantial human modification likely clears the human-authorship bar. Credit pricing shifts constantly; all AI figures reflect September 2026 pricing pages.

A floor plan is not a house; it is a proposition about how light will move through rooms you have not built yet, and the value of the proposition was never in the lines, which are cheap now and have always been the cheap part. What costs money is everything the lines promise: that the wall will stand, that the roof will shed water, that the inspector will sign, that the house will still be beautiful in thirty years. Machines can draw, but somebody still has to mean it.
