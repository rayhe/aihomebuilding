Your Crew Had 600 Near-Misses This Year. Your Safety Log Shows Zero.

By Marcus Washington · September 22, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Picture a framing crew on a Tuesday morning, second-story deck going up, sun already hard by nine. A hammer slips out of a framer's glove at the top plate. It turns once in the air, end over end, and plants itself in the mud six inches from the cut man's boot, close enough that he feels the thump through his soles before his brain has time to name what just missed him. From the deck, the foreman yells: "Heads up, huh?" Everybody laughs, or doesn't, and goes back to work. No form gets filled out. No log gets an entry. By lunch, the only record of the morning's closest call is a dent in the dirt that the rain will erase by Friday.

That dent is the most valuable safety data in construction, and the industry throws it away by the thousand every single day, on every residential site in America, because the only system for recording it is a man's memory and the rain.

A near-miss is the thing that almost hurt somebody and didn't: the dropped tool, the ladder foot that skated six inches on a slick slab, the laborer who stepped clear of the skid-steer half a second before the bucket swung through. OSHA's recordkeeping rule requires employers to log injuries and illnesses on the 300 Log, which means the system meticulously counts the damage after it happens while remaining legally blind to everything that almost happened. It requires exactly nothing about near-misses. No federal rule makes a contractor write down the hammer in the mud, the slipped ladder foot, or the bucket that missed, which is why the most predictive safety data in the industry exists entirely outside the law.

In 1931, an insurance engineer named Herbert Heinrich proposed that for every major injury there were 29 minor ones and 300 no-injury incidents. In 1969, Frank Bird studied 1.7 million accident reports from nearly 300 companies and sharpened the picture: one serious injury for every 10 minor injuries, 30 property-damage incidents, and 600 near-misses. Safety researchers still argue about the exact numbers, and honestly they should. Nobody serious disputes the direction. Under every death and every broken leg sits a mountain of almosts. (Accident triangle)

~600,000 Estimated near-misses across American construction last year, applying Bird's 600:1 ratio to the Bureau of Labor Statistics' 1,032 construction and extraction worker deaths in 2024. Required by law to be reported: zero.

Residential building construction accounted for 62 of the industry's fall deaths in 2023, about 15 percent of construction's fall fatalities. Apply the ratio and the homebuilding sector's share of the unlogged almosts runs into the tens of thousands a year. Every one of them vanished like the dent in the mud, because nothing and nobody required writing them down.

On a big commercial job there's a safety manager with a trailer and a binder, a full-time professional whose entire job is converting almosts into paperwork before they convert into funerals. On a residential framing crew there are eight guys, a foreman who doubles as the lead carpenter, and a clipboard in the truck that hasn't been touched since the drug-test forms. Near-miss reporting lives or dies on somebody taking ten minutes to write down what almost happened, and on a ten-man crew where the foreman is also the lead carpenter, the estimator, and the guy who picks up the lumber, that somebody doesn't exist.

The Cameras Arrived Anyway

They didn't arrive for near-misses, at least not at first. A company called viAct sells a vision system that watches site video for hard-hat violations, work-at-height risks, and people wandering into zones they shouldn't enter; this year it carried the pitch into Australia on the argument that manual inspections miss whatever happens between walkthroughs. Protex AI bolts computer vision onto a site's existing CCTV and flags unsafe acts as they happen. IntelliSee does the same overlay trick on cameras a contractor already owns. Marketplace listings put viAct's site system from around $100 a month, which is less than the porta-potty, less than the coffee fund, and a rounding error next to the workers' comp check the same GC writes every month. (SourceForge listing)

Behind the products sits a decade of published research, most of it from university labs where computer vision researchers have been teaching machines to read construction sites the way an old superintendent reads them, except the machine never blinks and never gets promoted away from the field. A team led by Peilin Li built a system combining YOLO object detection with a spatial-temporal graph network to catch unsafe interactions between workers and their environment, things like throwing tools or crossing railings, reporting accuracy from 86 to 100 percent across behavior types. Another group improved the YOLOv5s detector for cluttered construction scenes and lifted mean average precision nearly ten points. A widely cited review documents a two-network system spotting missing fall harnesses at 99 percent precision. Scientifically speaking, the detection half of the problem is solved enough to sell. (Li et al.; DAF-YOLO; Fang et al. review)

What changed is not the camera. It's what the camera remembers. A foreman sees the hammer fall and forgets it by lunch. Instead, the system timestamps it, tags the location, and adds it to a count that never forgets, never goes home for lunch, and never decides the paperwork isn't worth it. For the first time, a residential GC could know how many almosts his crews generate in a month. Nobody in homebuilding has ever known that number, because knowing it used to require a human being willing to write it down.

The Money Is in the Mod

Here's why a GC should care, in dollars. Workers' comp premiums multiply a base premium by the Experience Modification Rate, and the EMR is a three-year report card on claims. SafetyEvolution's example: a contractor with a $150,000 base premium and an EMR of 1.25 pays $187,500 a year, $37,500 more than the industry-average competitor, and the elevated mod sticks around for the full three-year experience window. One bad stretch is worth about $112,500. A vision system at a few hundred dollars a month costs less per year than a single modest claim costs in a week. If the near-misses the system logs prevent even one lost-time injury that would otherwise have moved the mod, which is to say one torn rotator cuff on a Tuesday in March, the camera pays for itself twenty times over and your broker sends a Christmas card.

For small crews, and small crews are exactly who builds America's houses, the math gets crueler than the big-commercial numbers suggest. Under the NCCI formula that sets mods in 39 states, frequency gets punished harder than severity: dollars below the per-claim split point count in full, while dollars above it get discounted. Three $20,000 claims in three years hurt worse than one $20,000 claim. AmTrust's safety writers put it bluntly: incidents land harder on small companies, and a single lapse in safety can raise premiums. Near-misses are, by definition, the frequency signal. They are the small claims before they become claims.

Now the Cold Water

Nobody has shown that auto-logging near-misses actually prevents injuries. Not one peer-reviewed study, not on a residential site, not anywhere. Detection, alert, human action: the evidence chain has three links, and the published research covers only the first, which means the camera that timestamps the falling hammer does nothing about the glove it slipped from, the foreman who didn't check the lanyard, or the schedule pressure that had a tired framer working the top plate at pace. Those lab accuracy numbers, 86 percent here, 99 percent there, were measured on curated datasets, not on a dusty production framing site where the lens films over by noon and the geometry of the work changes every four hours.

And nothing on the market is built for the ten-man crew. Every product page shows refineries, high-rises, and logistics hubs. ViAct's own Singapore case study involved 12,000 workers across multiple megaprojects, which tells you exactly which customer the product was designed for. A residential GC doesn't have a safety manager to read the dashboard, an IT closet to rack the server in, or any appetite for enterprise software pricing. Nobody disputes that the technology exists. What doesn't exist is the packaging for homebuilding: the ten-man price point, the no-IT-required install, the dashboard a working foreman will actually open between the morning huddle and the lumber delivery. Nobody has announced plans to build it.

Then there's the triangle itself, which working safety scientists have been kicking for years. Working safety scientists have a sharper objection, drawn from the serious-injury-and-fatality literature: the things causing paper cuts are often not the things causing funerals: grinding down near-miss counts can produce beautiful dashboards while the actual killers, falls, struck-bys, electrocutions, keep killing on schedule. A near-miss quota teaches crews to log trivia, so the extension cords get written up in triplicate while the unguarded edge, the thing that actually kills, never makes the list because nobody wants to be the guy who reported his own foreman.

Deepest cut of all is cultural, and it's the one my father would have made. A camera logs the ladder slip, but only a foreman with the authority to stop work fixes why the ladder slipped. Auto-logging without stop-work authority is surveillance wearing a safety vest. This publication investigated that exact problem last month, the footage-ownership and worker-tracking questions around AI safety cameras, and nothing in this article overrules it. Who owns the footage, who disciplines from it, how long it's kept: that has to be settled with the crew before the first camera goes up, not discovered in a termination hearing.

The Liability Paradox

Which brings the paradox nobody selling these systems puts on the brochure. A written near-miss log is a discoverable record of hazards you knew about. Lawyers already subpoena site camera footage after serious incidents. When the camera logs forty ladder near-misses in a month and the corrective-action column says "reminded crew," that spreadsheet becomes Exhibit A. This is the honest reason small contractors don't run near-miss programs today, and auto-logging doesn't remove the reason. It automates the evidence.

Follow the incentives one step further, past the contractor's office and into the actuarial tables, and the picture gets darker for the honest GC. Insurers price workers' comp on EMR today because claims are the only safety data that exists in standardized form. Give them three years of timestamped near-miss density per contractor and they will price on it the way they price on everything else. Log diligently and you could end up paying for your honesty, while the GC with no cameras keeps the clean record that ignorance provides and bids lower than you next spring. Until carriers and courts decide what a near-miss log means, early adopters are volunteering for a grading system that hasn't published its rubric.

What This Analysis Didn't Prove

A few honest boundaries. No dataset measures actual near-miss rates on residential sites, so the 600,000 figure is arithmetic on a contested ratio, not a count; treat it as an order of magnitude, not a census. Vendor claims, including viAct's Singapore results and the $100-a-month marketplace listing, come from vendors and marketplaces, not independent audits. My EMR math uses the NCCI formula covering 39 states; eleven states run their own rating bureaus, class codes and split points vary, and the smallest contractors aren't experience-rated at all, which means the break-even argument doesn't touch the two-truck outfits. On discoverability, this is general legal reasoning, not legal advice; privilege and work-product rules differ by state, so talk to your own lawyer before you build the log.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you run crews, don't buy anything yet. For one month, put a five-dollar notebook in the gang box and pay a standing bounty, twenty bucks a week split among everyone who logs a near-miss, no names required, no discipline from the notebook for ninety days. Count what comes back. That number is the business case, and it costs less than lunch. Then price a vision system against your mod, not your gut: call your comp broker, ask what one lost-time claim does to your EMR over three years, and compare it to a few thousand a year in cameras. And write the footage policy before the cameras go up, covering who watches, how long it's kept, and a written promise that auto-logged events don't become write-ups in the first quarter. Trust is the only thing that makes the data honest, and without honest data the cameras are just expensive witnesses recording a performance the crew puts on for the lens instead of the work as it actually happens.

If you're hiring a builder, ask one question the license board never will: how many near-misses did your crews log last month? A GC who knows the number is running a safer site than one who stares at you. A GC who says "we've never had an accident" is telling you his reporting system is a dent in the mud. Ask who owns the site camera footage, too, while you're at it.

Somewhere that hammer is still in the mud. Or it rusted out years ago. Somebody's father, somebody's son, walked away from it that Tuesday and never knew how close the math came. Six hundred almosts for every one that doesn't miss. For a century, the industry didn't write them down. Now the cameras will write them down whether anybody asks or not. One question is left, the oldest in construction safety, and no algorithm answers it: when the log says the ladder keeps slipping, does somebody with the authority to stop work actually stop it?
