Your $11,000 Mini-Split Bid Is Missing $2,400. Check the Line Set Math.

By Jake Kowalski · September 26, 2026
← Back to articles

Two bids landed on the kitchen counter. One said $9,800, the other $11,600. Both promised the same four-zone mini-split, same Mitsubishi heads, same SEER2 rating. Obviously the cheaper one won: the numbers sat right there in black and white, and $1,800 is $1,800. Then the change orders started arriving: line set extension, condensate pump, line-hide cover, dedicated circuit, permit. Final invoice: $12,100, which means the winning bid lost.

That story is not about a dishonest contractor. It is about a number nobody puts on the quote. HVAC people call it balance of system, BOS for short, and it is everything that is not the shiny equipment: copper line sets, refrigerant to fill them, condensate pumps, the plastic covers hiding the copper on your siding, the electrician's wire, the disconnect box, the pad under the condenser, the permit on the wall. On a typical mini-split job, that stuff is not a rounding error. A Fine Homebuilding teardown priced every BOS component on a real one-ton install, down to the zip ties, and found that even a no-frills job carried balance-of-system costs equal to a third of the equipment and materials. On fancier installs it pushed toward 40 percent.

Ninety-eight feet, and everyone misreads it

Here is the piece of math I want you to carry into every bid meeting, because it is the one that surprises people. Mitsubishi's MXZ multi-zone outdoor units ship from the factory with a refrigerant charge sized for 98 feet of piping. Chargeless piping length, the manual calls it, and the sheet for the MXZ-4C36NA3 prints it plainly: 98.0 feet, with additional charge of 0.216 ounces per foot beyond that.

Read that again: ninety-eight feet of total piping across all four heads, not per run.

Walk a real house with me: the condenser sits on a pad at the side of the garage. Head one serves the master bedroom, 35 feet of copper away once you count the wall penetration, the vertical rise, and a clean sweep instead of a kinked corner; head two reaches the living room at 45 feet, head three the kids' room at 50, and head four, the office over the garage, runs 60 feet, because copper does not travel in straight lines through finished walls. Add it up: 190 feet of total piping on a job where the factory charge covered 98.

For the excess, the manual is exact. Ninety-two feet beyond the allowance, times 0.216 ounces per foot, equals 19.9 ounces of added refrigerant, about 1.24 pounds. At the 2026 national average recharge price of $63 a pound, that is roughly $78 of refrigerant. I am giving you the honest small number on purpose, because this is where I want to correct an instinct: the added refrigerant is not the money. Undercharging a system is a performance problem, sluggish heating, a compressor running hot, possibly an early compressor death, and a botched charge can turn into a multi-thousand-dollar repair. But on the invoice it is eighty bucks. Follow the money: it is in the copper, the pumps, the covers, and the wire.

What a standard bid actually includes

Most mini-split quotes include 15 to 25 feet of line set per zone. That is the industry-standard allowance, and it covers a head mounted on the same exterior wall as the condenser with a tidy back-to-back run. Once a head sits on an interior wall, or the condenser moves to the far side of the house for noise, or the run climbs two stories, you are buying copper by the foot. Installers charge it as a long-run adder of $200 to $500, or itemized at roughly $75 to $200 per additional 10 feet. On the four-zone example above, three of the four runs blow past the allowance, which puts $350 to $600 of copper and insulation on the table before anyone has touched a wrench.

Then comes the condensate, because every mini-split head wrings water out of the air, gallons of it on a humid August week, and that water has to go somewhere. A head on an exterior wall drains by gravity through a hose, cheap and foolproof. A head on an interior wall, or anywhere below the drain point, needs a condensate pump, a small unit that lifts the water to a drain. They run $150 to $300 each installed, and on a four-head layout you will commonly need two of them. That is another $300 to $600 that appeared on zero of the two bids on the kitchen counter.

From there the invoice fills in fast. Line-hide covers, the paintable plastic channels that protect the copper from UV and make your siding look finished instead of improvised, run $150 to $400. Electrical runs $300 to $800 for the dedicated circuit and disconnect box, more if your panel is full and needs work. Permits run $100 to $400, and through brick or stucco, each wall penetration can add $200 to $500 for the sleeve. Add the column: $350 to $600 in extra copper, $300 to $600 in pumps, $150 to $400 in covers, $300 to $800 in electrical, $100 to $400 for the permit. Realistically, the unquoted work ranges from $1,200 to $2,800 on a job bid at $10,500 to $12,500. That is the $2,400 in the headline: never anyone's secret, just never on the quote.

Objections, stated honestly

Good contractors will push back on this framing, and some of the pushback is fair. Most reputable shops already bake realistic allowances into their numbers, which means the bid that looks $2,000 cheaper is sometimes just the one that itemized honestly, and the bid that looks expensive was the one that told you the truth up front. What I am describing punishes the careful shopper who compares headline numbers and rewards the contractor who lowballs the quote and makes it up in change orders, which is a market failure, not a conspiracy.

DIY pre-charged systems sidestep most of this, arriving with the refrigerant circuit sealed at the factory: no vacuum pump, no gauges, no nitrogen purge, and 2025 customer data from one manufacturer put the average savings over contractor installation above $3,000. That path has its own risks, warranty fine print, permit sign-offs, and the fact that you are now the HVAC technician, but the BOS math gets radically simpler when the line set is 16 feet and the refrigerant is already in it.

And single-zone installs mostly dodge the charge math entirely. A bedroom unit hung back-to-back with its condenser uses 15 feet of copper against a 98-foot allowance, and the added-refrigerant column reads zero. This 98-foot trap is a multi-zone problem, which is exactly why it bites whole-home retrofits, the jobs where the dollars are biggest and the homeowner is least equipped to audit the bid.

Jake's six-line bid audit: run this before you sign
  1. Get the line set footage per run, in writing. Ask for the measured length of every head's run, not the total. Anything past 25 feet per zone is copper you are buying.
  2. Get the added refrigerant per the manual: for a Mitsubishi MXZ, total piping past 98 feet gets charged at 0.216 ounces per foot, so ask which manual table the installer used and what the computed addition is.
  3. Price the condensate pumps per head, because any head that cannot drain downhill by gravity needs one at $150 to $300 installed. Count the interior-wall heads on your plan.
  4. Decide on line-hide covers before install day: $150 to $400, and bare copper on sun-facing siding degrades the insulation while looking unfinished.
  5. Name the dedicated circuit. Who pulls the wire, what size breaker, and is your panel full? A $300 circuit becomes a $1,500 panel conversation when nobody checks first.
  6. Assign the permit and inspection: $100 to $400, with somebody's name on the scheduling, because unpermitted HVAC is a resale disclosure problem wearing a work jacket.

What this article did not prove

I audited zero real bids for this article. That $1,200 to $2,800 unquoted range is built by summing published 2026 national cost ranges, and actual jobs vary enormously: labor runs 15 to 40 percent higher in expensive metros, and a simple back-to-back install can land near the bottom of every range. My refrigerant math uses Mitsubishi's published tables; Daikin, Fujitsu, and LG publish their own chargeless lengths and per-foot rates, so check the manual for the unit you are actually buying. Copper pricing is volatile and installer markup on line sets was not independently verified. Fine Homebuilding's BOS figures come from a single 2021 deep-dive on a one-ton system, and I applied its percentages to 2026 four-zone pricing, which is directional rather than measured. Federal tax credits for heat pumps are genuinely unsettled as of this writing, with one industry source reporting the credits expired at the end of 2025 and another still describing the 30 percent IRA credit, so verify current incentives with a tax professional before counting them. None of that changes the audit: six line items, in writing, before you sign.
