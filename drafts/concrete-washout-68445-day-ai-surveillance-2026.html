Your Concrete Truck Washed Out on the Curb. That's a $68,445-a-Day Violation.

By Catherine Chen, September 18, 2026, Policy & Regulation

The pour ends at 3:40 on a Tuesday. He pulls the chute, lifts the hose, and rinses a half-gallon of gray slurry into the gutter. It takes eleven seconds. Then he coils the hose, climbs into the cab, and drives to the next job, which is the entire transaction from his point of view: equipment cleaned, site clean, everyone moving. What stays behind in the gutter is a slurry with a pH near 12, roughly the causticity of liquid drain cleaner, carrying chromium and other metals that do not evaporate and do not politely decompose. When the next rain comes, the gutter carries it to the storm drain, and the storm drain carries it wherever storm drains carry things. Federal law has a specific, two-part opinion about this, and both parts are quotable. Before the code sections, the scene is worth holding in mind, because everything the permit regulates and everything the cameras will eventually film reduces to one man, one hose, one gutter, and eleven seconds of gray water running toward a drain that was designed to carry rain, not chemistry.

Part one lives in EPA's Construction General Permit, Part 2.3.4, which implements 40 CFR 450.21(e)(1) and (e)(2): wash water from concrete equipment must go into a leakproof container or a leakproof, lined pit sized so it cannot overflow from rain, and liquid washout waste may not be dumped on the ground, into site drainage, or into storm inlets. No exceptions for residential sites covered by the permit. No exception for "it's just a little rinse." The container is the entire compliance strategy, and it is not a strategy that requires engineering. Notice what the regulation does not contain: no threshold quantity, no de minimis exception for a single truck, no grace period for rinsing a chute on a residential street, just the container, the sizing, and the distance from water, which is why compliance lawyers describe this particular rule as unusually binary for environmental law, a rare case where the compliant act and the noncompliant act are visually distinguishable from the curb.

Under the January 2025 federal penalty adjustment, the maximum civil penalty for Clean Water Act violations sits at $68,445 per day, per violation, under 33 USC 1319(d). Violations accrue by the day: every day the uncontained washout remains uncorrected is another day, another violation, another theoretical $68,445. A week of doing nothing about it puts the arithmetic at $479,115. Meanwhile a 4-by-4-foot corrugated washout pan with a plastic liner, the industry-standard version of the mandated leakproof container, retails for $91.34 and is rated for ten uses. Put the ratio in plain terms: one day of maximum exposure costs roughly 750 times the container, which is the kind of number that makes a compliance officer wonder what everyone has been doing all these years.

EPA considers this the serious kind

The strongest evidence for how regulators view washout is not a penalty number but a policy carve-out. EPA runs an expedited settlement program for construction stormwater violations, a fast track for minor infractions with reduced penalties, and the program's own eligibility language excludes sites with "discharges of concrete washout." Read that twice. There is a program specifically designed to forgive construction stormwater violations cheaply, and a chute washout in the gutter is considered too serious to qualify for it. EPA is telling you, in administrative prose, that this particular eleven-second act sits on the wrong side of the line that separates paperwork violations from real ones.

The enforcement record backs the reading. When EPA settled with homebuilder M.D.C. Holdings for $795,000, the alleged violations listed in the complaint included the specific failure to install concrete washout basins to keep concrete out of storm drains. That was not an industrial facility. That was a homebuilder, building homes, fined on the order of a million dollars with washout sitting in the violation column.

How the detection model is changing under everyone's feet

Historically, washout enforcement relied on an inspector driving by at the right moment, which is to say it relied on luck. That model is being replaced by continuous machine observation, and the replacement is arriving from three directions at once.

First, the builders' own cameras. Sitemetric launched an AI camera in June 2026 trained on construction environments, with real-time detection of safety violations, restricted-zone breaches, and, in the company's own words, "fire and hazardous conditions." The camera watches everything on the site, all day, and the site includes the curb where the mixer washes out.

Second, the city's cameras. 3rd Eye, the Terex-owned AI camera company, announced its Connected City product this month: AI cameras mounted on municipal garbage trucks that identify "stormwater concerns, debris, illegal dumping" during normal routes. A garbage truck drives every street in the city, weekly, with an AI that now flags stormwater violations. A mixer truck hosing its chute at the curb is, from that camera's point of view, exactly the kind of visible event it exists to catch.

Third, the sky. A survey of state DOTs found 46 percent already use drones for environmental site assessments or permitting, and 29 percent use them specifically for stormwater inspections. Drone-based stormwater surveillance is not a pilot program anymore; it is a routine inspection modality, and it produces exactly the kind of aerial evidence that makes a washout dispute very short.

None of these products is marketed as a washout detector. That is the honest caveat, and it matters: there is no SKU labeled "AI concrete washout monitor." But the gap between what these systems detect and a chute washout is a model-training afternoon, not a product generation. The violation surface is becoming machine-readable. What once required an inspector with good timing now requires only a route, a camera, and a model trained to recognize a gray plume leaving a chute and pooling where the curb meets the gutter, which means the enforcement bottleneck has quietly moved from detection to someone's willingness to act, and willingness, in municipal government, follows visibility the way water follows grade.

Rendering the math honestly

Before anyone prints the $68,445 figure on a poster, the caveats. It is the federal statutory maximum for judicial penalties, not a typical outcome. Most residential washout enforcement happens at the state or municipal level, where fines run from hundreds to a few thousand dollars per day. EPA's New England settlements against small residential developers ran $4,200 to $8,700. The federal permit covers sites disturbing one acre or more; a single custom home on an infill lot is usually smaller and answers to state and local stormwater ordinances instead, which also prohibit washout but rarely threaten five figures.

None of that changes the ratio. A local citation of $500 a day is still a 5-to-1 multiple over the $91 pan, and an EPA Class I administrative penalty runs to $27,378 per violation. A custom home needs four to six concrete events, footings through driveway, and each one is a separate washout, a separate eleven seconds, a separate decision not to have the pan on site. Run it as a ledger: five concrete events, one $91 pan rated for ten uses, and the compliance line item for the entire build lands under a hundred dollars, a sum that would not cover the postage on the first page of the enforcement file if the city ever opened one.

And one more consideration that runs the other way: the cameras cut against the builder. Consider the Sitemetric footage: it proves your washout station was in place on Tuesday, but it is the same footage that proves it was overflowing on Friday, because the rain fly was off and the pit was undersized. AI monitoring produces a discoverable record of everything it saw and nobody acted on. Surveillance is a defense exhibit only if the defense is true.

What this analysis did not prove

This piece assembles published numbers into an exposure calculation that no cited source frames explicitly; the 750-to-1 ratio and the per-pour violation math are mine. I have not measured the actual probability that any given washout triggers enforcement, because nobody publishes that number, and any expected-value claim I built on a made-up probability would be fiction wearing a lab coat. The penalty figures reflect the January 2025 inflation adjustment; a 2026 adjustment may be pending. The EPA expedited-settlement exclusion comes from the archived 2019 revision of the pilot program document, and its current status should be confirmed with counsel before being quoted in a compliance meeting. The washout-pH figure is EPA and Connecticut DEEP BMP guidance, not a lab measurement from a residential pour.

What to do before the first truck arrives

The compliance sequence fits on an index card. Order the washout pan with the concrete order, not after. Put the washout location on the pre-pour checklist and tell the driver where it is before he starts discharging, because drivers default to wherever is convenient, and wherever is convenient is the gutter. Size for rain: the permit requires the container not to overflow from precipitation, so the rain fly goes on and the unit gets emptied before the next pour. Photograph the station on every pour day, time-stamped, because that photo is simultaneously your SWPPP inspection record and your defense exhibit.

If you are buying the home, walk the lot after the foundation pour. Chute residue on the lawn or the curb is evidence the builder's stormwater compliance is decorative, and the question "where was the washout station" is worth asking before the slab goes in, when the answer is still cheap to fix.

Eleven seconds. A $91 pan. A 750-to-1 ratio. The law has done the math for you; the only remaining question is whether anyone on the job site has read it.
