38% of Contractors Report AI Is Working. The Other 62% Are Still Logging Into 15 Different Systems.

By Frank DeLuca · July 30, 2026

I have managed residential construction projects for twenty years, and in that time I have watched exactly two technologies actually change how a job gets built: laser levels and smartphones. Everything else was a slide deck at a trade show and a subscription nobody renewed after the free trial expired and the salesperson stopped calling. So when I tell you that the contractor market is splitting into two distinct operational tiers based on AI adoption, understand that I am not selling software, and I am not easily impressed.

The numbers are clear. ServiceTitan's 2026 Commercial Specialty Contractor Industry Report surveyed more than 1,000 construction leaders and found that 38 percent now report measurable business impact from AI tools, a number that was 17 percent just twelve months earlier, which means adoption doubled in a single year. Among early adopters, 46 percent have saved between 500 and 1,000 hours, and 68 percent report at least $50,000 in direct savings, according to data compiled from the Bluebeam AEC Technology Outlook and reported by Construction Week Online.

At the same time, only 20 percent of contractors operate on a single integrated platform, which means four out of five are running their businesses across disconnected systems, transferring data manually between estimating software and purchasing spreadsheets and scheduling apps that do not share a common language or even a common file format.

That is the gap. It matters.

Where the Money Actually Goes

Twenty-four percent of contractors are applying AI to cost estimating and 22 percent to bid management, the two functions where data volume is highest and manual error is most expensive. A misread takeoff on a $400,000 custom home can produce a $12,000 material variance that surfaces months after the contract is signed, when the framing crew is standing in the driveway and the lumber delivery is either $12,000 short or $12,000 wrong. I have seen both outcomes, and neither ends with the homeowner saying thank you.

Administrative workflows show the steepest hour reductions among all the categories surveyed. Contractors using AI for field reports, invoice processing, and dispatch scheduling report cutting admin time by 30 to 50 percent. On a ten-person crew running six projects simultaneously, that represents between 15 and 25 hours per week that nobody is spending on paperwork, phone calls to suppliers, or reconciling three different versions of the same change order across three different inboxes.

Cash flow forecasting is climbing the priority list in ways that would have seemed abstract two years ago. AI systems now model revenue against expense timing across the full project lifecycle and surface potential shortfalls weeks before they become emergencies. For a contractor carrying $200,000 in materials on a line of credit at current rates, knowing that a draw schedule slip will create a 22-day cash gap in September is the difference between managing the problem and discovering it when the lumberyard puts your account on hold.

The $15 Billion Supply Chain Is Moving

This matters now because the supply chain itself is changing. Builders FirstSource, the nation's largest supplier of building materials with $15.2 billion in annual revenue, announced at the 2026 NAHB International Builders' Show that it is rolling out AI-enabled tools that connect design, purchasing, sales, and construction into a single integrated workflow.

"If you have to log in and out of 15 different systems, and move the data back and forth, the odds of it being error free are not good," CEO Peter Jackson said. "So anything you can do to centralize that data is powerful."

Fifteen systems. He said it out loud. Think about that for a moment.

What that means in practice is this: a builder working within the BFS digital ecosystem can feed plan data into the platform, and the AI translates that design information directly into material quantities, vendor availability, lead times, and pricing across the supply chain. The platform knows which jurisdictions require which code specifications. It can search historical project data using natural language queries and convert 2D plans into 3D digital twins for sales presentations.

That is a lot of capability. Gayatri Narayan, president of technology at Builders FirstSource, described the current state more bluntly, saying that builders have been forced to manage multiple systems that do not talk to each other, which creates inefficiencies, errors, and rework throughout the process.

Running the Numbers on the Gap

The supply chain shift matters because it changes the math on every project that touches a connected supplier. Let me put the adoption gap in terms a project manager thinks about, starting with a mid-size residential contractor running 20 homes a year at an average contract price of $450,000. Labor typically runs 38 to 42 percent of total cost on a custom build, so call it $180,000 per home in labor-related expenses including supervision, scheduling, administrative overhead, and coordination.

If AI tools cut administrative time by 30 percent on the conservative end of what early adopters report, and labor typically represents $180,000 per home on a $450,000 custom build, the savings add up fast. Roughly 15 to 20 percent of that labor cost qualifies as pure admin overhead: scheduling coordination, material ordering, change order processing, field report documentation. A 30 percent reduction on $27,000 to $36,000 in admin costs per home saves $8,100 to $10,800 per project. Across 20 homes, that totals $162,000 to $216,000 per year.

Separately, the ServiceTitan data shows 68 percent of adopters saving at least $50,000 annually, which aligns with the lower bound of that estimate for a contractor of this size. The math is not speculative; it is arithmetic, and the inputs come directly from the contractors doing the work.

What does this mean for the person writing the checks? A builder saving $162,000 a year on admin overhead can either pocket that margin or sharpen the bid. In a competitive market, the builders who reduce their internal costs are the ones who can hold price while everyone else is adding contingency. If you are comparing three bids in 2026, the lowest one might not be cutting corners but cutting paperwork instead.

Meanwhile, 71 percent of contractors reported rising wages in 2026, up from 55 percent the year before, while 67 percent rely on lines of credit to fund materials and 56 percent are negotiating extended supplier payment terms. These are contractors operating under real margin pressure. The ones who reduced their administrative drag by a third have a structural cost advantage that compounds with every project, every quarter, every year over the ones who have not.

Why the Skeptic in Me Is Still Skeptical

"Measurable impact" is self-reported. I have watched a superintendent use ChatGPT to draft a safety plan last March, never open it again, and still tell his boss that AI improved his workflow. ServiceTitan is a software company surveying contractors about software adoption. You do not need a statistics degree to spot the conflict, and Builders FirstSource is selling the platform it is promoting. These are vendors, not referees.

And the survey covered commercial specialty contractors working large-scale mechanical, electrical, and plumbing jobs where data volumes and process standardization are naturally higher than on a 15-home custom builder's operation. A $50,000 annual savings for a commercial HVAC contractor running $8 million in revenue lands very differently than it does for a three-person framing crew building custom homes by referral in a market where the phone still rings without a website. Blurring that distinction is what vendor decks do.

Here is what we do not have: a peer-reviewed study tracking residential contractors over 12 months, measuring actual cost reductions against a matched group that did not adopt AI tools. Not one. The admin time reductions cited above come from early adopter self-reports compiled from the Bluebeam AEC Technology Outlook, not independent verification. The $162,000 to $216,000 annual savings estimate relies on assumed overhead percentages within assumed labor cost ratios, and actual results will vary by contractor size, project complexity, and regional labor markets. The 38 percent number tells us that contractors believe AI is working. Whether it actually works, at scale, in your kind of project, is a different question, and nobody has answered it yet.

What This Means If You Are Building

You will probably never see your builder's estimating software. Nobody tours the back office. Good. But if you are building a custom home or buying new construction in 2026, you will see the results in ways that matter to the timeline and the budget. A builder running integrated digital tools catches a specification conflict during preconstruction instead of during framing, which is when catching it starts costing real money. A builder whose material orders flow directly from plan data to supplier availability does not call you in week nine to say that the window package is backordered because somebody transposed two digits on the order form.

Here are five questions worth asking before you sign a contract, and none is a deal-breaker but all are revealing.

How many software systems do you use from estimate through closeout? If the answer is more than four, your builder is almost certainly transferring data between systems manually, and that manual transfer is exactly where errors enter the process and compound through construction until they surface as cost overruns that nobody can trace back to one misentered line item.

Do you get automated material availability alerts, or does someone call the lumberyard? Builders connected to their supply chain digitally know about backorders before they affect the schedule; builders who find out by phone find out late, and late is expensive, because a two-week window delay does not just push the window installation back two weeks but cascades through siding, trim, paint, and final inspection in ways that add up to a month before anyone realizes what happened.

How do you handle change orders? A builder with integrated systems can price a change and show you the schedule impact in hours, while a builder passing paper between the office and the field will take days and may miss downstream effects on other trades.

What does your punch list process look like? Photo documentation with trade assignment is the answer you want to hear, because handwritten lists on yellow pads do not survive a rainstorm in the superintendent's truck and they do not create the auditable record you need when something gets disputed three months after closing.

Can you show me the schedule and where we stand against it right now? A builder who can pull up a current timeline or a clean dashboard has real visibility into the project, and a builder who says "we're on track" without showing you anything measurable may or may not be right.

None of these questions disqualify a builder on their own, because some of the best homes I have ever seen completed came from two-person operations running on notebooks and a phone. Craft and competence do not require a dashboard. Never did. But at scale, when projects multiply and complexity compounds, the contractors who have absorbed AI into their operations are building the same homes with fewer errors, tighter schedules, and lower overhead, and that advantage eventually shows up in your bid whether you are looking for it or not.
