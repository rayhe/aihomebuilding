A $4 Billion Industry Watches Every Bridge in the Country for Cracks. Your Foundation Gets a Guy With a Flashlight.

By Jake Kowalski · July 29, 2026

Somewhere in Kansas City, a MEMS accelerometer the size of a postage stamp is measuring vibrations on the Chouteau Bridge at 200 samples per second, one of 400 sensors on that span feeding data to an AI system that detects micro-cracks in reinforced concrete before the human eye can see them. Each sensor cost about $12. Installation for the entire monitoring array ran north of $2 million, and nobody questioned the expense because the alternative is a bridge inspector with binoculars and a prayer.

Meanwhile, 38.4 million American homes built between the 1970s and 1990s are entering what the foundation repair industry calls the 40-year structural service window, the age at which soil movement, moisture cycling, and settling historically begin cracking concrete and shifting load paths. These homes get monitored by a homeowner squinting at a basement wall, wondering whether that crack was always there, and that is not a metaphor. A June 2026 Groundworks national survey found that 86 percent of homeowners believe their home is structurally sound, while more than a third cannot verify that claim by any means at all. Foundation repair demand surged 20 percent in the first five months of 2026 alone.

The Scale of What Nobody's Watching

One in four U.S. homes sits on expansive soil, according to the American Society of Civil Engineers. Expansive soils cause more than $15 billion in annual damage to buildings and infrastructure, which is more than earthquakes, hurricanes, tornadoes, and floods combined. The U.S. Department of Agriculture estimates that half of all American homes sit on these soils, and roughly one in ten will sustain damage significant enough to threaten the structure.

When that happens, it costs real money: foundation repair averages $5,175 nationally, and piering and underpinning, the heavy interventions required when settlement has progressed past the crack-sealing stage, run $10,000 to $40,000. A homeowner who catches a quarter-inch of settlement early might spend $300 on epoxy injection. Catch it eighteen months later, after the floor has tilted and the drywall has started separating from the ceiling, and the bill escalates to helical piers at $1,825 to $3,350 each, times however many piers the engineer says the house needs, which on expansive clay in Texas or Colorado is often ten to fourteen.

Structural health monitoring could catch that quarter-inch of settlement, and it does exactly that on bridges, dams, offshore wind platforms, and parking decks. Wireless sensor networks account for 33.9 percent of the SHM market, and MEMS accelerometer prices have fallen more than 40 percent since 2018. A battery-free wireless tilt sensor published in Nature Communications Engineering in June 2026 can detect foundation tilt at 1.8 degrees of measured phase per degree of actual movement, requires no battery, no cables, and integrates directly into IoT networks.

None of this hardware is sold for houses.

Why the Gap Exists

It is not a technical problem. A residential foundation monitoring kit built from existing hardware would be simple: four wireless tilt sensors at mounting points along the foundation perimeter, one moisture sensor near the footing drain, and a gateway that streams data to a cloud dashboard. OmniSense, which already sells wireless building sensors for temperature, humidity, and wood moisture at $50 per sensor with 5- to 20-year battery life, makes hardware that could do this today. Gateway cost runs $200 to $450, bringing the total installed system to roughly $550.

Five reasons nobody has pulled the trigger on productizing it.

Economics is the clearest barrier. An SHM vendor landing a bridge monitoring contract installs 200 to 400 sensor channels at a project value measured in millions. A residential kit is a $550 sale, and no SHM sales engineer is spending their Tuesday afternoon configuring a Zigbee mesh network for a crawl space in Plano when the next bridge RFP is worth 4,000 times more.

Liability is the second reason. If a $50 sensor reports nominal tilt readings and the foundation cracks anyway, the sensor manufacturer inherits a product liability claim that no insurance underwriter has priced because no residential SHM product category exists in actuarial tables. Bridges have professional engineers on retainer who interpret sensor data within a licensed scope of practice. Your house does not.

Interpretation is third, and it represents the gap that AI should solve but has not. A structural engineer knows what 0.3 degrees of differential tilt across a 60-foot span means. A homeowner does not, and the software layer that translates raw tilt and moisture data into actionable guidance does not exist for residential applications. Commercial SHM platforms like Bentley iTwin and Autodesk Tandem assume a professional user who can read a structural response dashboard, and nobody has built the consumer version that says "call a contractor" in plain language when your kitchen corner drops an eighth of an inch over six months.

Regulatory vacuum is the fourth reason, and it may be the most damaging. After the 2021 Surfside condominium collapse killed 98 people, Florida passed SB 4-D, mandating "milestone inspections" for residential buildings over 30 years old and three or more stories tall. New Jersey enacted a Structural Integrity Act requiring initial inspections within 15 years of occupancy. Both laws explicitly exempt single-family homes, duplexes, triplexes, and fourplexes. Legislation that was supposed to prevent the next structural failure created a two-tier system where condominiums get mandatory inspections and houses get nothing.

Homeowner psychology is the fifth, and the Groundworks survey makes it brutal: 82 percent of homeowners feel "in full control" of their home's long-term structural safety. Southern states, with the newest housing stock in the country, report the highest rate of homeowners uncovering structural problems worse than expected, at 39 percent. Selling prevention to people who are confident they do not need it is a marketing problem that no startup has been willing to fund through the customer education required to solve it.

The Market Nobody Calculated

Run the numbers that SHM vendors apparently have not. According to the Colorado Geological Survey, 250,000 new homes are built on expansive soils each year. At $550 per monitoring system, that is $137.5 million in annual new-construction addressable market, which amounts to a rounding error against the $4.19 billion SHM market.

But new construction is only the surface of the opportunity. First American Data & Analytics, cited in the Groundworks survey, counts roughly 161 million homes in the United States, with nearly half built before 1980. If even 5 percent of the 38.4 million homes now entering their 40-year window adopted a monitoring system, that is 1.92 million units and $1.06 billion in hardware sales, not counting the subscription revenue from the cloud dashboard that interprets the data.

At that scale, the consumer AI problem becomes tractable. Train a model on foundation tilt and moisture data from two million crawl spaces across every soil type in the country, and the interpretation layer that does not exist today could become the most accurate early-warning system for residential structural failure ever built. Every sensor that ships feeds the training set. Every homeowner who catches settlement early becomes a data point that makes the next alert more precise.

What Would Actually Work

Not a professional SHM system scaled down to fit a house. What fills this gap looks more like a Nest thermostat than a Bentley iTwin dashboard: a kit the homeowner installs in an afternoon with a drill and a phone app, sensors that mount to the interior foundation wall with two screws and a silicone bead, a gateway that plugs into the router, and an AI backend that establishes a baseline over the first 90 days and then alerts the homeowner when readings deviate beyond seasonal norms. No structural engineering license required to install, no interpretation expertise required to read. When the system detects anomalous movement, it tells the homeowner what to do next and connects them to a licensed inspector who can verify the finding in person.

Self-supervised vision models are closing the remaining gap. Researchers at École de technologie supérieure published work in May 2026 showing that DinoV2, a self-supervised learning model, outperforms conventional supervised crack detection systems on noisy backgrounds and previously unseen materials, while requiring far less labeled training data. Mount a $15 camera on the crawl space wall next to the tilt sensor and the crack detection model could close the loop entirely, catching visual crack propagation without a human ever crawling down there with a flashlight and a prayer.

Limitations of This Analysis

Estimated costs for a $550 residential monitoring kit are assembled from publicly available sensor pricing, not a production bill of materials with enclosure, weatherproofing, regulatory certification, retail margin, and warranty reserve factored in. Actual consumer product pricing would likely run higher. A $1.06 billion addressable market assumes 5 percent adoption among homes entering their 40-year window, a figure chosen for illustration, not derived from consumer demand research or survey data. Sensor accuracy specifications cited from academic publications describe laboratory and controlled-environment performance; residential crawl spaces with temperature swings, moisture variation, and spider webs present different operating conditions. DinoV2 crack detection has not been validated in residential foundation applications. OmniSense products referenced are designed for building science research, not consumer deployment, and would require modifications for a homeowner-installable form factor. Florida SB 4-D milestone inspection requirements and their exemptions are cited from secondary sources; readers should consult the statute directly for their jurisdiction. Foundation repair cost ranges reflect 2026 national averages and vary significantly by region, soil type, and repair method.
