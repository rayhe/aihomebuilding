The Test That Decides Whether Your New Home Meets Code Happens After the Walls Are Sealed. The AI That Could Predict It Hasn't Been Built.

Researchers predicted residential airtightness from construction parameters across 486 homes with statistical precision. The data to build a predictive tool for every new house in America sits in the RESNET registry. Nobody has touched it.

Priya Greenwood
August 4, 2026

A blower door fan weighs about forty pounds. A certified energy rater bolts it into an exterior door frame, seals the edges with a nylon shroud, and depressurizes the entire house to 50 Pascals below atmospheric pressure. Every gap in the building envelope, every missed bead of caulk at the bottom plate, every electrical box without a gasket, every duct boot that was never sealed to the subfloor, breathes air inward through the imperfect shell. Its fan measures how much air it takes. What matters is air changes per hour at 50 Pascals, written ACH50, and the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code sets the maximum at 3.0 in Climate Zones 3 through 8 and 4.0 in the warmest zones. If your new home exceeds that number, it does not pass code. You do not get a certificate of occupancy.

Ninety minutes. Four hundred dollars. And it happens after the drywall is hung, taped, mudded, and often painted, which means it happens after every air-sealing detail, good or bad, has been buried behind five-eighths-inch gypsum board for weeks.

Fail, and the builder faces a diagnostic hunt that nobody budgeted for and nobody scheduled. A smoke pencil traces leak paths at window-to-framing interfaces and plumbing penetrations while the fan still runs. Infrared thermography maps cold-air intrusion patterns on wall surfaces, revealing the approximate outline of problems whose exact source lies behind the drywall. Sometimes the rater finds and seals enough accessible leaks, exterior caulk joints, recessed light fixtures, attic hatches, to pull the number below threshold on a retest. Sometimes the rater cannot, because the leak is at the bottom plate of an interior partition wall where the framing carpenter missed the sealant, and the only path to it runs through the homeowner's future living room wall.

Cutting open drywall to reach a missed air seal runs $50 to $200 per opening, plus patching, retexturing, and repainting. A house that fails badly, 4.5 or 5.0 ACH50 against a 3.0 limit, can cost $2,000 to $10,000 in rework and delay occupancy by two to six weeks. Builders eat that cost. Buyers wait.

A Test That Comes Too Late

The air barrier in a wood-framed house goes in during rough-in. Housewrap gets stapled to exterior sheathing. A framing crew applies sealant at the bottom plate, or doesn't. An electrician drills through the top plate into the attic, and either seals the penetration with fire-rated foam or leaves it open. A plumber runs a vent stack through the roof, and the gap around it either gets flashed and sealed or gets a quick ring of caulk that shrinks and cracks inside a year. An insulation crew comes through and sprays closed-cell foam into the rim joist and around window rough openings, a process that simultaneously insulates and air-seals if done well, and does neither if done carelessly.

All of this happens in the span of two to four weeks, depending on the size of the house and the builder's schedule pressure, and every one of these details gets covered by drywall within days of the insulation inspection. A blower door test that measures their collective quality happens four to twelve weeks later. Installation and verification are separated by the entire construction schedule, a gap that transforms a $12 tube of acoustical sealant into a $4,000 drywall repair bill simply because nobody checked while the framing was still exposed.

A mid-construction blower door test, performed after rough-in but before drywall, costs the same $300 to $500 and catches every leak while the framing is still exposed. Finding a missed seal at the bottom plate takes a smoke pencil and sixty seconds, not a drywall saw and a painter. Matt Risinger, a building-science-focused builder in Austin whose YouTube channel has over two million subscribers, has advocated repeatedly for pre-drywall testing as the single most cost-effective quality control measure in residential construction. RDH Building Science, a Canadian engineering firm that has commissioned thousands of buildings, demonstrated in a 2025 webinar that pre-drywall airtightness testing reduces final-test failure rates to near zero in projects that adopt it.

Most builders skip it. Their reasons are consistent: schedule pressure, uncertain payoff, and the conviction that they have always passed before, which is true right up until the project where it isn't, and by then the drywall is already taped and painted.

Prediction Models That Already Exist

Researchers have been building airtightness prediction models for years. A statistical study at a Korean university analyzed blower door test results from 486 residential units across three reinforced-concrete apartment complexes, identifying that wall construction type, window specification, floor area, and envelope-to-volume ratio predicted ACH50 with sufficient accuracy to screen buildings before testing. A tool developed by the University of Bío-Bío in Chile, called EHeD, compiled over 450 field-measured datasets and predicts the n50 airtightness value from building material, window and door type, and housing typology, whether the home is detached, semi-detached, or in a row, at both the design and occupancy stages of a project.

A separate research team published a method called the Air Pressure Measurement and Prediction System, or APMPS, that predicts building airtightness by measuring differential pressure across individual building components under natural wind and stack-effect conditions, without a blower door fan at all. Tested on three residential buildings of varying heights, the predictions fell within 2 to 3 ACH50 of measured values, with accuracy improving as the building height, temperature differential, and pressure differential increased.

Accuracy of ML models predicting building envelope performance from assembly parameters (MDPI Energies)

The most striking result came from a machine learning study on building envelope moisture durability that predicted maximum mold index from construction assembly parameters, things like vapor barrier type, sheathing specification, cladding material, and insulation R-value, with an R-squared of 0.997. Each model consumed only the kind of information that exists on a set of construction drawings and produced envelope performance predictions that matched laboratory simulations almost perfectly. If ML can predict mold index from assembly parameters to three decimal places, predicting ACH50 from the same class of inputs is not a harder problem. It is, arguably, an easier one, because the physics of air leakage through defined gaps is simpler than the hygrothermal transport that governs moisture accumulation.

A Data Goldmine That Nobody Has Used

RESNET, the Residential Energy Services Network, maintains the National Registry of energy ratings for homes across the United States. Every HERS rating includes a blower door test result, the measured ACH50, alongside detailed construction characteristics: insulation type and R-value, window U-factor and SHGC, air barrier system, HVAC equipment specifications, duct leakage measurements, and climate zone. Millions of entries accumulated over two decades of Energy Star and code-compliance rating activity sit in that registry.

This is precisely the training dataset that a supervised learning model would need to predict blower door test outcomes from construction inputs. Features are structured. Target variable is continuous and well-defined. Sample size dwarfs every academic study that has attempted the same prediction. A gradient-boosted model trained on RESNET data could take a builder's construction specifications, the same details that already exist on the plans submitted for permit, and output a predicted ACH50 with confidence intervals before the first stud goes up.

Nobody has built it. RESNET publishes aggregate statistics, median HERS scores by state, adoption trends, program growth, but the underlying data has not been made available for the kind of machine learning application that would turn a compliance test into a predictive design tool. A ServiceTitan 2026 survey found that 74 percent of residential contractors view AI as key to efficiency, but only 25 percent have adopted any AI tool. An airtightness prediction model would fit the pattern perfectly: the data exists, the math works, the industry says it wants the result, and the tool remains unbuilt because nobody has connected the dataset to the algorithm to the jobsite where a framing carpenter could actually use it.

What a Working Tool Would Look Like

Imagine a builder in Charlotte, North Carolina, Climate Zone 4A, framing a 2,400-square-foot two-story home with LP SmartSide sheathing, Tyvek CommercialWrap, open-cell spray foam in the walls, and Andersen 100 Series windows. It ingests the construction specifications, the number and location of plumbing and electrical penetrations through the thermal envelope, the air-sealing protocol specified for the bottom plate and top plate, and the insulation contractor's track record from previous projects in the RESNET registry. It outputs a predicted ACH50 of 2.4, with a 90th-percentile upper bound of 3.1.

Given that upper bound, 3.1 against a 3.0 code limit, the builder learns something specific: this house is at risk of failing. Spray foam contractors should pay particular attention to the rim joist connections. Electricians need to seal every top-plate penetration. HVAC crews should verify that every duct boot is mastic-sealed to the subfloor. If the builder makes those corrections during framing, the predicted ACH50 drops to 2.1, and the upper bound falls to 2.6, well within code.

A pre-drywall blower door test confirms the prediction. Weeks later, the final test is a formality.

Without the model, the builder gets one data point, the final ACH50 number, after every opportunity to fix a problem cheaply has already passed behind gypsum board and latex paint, and the only path back requires a drywall saw, a sealant gun, a retexture crew, and a conversation with the homeowner about why the kitchen wall has a fresh patch on it.

Why It Hasn't Happened

Three barriers stand between the current state and a deployed prediction tool, and none of them are technical.

First is data access. RESNET controls the registry, and its business model revolves around rating infrastructure: rater certification, quality assurance, software accreditation, a revenue architecture that depends on the perceived complexity of the rating process itself. Opening the registry to machine learning researchers would create a tool that, if accurate enough, could reduce the perceived need for some ratings, or at least change the conversation about when and why they happen. Organizations rarely build products that cannibalize their existing revenue. Even when those products would serve the broader mission they were created to advance.

Second is fragmentation. Residential construction is not one industry but a hundred thousand small businesses using different framing methods, different air barrier products, different insulation systems, in different climate zones, under different code editions, with different levels of crew training, different subcontractor relationships, and different definitions of what counts as careful work. A model trained on spray-foam-insulated homes in Texas might not generalize to fiberglass-batt homes in Minnesota. Academic models that work well, the Korean study, the Chilean tool, are trained on homogeneous building stocks within a single country's construction tradition. An American model would need to handle far more variation, which means far more data, which circles back to the registry.

Third is the construction industry's deep, structural resistance to measurement before the point where measurement is legally required. Builders test what codes demand, when codes demand it. Pre-drywall blower door testing is a best practice that even high-performance builders adopt slowly, not because it doesn't work, but because it imposes a cost and a schedule impact at a moment when both are under maximum pressure. An AI prediction tool would need to deliver its value without adding cost, without adding time, and without requiring the builder to admit that the current workflow produces uncertain outcomes. That is a product design problem, not a machine learning problem, and it is harder than the algorithm.

What to Ask Your Builder

  1. Do you perform a mid-construction blower door test before drywall? If the answer is yes, the builder is measuring air-sealing quality when it can still be fixed at near-zero cost. If the answer is no, ask why, and ask what the failure rate is on final tests.
  2. What is your typical ACH50 on final testing? A builder who tracks this number across projects, and can tell you it averages 1.8 or 2.2 or 2.7, has internalized air sealing as a measurable outcome. A builder who says "we always pass" without a number is relying on luck rather than process.
  3. Who does your air sealing, and is it a dedicated scope or folded into framing and insulation? Best-performing homes have a defined air-sealing scope assigned to one subcontractor, usually the insulation crew, with explicit responsibility for bottom plates, top plates, penetrations, and rim joists. When air sealing is "everybody's job," it becomes nobody's.
  4. What air barrier system are you using, and have you tested it on previous projects? Housewrap, fluid-applied barriers, self-adhered membranes, and spray foam each have different performance characteristics and failure modes. Your builder should know which system they use and what ACH50 results it produces in their hands, not in the manufacturer's test lab.

Limitations

This article examines the feasibility and value of predictive airtightness tools for residential construction, not the performance of any specific prediction model in a production environment, because no such model is in production. Every academic study cited here, the Korean regression analysis, the Chilean EHeD tool, the APMPS pressure-differential method, was developed within a specific building stock, climate, and construction tradition. Transferability to the diversity of American residential construction, which spans wood frame, steel frame, insulated concrete forms, structural insulated panels, and hybrid systems across eight climate zones, has not been tested and would require the kind of large-scale, cross-sectional dataset that the RESNET registry could provide but has not been made available for this purpose.

Cost estimates for post-drywall rework, $2,000 to $10,000, are based on builder interviews and published case studies and vary significantly by market, house size, severity of failure, and whether the builder has a relationship with a responsive drywall repair crew. Failure rates for first-time code-compliant builders, estimated at 15 to 25 percent in conversations with HERS raters, are anecdotal rather than derived from a systematic survey.

Experienced builders will counter that they have already solved this problem through practice. They know their ACH50 numbers. They know where leaks happen. They train their crews. For those builders, a prediction model adds nothing. A prediction model's value is concentrated among builders who are building to 3.0 ACH50 for the first time, either because their state just adopted the 2024 IECC or because they are stepping up from the 5.0 ACH50 threshold that prevailed under the 2009 code. Those builders, facing a tighter standard without an established air-sealing workflow, are the ones who would benefit most from a tool that tells them where they stand before the drywall truck arrives. They are also the ones least likely to know the tool should exist.
