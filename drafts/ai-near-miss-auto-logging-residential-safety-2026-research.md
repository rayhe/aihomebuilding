## Thesis
For every construction worker killed on the job, roughly 600 near-misses happened first — the dropped hammer that missed a skull, the ladder slip that didn't become a fall, the worker who stepped out of the swing radius a half-second early. OSHA requires exactly zero of them to be reported. On residential sites, where most crews are under 20 people and the "safety program" is a foreman's memory, the reported count is also zero. AI vision systems (viAct, Protex AI, IntelliSee and the academic systems behind them) now log these events automatically from ordinary site cameras. That creates the first honest near-miss dataset residential construction has ever had — and a legal paradox, because a logged near-miss is a documented hazard you knew about.

## Original contribution: three calculations nobody has put together

**1. The unlogged near-miss arithmetic.** Bird's ratio (1 serious injury : 600 near-misses, directional not law) applied to BLS's 1,032 construction/extraction deaths in 2024 implies on the order of **600,000+ near-misses per year** across U.S. construction — events with no reporting requirement, no log, and on residential sites, no witness but the crew. Residential building construction alone (62 fall deaths in 2023, 14.7% of construction fall deaths) implies tens of thousands of unlogged near-misses a year in the homebuilding sector specifically. Stated with the caveat: the ratio is a diagnostic, not a law; the point is the order of magnitude and the zero in the reporting column.

**2. The EMR break-even.** Take SafetyEvolution's $150K-base-premium contractor: one bad stretch pushing EMR from 1.00 to 1.25 costs $37,500/year extra, and the elevated mod sticks for the 3-year experience window = ~$112,500. A vision safety system at marketplace-listed ~$100-500/month = $1,200-$6,000/year. If auto-logged near-miss data prevents even one lost-time injury per decade that would otherwise move the mod, the system pays for itself roughly 20 times over. Assumptions stated: base premium varies by class code and state; mod math uses NCCI 39-state formula; prevention is assumed, not proven (see limitations).

**3. The liability paradox (analysis).** The legal reason small GCs don't run near-miss programs: a written near-miss log is a discoverable record of hazards you knew about and the corrective actions you did or didn't take. Auto-logging doesn't ask permission — the camera creates the record. That flips "we didn't know" from a defense into a data point. Insurers may eventually price on near-miss density the way they price on EMR; plaintiff attorneys already subpoena site camera footage. The article states this as the honest reason adoption will be slower than the technology warrants.

## The AI angle and its limits
- **What exists:** viAct (near-miss + leading-indicator dashboards, from ~$100/mo listed), Protex AI (CCTV overlay, real-time unsafe-act detection), IntelliSee (existing-camera overlay), and the academic layer (Li et al. ST-GCN+YOLO near-miss interaction detection; DAF-YOLO; Fang et al. harness detection at 99% precision). The detection technology is real and published.
- **What it cannot do:** prove it prevents injuries — no randomized or peer-reviewed study shows near-miss auto-logging reduces TRIR on residential sites; the evidence chain is detection -> alert -> human action, and the last link is a foreman, not software. Accuracy figures are lab/dataset numbers, not residential-jobsite numbers (dust, occlusion, changing geometry).
- **Where the gap is:** every product targets industrial/large commercial. The residential GC market (crews of 5-20, no safety manager) has no purpose-built offering — the opportunity is a near-miss logger priced and packaged for a 10-person framing crew, not a 12,000-worker Singapore megaproject.

## Limitations
- No dataset measures actual near-miss rates on residential sites; the 600:1 arithmetic is illustrative, built on a contested ratio (see counterargument).
- Vendor claims (viAct "10x safety score" in Singapore, ~$100/mo pricing) are vendor/markplace claims, not independent verification.
- EMR math uses the NCCI 39-state formula; 11 states use independent bureaus, class codes and split points vary, and small contractors below premium thresholds aren't experience-rated at all — the break-even doesn't apply to the smallest crews.
- No study proves near-miss auto-logging reduces injuries; the causal chain is assumed.
- Discoverability/liability analysis is general legal reasoning, not legal advice; privilege and work-product protections vary by jurisdiction.

## Strongest counterargument
The triangle itself is contested. Modern safety researchers (the SIF-prevention literature) argue Heinrich/Bird ratios mislead: the things that cause minor injuries are often *not* the things that cause fatalities, so grinding down near-miss counts doesn't necessarily move the fatality count — it can just generate paperwork. Worse, a near-miss quota system teaches crews to log trivia ("saw a cord on the ground") while the real killers (falls, struck-bys, electrocutions — the Focus Four) continue unaddressed. And the deepest objection is cultural, not statistical: a camera logs the ladder slip, but only a foreman with the authority to stop work fixes the reason the ladder slipped. Auto-logging without stop-work authority is surveillance with a safety label — which is exactly what the site's #805 surveillance article warned about, and this article doesn't get to pretend otherwise.
