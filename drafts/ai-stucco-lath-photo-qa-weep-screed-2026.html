A $1,500 Inspection or a $12,000 Rot Repair: The AI Wants Your Lath Photos First

By Jake Kowalski • October 6, 2026

Thursday, the brown coat goes on. After that, the weep screed disappears under seven-eighths of an inch of cement and nobody on earth will see it again: not the inspector, who got rained out Tuesday, nor the homeowner, who will never know its name, nor the moisture surveyor, who will find its absence six years from now with a probe and a four-figure invoice.

This is the part of the stucco story nobody photographs, which is exactly the problem. Three-coat stucco over metal lath is a great wall assembly, and it is an assembly whose most failure-prone details get permanently buried about 48 hours after installation, long before anyone thinks to document what the camera will never see again. Finish coat gets all the attention. Drainage gets none.

ASTM C1063, the standard for installing lathing to receive plaster, reads like it was written by people who had already paid for the failures. At the base of every framed wall goes a weep screed, a sloped flange that kicks water out of the wall cavity, with a vertical attachment flange at least 3.5 inches tall, its bottom edge at least an inch below the joint where framing meets foundation, its nose at least 4 inches above raw earth and 2 inches above paving, the weather-resistive barrier and lath covering the vertical flange completely and terminating at the top edge of the nose. Control joints break the wall into panels no bigger than 144 square feet, spaced at most about 18 feet apart, and the lath gets cut at every joint, never run continuous through it, because movement has to go somewhere and the standard would rather it go into a joint than into a crack. Working inspectors still quote these numbers from memory, because every one of them exists for a wall that rotted without it.

Now the money, because money is what turns a standard into a story. A professional moisture inspection runs $500 to $1,500, per StuccoSafe's published cost data. Severe water-damage remediation runs $60 to $120 per square foot, which means a 100-square-foot rot zone bills between $6,000 and $12,000 before anyone opens up framing, while full EIFS replacement lands at $14 to $34 a square foot and even a localized repair of a small damaged section costs $30 to $50 a square foot, per EngineerFix. The weep screed at the start of this chain of causation costs a few dollars a foot and takes minutes to hang. Stucco failure has the economics of a cheap part installed where nobody checks.

AI inspection has finally reached the job-site photo roll. Spectora, the inspection software used across the residential industry, launched AI Report Assist in June, a tool that lets inspectors dictate observations and snap photos while the model matches what it sees to pre-approved report narratives, and early-access inspectors are cutting about 25 percent of their time per inspection, per the company. In September, YYForce stood up Facadevision AI, a drone-plus-AI operation scanning building envelopes for cracking, spalling, sealant failure, and water ingress. TÜV SÜD sells the heavyweight version: LiDAR scans compared against the BIM model, full defect analysis in 24 hours. Different price points, one thesis, and the photos already exist, so make them do the checking.

Point that thesis at the lath stage and it gets genuinely interesting, because somebody has to verify the buried details before the brown coat, and a phone photo plus a model that knows the standard is a credible way to do it, provided the model was trained on walls that look like yours and not on a dataset of finished facades shot for real-estate listings. So here is the matrix nobody's vendor deck publishes: the C1063 checkpoints that decide whether a framed stucco wall drains, crossed against what photo-based AI can actually verify.

Read the right column top to bottom and the pattern is obvious, because everything the crew can skip, the camera can catch, and the three details most likely to be skipped on a production schedule happen to be the three the photo verifies best. Weep screed present with the nose at the right height above grade: one photo with a tape measure in frame settles it. Casing beads at every opening: countable against the window schedule. Control joint spacing: one wide shot per elevation. This is the happy half of the matrix.

Then the unhappy half, which is where the honesty lives, because WRB lapped correctly behind every accessory is not verifiable once lath is up, the laps being underneath it, and the lath fastener schedule and embedment is not visible in any photo of finished lath, and even the precondition for all of it, whether the wall is even framed, since stucco over masonry needs no weep screed at all, is sometimes obvious and sometimes a guess from across the yard. What the AI audits is the visible geometry. Old failures live in the layer cake underneath the visible geometry. No vendor slide mentions that gap, which tells you who the slides are for.

Give the counterargument its full weight, because it has some, starting with the fact that these vision models learned their trade on finished houses and commercial facades, not on open lath walls at 7 a.m. with the sun in the lens, and training-data mismatch is the quiet killer of every construction AI demo ever pitched in a site trailer. Most jurisdictions already send a human inspector to walk the lath stage. Where that inspection is competent, an AI second opinion is a nice-to-have wearing a must-have costume. Doxel raised $4.5 million back in 2018 to drone-scan job sites daily and flag what was wrong, and the tech worked while the company faded anyway, because a defect report nobody opens is expensive wallpaper. Add the masonry trap: point a framed-wall-trained model at a CMU wall in Florida and it will confidently flag the missing weep screed the standard never required. False positives burn trust faster than misses do.

So buy the tool for what it is. A second set of eyes at the cheapest inspection moment in the wall's life. Backup camera, not driver.

What to do Monday morning

If you run the crew, make the three-photo set a hard gate before the brown coat, because thirty minutes of phone photography is the cheapest insurance policy in residential construction and the only one that pays out before the failure instead of after it. One: the full foundation line, weep screed visible, tape showing nose height above grade. Two: every window and door head, casing bead in frame. Three: one wide shot per elevation for control joint spacing. Zero new software if your photo documentation already exists, which on any serious job it does.

Buying a new stucco home? Ask for the lath photos in writing before you close, because the sentence that separates builders with a process from builders with a prayer is a simple one: do you have pre-brown-coat documentation of the weep screed and control joints? No photos: weigh the $500 to $1,500 moisture survey against the $6,000 to $12,000 repair and price your offer accordingly.

Selling AI inspection? Train on lath, because finished-wall datasets are saturated and the open-wall datasets barely exist, which means the first vendor with ten thousand labeled lath photos owns the category outright.

What this analysis did not prove

The C1063 section language here comes from published excerpts and manufacturer guidance, not the full current text of the paywalled standard, so fine wording may differ by revision. Cost figures are published contractor and inspector ranges, not a controlled dataset, and regional labor spreads are wide. Vendor AI figures, Spectora's 25 percent and Facadevision's defect taxonomy, are press-release claims without independent verification. No published study apportions stucco moisture failures among weep-screed omission, flashing defects, and sealant failures, so this article argues by mechanism rather than by measured share. Treat the matrix as a reasoning tool, not a statistic.

Brown coat goes on Thursday. Take the photos Wednesday.
