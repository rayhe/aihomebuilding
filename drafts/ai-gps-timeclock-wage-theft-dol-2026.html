Your Framer Worked Saturday. Nobody Wrote It Down. The DOL Believes Him, Not You.

By Marcus Washington · October 8, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Marco has framed houses in the Inland Empire for eleven years. He reads a cut list faster than most supers read a text message, and his crew knows that when he says the walls go up today, they go up today. Last spring his foreman asked six men to come in Saturday to close out a punch list before the drywallers arrived Monday. They worked until two, and when checks came Friday, Saturday was not on them. Not the hours, not the overtime rate. Nothing. Marco asked; the foreman said he would check with the office, and nobody ever called back.

Multiply Marco by half a million. In fiscal year 2025, the U.S. Department of Labor’s Wage and Hour Division recovered more than $259 million in back wages for 176,957 workers across the economy, according to the division’s congressional budget justification. Construction is where the division finds the most to recover. An Economic Policy Institute analysis of two decades of federal enforcement counted 68,012 construction cases, 52,441 with violations, 542,034 workers owed back wages, and $847.9 million assessed in 2021 dollars, an average of $1,564 per worker. No other industry the researchers measured came close.

Those are only the cases the government caught. EPI estimates workers lose roughly $15 billion a year to minimum-wage violations alone. In Wisconsin, Minnesota, and Illinois, researchers compared Census household surveys against state unemployment-insurance payroll records and found nearly 1 in 5 construction workers facing some form of wage theft: about 100,000 of 538,000 workers either misclassified as independent contractors or paid off the books in cash, costing taxpayers more than $362 million a year. In Missouri, 21 percent of construction workers were misclassified or off the books in 2020, costing workers $334 million to $465 million in wages and fringe benefits every year. The pattern holds wherever anyone bothers to measure it.

Case files read like Marco’s Saturday with the decimal point moved. In January, the Labor Department obtained a consent judgment requiring an Escondido drywall contractor to pay $790,000 in back wages, damages, and penalties after investigators found 580 workers denied minimum wage and overtime. In April, a Newport Beach construction company agreed to $468,505 for 137 workers denied minimum wage and overtime. In December, federal investigators found a San Bernardino roofing and painting contractor had denied 62 employees pay for hours they worked.

Ten Dollars a Month Buys the Receipt

This is where it becomes a technology story. What would have settled Marco’s Saturday costs about ten dollars a month per worker: GPS-verified time clocks, apps like Workyard, busybusy, ClockShark, and ExakTime, that record the moment a worker crosses the job-site geofence, stamp each punch with location and often a photo, keep working through dead zones offline, and export the week straight to payroll. Workyard starts around $6 per user per month. Busybusy runs about $10 to $12 plus a $40 admin license. ClockShark is roughly $42 to $63 in base fees plus $9.50 to $11.50 per user. ExakTime, the old job-site hardware company now owned by Arcoro, starts near $9 per user plus a base fee and will sell you a rugged kiosk built to survive a trailer. Every major option captures punches offline and syncs them later, which matters because the dead zones are exactly where the work happens.

The Math Nobody in Residential Has Done

26 to 1 A $1,200-a-year GPS time-clock subscription for a ten-person crew costs one twenty-sixth of a single average DOL enforcement finding ($31,280 in back wages plus liquidated damages at the $1,564-per-worker average).

Run it for a ten-person residential crew at ten dollars a head, which is $1,200 a year, then apply the EPI average finding of $1,564 in back wages per worker to those same ten people, which comes to $15,640 in back wages owed to the crew. Current federal law adds liquidated damages equal to the back wages, so double it: $31,280, before a dollar of civil penalties. Against a single average enforcement finding, the app costs one twenty-sixth.

Congress is considering bills that would make the math uglier. Liquidated damages would rise to twice the unpaid wages, triple for retaliation, with civil penalties up to $10,000 per employee for repeated violations, a four-to-five-year lookback, and fines for missing time records. Under that regime, the same ten-worker finding approaches $150,000. Less than one percent of it is what the app would cost; these are proposals, not law, and more on that below, but the direction is unmistakable, because the record-keeping standard is going up, not down.

There is a second number that matters more than the fine, and it is why honest contractors should want the record even if they have never shorted anyone. When an employer keeps no time records, courts apply a burden-shifting standard from the Supreme Court’s 1946 Anderson v. Mt. Clemens Pottery decision: the worker’s testimony about hours worked is sufficient, and the employer must produce evidence to rebut it. No records, no rebuttal. Proposed bills would harden this into a presumption favoring the worker’s claims, rebuttable only by clear and convincing evidence. A GPS-stamped, tamper-evident log of every punch, showing who was where and when without relying on anyone’s Friday-afternoon memory, is the employer’s alibi. A paper timesheet filled out Friday from memory is not.

The Objections Are Real

None of this is an endorsement, because the honest objections are strong, starting with the obvious one. Workers experience GPS breadcrumbing as surveillance, and they are not wrong to, because field reality includes battery drain, false geofence exits that dock real minutes, and rural dead zones where the offline story works better in marketing than on site. App-store reviews from the people carrying the phones run notably worse than the B2B software ratings. A time clock the crew does not trust gets sabotaged, and sabotaged records are worse than paper, because they look authoritative while being wrong.

Second, the worst wage theft is invisible to any clock. A worker misclassified as an independent contractor or paid off the books in cash is not in the system, and the system records nothing. Roughly 100,000 workers sitting outside the payroll system entirely, the Midwest finding, is the ceiling on what this technology can fix. Third, cost was never the barrier for the thieves. An employer stealing wages will not buy the instrument of his own conviction, which means the realistic buyer is the honest GC who wants audit insurance and protection against sub-tier theft he did not commit, not the Escondido drywall contractor. Fourth, the federal bills are bills. Current law provides liquidated damages equal to back wages, not double, and presenting the proposed penalties as live exposure would be dishonest.

The Lien Lands on Your House

There is one more party in this story: the person reading this site because they are building a house. In California, an unpaid subcontractor, laborer, or supplier can put a mechanics lien on your property for work on your project, and the statute’s own preliminary notice says it plainly: even though you have paid your contractor in full, if the person who gave you this notice is not paid in full for labor, service, equipment, or material, a lien may be placed on your property, and foreclosure of the lien may lead to loss of all or part of your property. Your builder’s payroll practices are your title risk, because the lien attaches to the property itself, not to the contractor who failed to pay. California’s 20-day preliminary notice is the early warning system, because subs who serve it are telling you they exist and expect to be paid. Homeowners who demand conditional and unconditional lien releases from every sub and supplier before each progress payment are not being difficult. They are reading the statute.

Where This Analysis Stops Short

A few things this article does not prove. $1,564 is an average drawn from federal enforcement data across two decades and all of construction, not residential remodeling specifically; your trade and your market will vary. Break-even math here uses vendor list prices from 2026 comparison reviews, which change, and it assumes the app is used honestly, which is the entire ballgame. No independent study I could find measures whether GPS time clocks actually reduce DOL findings at residential contractors; the argument here is arithmetic plus legal logic, not an outcome trial. Those proposed federal penalties are not law and may never become law, while that opening anecdote is a composite drawn from the patterns in DOL case files rather than a single named interview, though every enforcement case cited is real and linked.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you run a residential crew: budget $6 to $12 per user per month for a GPS-verified clock. Workyard, busybusy, ClockShark, and ExakTime are the established names. Require geofenced clock-in and clock-out, export to payroll weekly, and keep at least four years of records even though the current federal payroll-record rule is three; the proposed lookback is longer, and storage is cheap. If you are hiring a builder: put payroll and lien-release language in the contract, require conditional releases with each pay application and unconditional releases before the next payment, and treat every 20-day preliminary notice as information, not an insult. If you are a worker: the number to remember is $1,564. That is the average back-wage finding per construction worker in twenty years of federal enforcement. Multiply it by your crew. That is the check the app is written against.

Ten dollars a month for the technology. The record is the point. And the next time a foreman asks for Saturday, the phone in the worker’s pocket should be keeping score, not the office’s memory of what happened.
