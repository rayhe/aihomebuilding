Your Lender Isn’t Sending a Draw Inspector Anymore. They’re Texting You a Link.

By Frank DeLuca · September 6, 2026

On a Thursday, the inspector drove out, walked the site with a clipboard, matched the slab and the framing against the draw schedule, emailed you photos nobody ever opened, and the money moved the following Tuesday. You paid for the inspection and waited five business days, while your plumber spent most of that time on somebody else’s job.

That Thursday is gone, along with the clipboard, the drive to the site, the three-day wait for the inspector’s calendar to open up, and the second wait for the report to clear somebody’s inbox on the other side.

Your phone buzzes. A text from your lender: a link. You walk the site yourself, hold the phone up to the framing, the slab, the roughed-in plumbing, and the app captures geotagged, timestamped photos that software authenticates against fraud before a human ever looks at them. Minutes later the report sits in the lender’s system. One construction lender says the whole cycle, from the email hitting the borrower’s inbox to the wire being funded, now takes under half an hour.

30 minutes
From draw request to funded wire at one construction lender using authenticated virtual inspections, per VP of Operations Chris Marohn. Industry median turnaround is 24 hours; the old way ran seven to ten business days.

How the money used to move

Draw inspections were never the bottleneck anyone talked about, because the bottleneck was everything around them. Rabbet’s 2024 data put the annual cost of slow payments in U.S. construction at $280 billion, and draw requests routinely took seven to ten business days to clear. Inside that window the lender ordered a third-party inspector, the inspector scheduled the site visit, the visit happened, the report was written, the report was reviewed, somebody’s accounts payable department eventually moved the money, and every one of those handoffs carried a day of float that the borrower paid for in interest. Every step was a queue, and every queue had a weekend attached.

Meanwhile the trades do not wait in queues. A sub who has not been paid in three weeks does not argue with your schedule; he takes a job that pays. Ninety-seven percent of general contractors raised their bids in 2024 to compensate for payment risk, which means the owner of every project in America is paying a surcharge for the lender’s paperwork moving at the speed of fax.

Your new draw inspector: you

Shipping has already started, quietly, at the largest lenders, where somebody in the draw department did the arithmetic and figured out that the borrower holding a phone is cheaper than the inspector driving a truck. Truepic Vision lets the borrower perform the draw inspection on their own phone, on their own schedule. Controlled capture technology authenticates every photo at the moment of capture: geolocation, time, date, tamper detection. The photos are validated as taken at the project address, each one unique, with a median turnaround of 24 hours.

Chris Marohn, vice president of operations at a construction lender using the system, put numbers on it: less than seven minutes from receiving the lender’s email to sending the Truepic inspection link to the borrower, and draws turned around in under half an hour, ready to fund. “Because of how quick Truepic is, we fund the draw as quickly as the borrower wants it,” he said.

Those rails got bigger on June 23, 2026, when Truepic announced a strategic integration with Cotality, the property data company formerly known as CoreLogic, embedding Vision directly into the Mercury Network and Collateral Management System platforms that mortgage lenders use to order appraisals and inspections, which is another way of saying the experiment is over and the product is now infrastructure. Initial roll-out covers home equity evaluations, 1004D final inspection alternatives, disaster documentation, default servicing, and construction and renovation draws. Cotality’s Sage Nichols framed the point bluntly: lenders need “trusted, verified imagery in today’s era of increasing AI-generated fraud risk,” with turnaround cut from days or weeks to hours.

On the commercial side, the push is going further: DroneDeploy’s Progress AI runs vision-language models over drone flights and 360-degree walkthroughs and generates progress reports the company describes as 95 percent accurate, automatically. Beta customers have used it to track milestones and validate pay applications without site walks. Your construction lender is not quite there yet. But the direction is visible: the inspection is becoming data, not a person.

Math your lender never showed you

Speed is nice, and money is nicer. Nobody publishes the carrying-cost math of faster draws, so I ran it. Take a $600,000 construction loan at 7 percent, eight draws across the project, and six business days of inspection-and-processing float eliminated per draw. Six business days is roughly eight calendar days. Eight draws times eight days is 64 calendar days of float recovered across the build. At an average outstanding balance of $300,000 during draw cycles, daily interest runs $57.53. Sixty-four days of it comes to about $3,680 in pure interest, per project, for the scheduling change alone.

That assumes the lender actually releases same-day on the virtual report, and it ignores the bigger number: the sub who stays on your job instead of leaving on day three of a ten-day draw, the schedule hole that becomes a two-week slip, the trades stacking up behind it, and the general conditions meter that never stops running while you wait. Interest is the part you can count; the crew that does not leave is the part that saves the schedule.

What the photo cannot see

Here is the part that should slow you down, and if you have been in this business long enough you already feel it in your knees.

A photo verifies that work exists; it cannot verify that work is right. No lens can see whether the waterproofing membrane was lapped in the correct direction, whether the shear wall nailing schedule was followed, whether the plumber’s slope actually drains. A draw inspector with a clipboard could not see most of that either. But at least he was paid to be wrong in person. Doing the inspection is unpaid work: the borrower is untrained in what to look at, performing the task inside fraud-detection software with no published appeal process.

That last part deserves a longer pause, because Truepic’s platform runs fraud detection and authentication testing on every image while the company operates a “risk intelligence network to help identify bad actors and fraud techniques in real time,” all of it vendor-reported and unaudited by any third party I could find. If the software flags your photos as fraudulent, there is no public process for disputing it, and the network’s findings about bad actors travel across lenders. You are doing the inspection work for free, inside a fraud-watch system you cannot see, to unlock your own loan, and nobody put that sentence in the marketing.

Arguing against my own excitement

The honest counterargument starts with what the old draw inspector actually did, which was less than his invoice suggested. Draw inspectors never performed quality control; they verified coarse percent-complete so the lender’s collateral position stayed ahead of the money. Framing is 80 percent up, the roof is dried in, release the funds. Virtual inspection performs the same coarse job faster and cheaper, which is exactly what lenders needed, and nothing about it makes the inspection smarter.

Which means the most dangerous outcome of this technology is a builder who believes the lender’s 24-hour photo review substitutes for real quality control, because it does not, and faster draws can fund faster mistakes, with a fraud algorithm’s stamp of authenticity on every photo of the mistake. And for the bulk of custom-home lending, which still runs through community banks with fully manual draw desks, none of this exists yet. early adopters are the large lenders with platform integrations, and your local bank is still sending the guy with the clipboard.

What to do on Monday

Ask your lender how draws are verified before you sign. If they offer virtual inspection, ask what the fraud-flag dispute process is. If they cannot describe one, that is your answer.

Write draw turnaround into the loan commitment. Inspection turnaround is a line item you can negotiate: five business days versus 24 hours changes your schedule, and schedules are money.

Do not skip your own QA because the lender’s photos came back clean. The lender verifies the money. Nobody verifies the waterproofing unless you hire them to. Budget for an independent pre-drywall inspection anyway.

Tell your subs the draw is moving faster. A ten-day draw teaches subs to leave, and a two-day draw is a retention tool, so say it out loud at the precon meeting.

Document as if every photo is evidence, because it is. Geotagged, timestamped, fraud-authenticated photos of your own work are the best dispute record you will ever have, so take more than the app asks for.

Limitations of this analysis

The $3,680 carrying-cost figure rests on stated assumptions: a $600,000 loan, a 7 percent rate, eight draws, six business days of float recovered per draw, and a $300,000 average outstanding balance, which means it describes a mid-size custom build in a normal rate environment, not the $1.4 million hillside project at 9 percent where the same arithmetic gets much uglier, much faster. Change any of them and the number moves. Treat the method as the point, not the total. Truepic’s fraud-detection claims, false-positive rates, and the risk intelligence network are vendor-reported without third-party audit. I could not find published data on how often borrower-submitted inspections are flagged or overturned. This is an early-adopter story: the Cotality integration was announced in June 2026, and most community banks, which handle the bulk of custom-home construction lending, still run manual draw desks. DroneDeploy’s 95 percent accuracy figure is the company’s claim from its own launch materials.

Once, the inspector drove out on Thursday because the lender needed to trust the site. Now the lender trusts the pixels, and the pixels arrive in minutes. That is a genuine improvement, and it is worth $3,680 and a crew that stays. Just remember who is doing the work now, who is watching it, and what a photo can never tell you. The money got faster, but the building did not get any easier.
