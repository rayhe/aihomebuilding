One Bad Sub Costs 3x Their Contract. Your Builder Picked Yours Over Coffee.

By Marcus Washington · October 8, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Halfway through framing, the crew stopped showing up, and not all at once: first it was Monday, then three guys short on Wednesday, then the foreman's phone went straight to voicemail and the lumber sat in the driveway getting rained on for two weeks. This homeowner had picked the builder because his bid came in $40,000 under the next one. Nobody asked where that $40,000 went. It went to the framer. He was the cheapest one the GC could find, hired on a handshake and a number he'd heard at the lumberyard. This framer was running four jobs with one crew and a workers' comp policy that had lapsed in March. When his guys scattered to a bigger job across town, nobody was left to finish the house. Finding a replacement at emergency rates took the GC six weeks, the schedule slipped two months, and the final accounting on the "cheap" framer came to nearly three times his original bid.

This is the most expensive sentence in residential construction: "My guy can do it cheaper." A single subcontractor default costs 1.5 to 3 times the original subcontract value, according to 2026 prequalification industry data. Rework alone eats 5 to 15 percent of total project costs, per Six Sigma construction analysis, and a 2024 Dodge Construction Network survey found 98 percent of contractors had suffered serious quality failures in the previous three years. The sub is not a line item. The sub is the project.

The Scorecard Your Builder Isn't Using

Big commercial GCs figured this out years ago. Before a sub touches a hospital or a data center, they run prequalification: financial statements, insurance certificates, safety records, license status, bonding capacity, references. It used to be a spreadsheet and a paralegal; now it is software with an AI layer, and the AI does exactly the parts humans skip, which is the entire point of the exercise.

Highwire feeds a sub's EMR data, OSHA incident rates, and safety program documents into a model that produces a risk score for who is most likely to have a safety incident on the job. Autodesk's TradeTapp maintains a prequalified network of more than 250,000 subcontractors, which sounds like scale until you remember that scale is the point: the whole value of a network is that somebody else already did the paperwork you were never going to do. Vertikal RMS claims its financial analysis can predict a sub's default probability six to twelve months out by recognizing patterns across thousands of contractors, and its real-time monitoring watches payment patterns and credit signals because an annual financial statement tells you where a sub stood a year ago, which is about as useful as last year's weather report.

Oddly, the cleverest piece is the most boring one. Billy's AI Review Assistant reads the ACORD 25 certificate of insurance, extracts limits, carriers, and expiration dates, then checks endorsements like additional insured and waiver of subrogation against the GC's requirements. Then it chases the sub and their broker automatically when something is missing. Insurance certificates expire mid-job constantly, and on most residential crews, nobody was checking at all.

Do the Math on One Build

Take a $2 million custom home with fourteen subcontractors averaging $50,000 each. Assume one in ten sub relationships goes sideways on a given build, which is not a published statistic, because nobody publishes per-build sub failure rates for residential work, so treat it as a working assumption and adjust it for your market. Assume each problem sub costs half their contract value in rework and delay, deliberately conservative against the published 1.5-to-3-times figure for a full default.

Run the arithmetic slowly, because nobody in residential construction does: 14 subs times 10 percent times half of $50,000 gives an expected loss per build of $35,000, which is the invisible line item in every custom home budget and the tax you pay for hiring blind.

Now price the alternative: Constrafor offers a free universal prequalification form, which means the zero-dollar version of this process is a form, an hour of admin time, and a habit of actually checking. Enterprise tools (Procore's prequal module, Highwire, TradeTapp) mostly hide pricing behind sales calls, which tells you plenty about who they are really built for. But the break-even math does not need precision: avoiding one problem sub per year, at a conservative $25,000 in avoided cost, pays for any realistic tool cost many times over. No software needs to be perfect here; it only needs to beat a handshake, and a handshake has never once checked an EMR, read an ACORD 25, or called a reference.

The Score Can Lie Too

Now the part the vendors leave out of the demo. A prequalification score is a snapshot. Snapshots go stale, and in construction they go stale fast, because the sub who looked solid when you signed him in March can be underwater by August, running four jobs with one crew and a workers' comp policy about to lapse. A sub with clean financials in March can be underwater by August, which is exactly why Vertikal RMS pitches real-time monitoring, but most platforms still run on annual statements and self-reported data. A financial statement from a struggling sub is creative writing. Fiction, really, the kind that costs a builder six figures and a homeowner a summer.

Scores also launder bias, because a small immigrant-owned framing crew with thin paperwork and a flawless ten-year safety record will score worse than a mediocre large shop with a compliance department and a binder full of policies. A GC who trusts the number blindly has traded one failure mode for another, and the crew that does the best work in the county may be the one the algorithm filters out. My grandfather carried a union card for forty years and would have scored terribly on paperwork, and he never missed a day.

For the small residential GC running two custom homes a year, the honest answer is that a spreadsheet, a license-board lookup, and phone calls to the last three references may genuinely be competitive with the software. These tools were built for firms managing hundreds of subs across dozens of projects, with compliance departments and lawyers on retainer, which is a universe away from a residential GC whose office is the cab of his truck. A two-house-a-year builder does not have a data problem at all, because his actual problem is discipline, and no software ever written will fix a GC who will not pick up the phone to check a reference.

Where This Analysis Stops Short

One disclosure up front: the opening anecdote is a composite built from the failure patterns in the sources above, not a single documented job, because no builder volunteers his worst sub story on the record, and the ones who would talk still need to get hired next month.

No published study isolates subcontractor default rates for single-family residential GCs, so the 1.5-to-3-times default cost comes from commercial-oriented prequal marketing and may not map cleanly to custom homes, because a tract-home framer and a custom-home framer fail in completely different ways, for completely different reasons, at completely different price points. Vendor pricing is almost entirely undisclosed, which means the cost side of the break-even math is qualitative at best, and the 10 percent problem rate in the expected-loss model is my assumption, stated openly and carried through the arithmetic, not a finding anyone can audit. AI default-prediction claims of six to twelve months of foresight have no independent validation I could find. EMR, the core safety input, is gameable and lags actual site conditions, and any score built on it inherits those flaws.

What to Ask Before You Sign

If you are hiring a builder, ask three questions before you sign. One: do you prequalify your subcontractors, and can you show me what that looks like? Two: can I see the certificate of insurance for every trade before they start work, not after something goes wrong? Three: who checks license status, and when was the last check? A builder who answers all three without blinking runs a professional operation. Believe him. One who laughs at question one is telling you exactly how your job will go, and believe that too.

If you are the builder, start free. Run every sub through Constrafor's universal prequal form or your own one-page version: license number, insurance with expiration dates, EMR, three references, and one question about the biggest job they finished in the last year. Automate the insurance expiry tracking next, because that is where residential jobs actually bleed. Score your five highest-risk trades first. Framing, electrical, plumbing, roofing, concrete. Hunt for red flags that are boring and specific: lapsed license, expired certificate, an EMR climbing past 1.0, no bonding capacity, a trail of change-order disputes, and if you find two of these on the same sub, keep looking, because the third one always shows up on your job.

That cheapest bid is information, because it tells you exactly what the builder thinks your job is worth to him, which is less than it is worth to you. And the most expensive sub you will ever hire is the one you never checked, so check.
