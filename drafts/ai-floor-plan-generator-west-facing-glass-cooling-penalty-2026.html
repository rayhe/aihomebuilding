Your AI-Designed Home Has a Window Problem. It's Facing West.

Text-to-floor-plan tools arrange rooms beautifully and never ask where the sun goes down. A glass wall the AI drew by default can add half a ton of cooling load and roughly $4,000 to $5,000 over thirty years in a climate like Sacramento's.

Elena Vasquez
September 5, 2026

A house designed by a machine begins its life as a picture. On the screen the great room glows, and at the far end of the plan a wall of glass frames the promise of a sunset that will arrive every evening for the next thirty years. Nobody at the kitchen table sees what the render leaves out. The air conditioner strains through August afternoons while the utility bill swells by a tenth, and the west bedroom never quite cools down before dinner. No render admits the failure. Physics keeps its own ledger.

Type "2,400 square foot bungalow, three bedrooms, open great room" into one of the new AI floor plan generators, Maket, FloorDrafter, or the ZURU pipeline built on Claude, and minutes later you hold a complete plan with rooms arranged, dimensions computed, and a walkable 3D model, which is genuinely impressive engineering. ZURU's engineers, working with Amazon's generative AI lab, reported a 109% improvement in instruction adherence and a 54% improvement in spatial-relation math after fine-tuning Llama on SageMaker, and every bit of that shows in the output. Their evaluation measured exactly two things, instruction adherence and dimension correctness, and solar orientation appeared nowhere in the benchmark. On FloorDrafter, orientation is a line the user may fill in, filed under "anything that needs to stay fixed," which means the default behavior is to ignore it entirely. The machine arranges rooms beautifully and never asks which way is west.

An architect trained on paper learns this on day one: buildings do not experience glass equally, and a square foot of window facing west is a fundamentally different thermal object than the same square foot facing north. Low in the sky, the late-afternoon sun strikes the west facade at a shallow angle, slides under every overhang, and arrives at precisely the hour when outdoor temperatures peak, the air conditioner is already running, and the family walks through the door. A full technical report from the Department of Energy's Pacific Northwest National Laboratory, PNNL-18038, documents why west-facing glazing carries the greatest cooling penalty of any orientation, and proposes a prescriptive cap of 110 square feet of west glass, defined as facing between 225 and 300 degrees of azimuth. Nobody adopted the cap. As the report notes, the energy code has never effectively regulated the orientation of glazing in new homes; it regulates the quality of the glass through solar heat gain limits and says nothing about where the glass goes.

Price the view

Nobody in this industry has published this number. Here is what the default costs, worked out on the back of an envelope. Take a 2,000 square foot single-story home in Sacramento, a hot-dry market where the International Energy Conservation Code caps glazing solar heat gain at 0.25. In the AI's version, the great room puts 160 square feet of glass on the west wall. Every image model in existence loves a sunset. An orientation-aware version puts 40 square feet there and redistributes the rest to the south, where deep overhangs shade the summer sun, and the north, where glass is cheap daylight.

Run the difference: 120 square feet of west glass. At a code-minimum solar heat gain coefficient of 0.25, the peak afternoon solar load through west glass runs about 50 BTU per hour per square foot, which gives 6,000 BTU per hour of extra cooling load, or roughly half a ton of air conditioning that the equipment schedule did not plan for. Over 800 equivalent full-load cooling hours in a Sacramento summer, that is 4.8 million BTU a year, and at an energy efficiency ratio of 11 for a code-minimum system, about 436 kilowatt-hours annually. At 32 cents a kilowatt-hour the AI's window placement costs around $140 every year, upsizing the equipment for the extra half ton runs $800 to $1,200 installed, and over thirty years the present cost lands between $4,000 and $5,000, all of it before demand charges and before the comfort penalty of west rooms running five to eight degrees hot on August afternoons.

This is a steady-state estimate, not a whole-building energy simulation, and I will name its assumptions so a reader can check the arithmetic: code-minimum glass, 800 cooling hours, 32-cent electricity, and a single-zone house with no shading devices. Change any of them and the number moves, though it never reaches zero. A separate case study in MDPI's Engineering Proceedings found that the worst combination of window size and orientation, an 80% window-to-wall ratio facing west, dramatically outpaced every other configuration, while the best, a modest 15% ratio facing north, barely registered. Back in 2007, the IPCC's building chapter said the same thing: orient the building to minimize east and west wall area, and avoid excessive glazing on those walls. Two decades of building science agree with the arithmetic; only the floor plan generator missed it.

The honest objection

Views and daylight are legitimate design goods, and I have argued for them in spaces where clients would happily have given them up, which is why a west-facing glass wall over a canyon sunset is a choice rather than a mistake, when it is priced honestly. In heating-dominated climates, where the code sets no solar heat gain requirement at all, west glass carries a smaller penalty and winter sun has real value. High-performance glass with solar heat gain below 0.20, exterior shades that block the low sun, and thermal mass that absorbs the afternoon spike can cut the penalty substantially. Narrower than it sounds: nobody is saying never glaze west. It is that the tool does not know what west costs, so somebody has to do the accounting the software skipped.

That somebody is you, and the fix is disarmingly simple. When you prompt the generator, put the orientation and the lot azimuth in the prompt explicitly. Optional input defaults to ignored input. Before you fall in love with the render, audit west-facing glass against the 110-square-foot benchmark the national lab proposed, which remains the only number anyone has put on this problem. Budget roughly four-tenths of a ton of cooling and about $115 a year in a Sacramento-like climate for every 100 square feet of west glass beyond an oriented design. And remember the asymmetry the AI never suggests: south glass with proper overhangs is the free lunch, north glass is cheap daylight, and west glass is the one you pay for twice, once in the equipment schedule and again in July.

What to do Monday morning

If you are generating a plan, add one sentence to the prompt: "Orient the house with the long axis running east-west, minimize glazing on the west wall, and place the great room's glass on the south." That sentence is free and the software obeys it. If you already bought or downloaded an AI-generated plan, draw a compass on the cover sheet, measure the west-facing glazing, and compare it against 110 square feet. If the number is larger, get a mechanical contractor to confirm the cooling equipment was sized for the glass as drawn, not the glass the model assumed. If you are a builder offering AI plans as a product, make the orientation question mandatory in the intake flow instead of optional, because every default your software ships is a thirty-year commitment your customer never signed.

Limitations

The penalty calculation is a steady-state estimate, not a simulated building, which means real numbers shift with shading, overhangs, thermal mass, and thermostat behavior, while those 800 cooling hours and the 32-cent rate are Sacramento-plausible assumptions rather than metered data, and different climates and rate structures will produce different totals. No public dataset exists of AI-generated plans' window distributions, so the claim that generators place glass west rests on product design (orientation is not modeled) plus the visual tropes of generated renders, not a plan corpus audit. The lab proposed that 110-square-foot cap in 2009; the energy code never adopted it. ZURU's reported improvements and FloorDrafter's product features are company claims, and the assessment that orientation is ignored in the default flow comes from the products' public interfaces, not their training data.

Steel-manning the other side

The strongest case against this article runs like this. Nobody promised that a floor plan generator was an energy model, and judging it for ignoring solar orientation is like judging a sketch artist for not being a structural engineer, because room adjacency, dimension accuracy, and client communication are the job, and the tools do that job well. Orientation belongs to the architect's later design phases, where a human professional has always handled it, and treating the generator's default as a final design mistakes a starting point for a finished house, and there is real truth in that. But defaults have gravity, and the plans these tools produce increasingly ship without that later phase, sold directly to homeowners who will never hire the architect the workflow assumes. When the sketch artist's output becomes the construction document, the sketch artist owns the orientation whether it asked for the job or not.
