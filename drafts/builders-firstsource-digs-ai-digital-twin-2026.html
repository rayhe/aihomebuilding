The Biggest Building-Products Giant in America Just Bet $25.3M That AI Will Remember Everything About Your House

By Frank DeLuca · September 12, 2026

Every project I have ever run ended the same way. Job trailers get hauled off, and the three-ring binders go into somebody's garage. Within eighteen months, nobody can find the window schedule, the plumbing as-built, or the paint formula for the touch-up. We build permanent objects with temporary memory. I have personally spent whole afternoons hunting for a single approved submittal while a buyer waited on the phone, and every superintendent over fifty reading this just nodded, because we have all lived it.

On August 25, Builders FirstSource announced it is leading a $25.3 million Series A for a startup called Digs, and signed a five-year commercial agreement alongside it. Digs builds an AI platform that ingests the whole messy pile, plans, change orders, emails, texts, finish selections, and structures it into one searchable record the company calls a digital twin of the home. CEO Ryan Fink describes it to HousingWire as "ChatGPT, but specifically trained on build data and it has access to your specific project." That pitch is doing a lot of work in one sentence, because it asks builders to believe that the chaos of texts, voice memos, revised PDFs, and supplier emails can be normalized into something queryable, that a superintendent will actually open the app instead of calling the framer, and that the resulting record will still be legible five years later when the warranty claim lands.

Builders FirstSource is not a startup with a slide deck. It is the country's largest supplier of structural building products, serving more than 140,000 professional builder customers. When a company with that distribution arm writes a $25.3 million check plus a five-year commitment, the impressive part is not the AI. It is the channel, already installed and reaching 140,000 builders.

The Takeoff Claim, With Real Math

Fink's headline number: Digs' AI can perform about 700 takeoffs in the time it takes one estimator to perform one. It sounds like marketing until you work out what takeoffs actually cost a mid-size builder.

Assume an estimator needs 90 minutes for one material takeoff on a typical single-family plan, a conservative number once options and revisions enter the picture. A builder running a 40-home community with four plan types, repricing material lists quarterly because lumber futures have a mind of their own, burns through 16 takeoff rounds a year. That is 24 estimator-hours annually, just to keep the numbers current enough to buy from. Fink's 700-to-1 ratio collapses the same work to roughly two minutes.

Real bottlenecks look like a lumber quote that expires in 72 hours, a framer who never reads the email, or a change order sitting on a superintendent's dashboard for six days because nobody wants to tell the buyer the beam cannot move, and no software ever fixed any of those things by making arithmetic faster.

Why This Time Might Be Different

Nobody in this industry needs reminding that the contech graveyard is the deepest one in venture capital. Katerra raised $2 billion promising to reinvent construction and collapsed in 2021. Veev raised $647 million and followed in 2023. Both are gone. Both tried to own the whole stack, factories and software and labor, and the stack owned them. I say that with love for the industry that buried them.

Digs is making a different bet. It is not building homes. It is selling to the people who already build them, through a distributor that already sells to those people. GeekWire reports the company, founded in 2022 in Vancouver, Washington, now has thousands of homes on its platform across all 50 states, 37 employees growing toward 60, and total funding past $47 million. Run the distribution math yourself: one percent of Builders FirstSource's 140,000 builder customers is 1,400 builders. If each puts ten homes a year on the platform, Digs goes from thousands of homes to six figures almost overnight. If that math sounds optimistic, consider the alternative every previous contech company had to attempt: a venture-backed startup spending five years and nine figures building a sales channel to reach the same builders that Builders FirstSource already invoices every single week, which is the entire reason the graveyard exists, and the entire reason this deal does not belong in it.

According to the RICS AI in Construction Report 2026, roughly two-thirds of construction professionals now use AI in some form, though only 19 percent use it regularly. That pilot-to-production gap is exactly the chasm where construction software usually falls in, and a five-year distribution deal with the biggest lumberyard in America is a bridge across it.

The Part Nobody Is Saying Out Loud

A digital twin is only as good as the data fed to it, and job sites are terrible at producing clean data. Beautiful as the AI search looks in a conference room demo, it depends on superintendents and trade partners actually uploading, tagging, and updating through the whole build. If the trades do not use it, the twin is a ghost: accurate on paper, empty in practice, and worse than no record at all because people will trust it. I have seen exactly one software rollout per decade that the trades adopted willingly, and none of them were sold on AI.

Then there is the ownership question, and I want you to sit with it. Builders FirstSource is your lumber supplier. If your entire project history, your selections, your warranty records, your client's conversations, lives on a platform your supplier co-owns, what happens when you switch yards? Nobody has published the five-year agreement's data terms. Revenue, exclusivity, and data-rights terms are all undisclosed too. Until someone publishes them, treat the platform like any other vendor lock-in risk and get the data portability terms in writing.

What This Means for Your Next Build

If you are a GC running $2 to $5 million in residential work: the value here is not the 700-to-1 takeoff headline. It is change-order documentation and the warranty handoff. A searchable record of every selection and revision pays for itself the first time a buyer claims the tile was supposed to be the other one. Ask your BFS rep about pilot pricing, run your own takeoffs in parallel for a month, and trust your own numbers before you trust the demo. Before you sign anything, ask what happens to ten years of project history if you take your lumber business elsewhere, ask whether the export format is readable without a subscription, and ask who trains the model on your data, because the answers to those three questions will tell you more about the deal than any demo ever will.

If you are buying from a builder on this platform: demand the record. At closing, ask for the selections binder, the warranty documents, and the as-built information in a form that survives the trailer demolition. Fink pitches Digs as a "CarFax for the home," and a CarFax is only worth something because it transfers with the asset. Make sure yours does.

If you are a remodeler who buys materials from BFS: watch this space. Nothing in the announcement covers remodel workflows yet, but a distributor-owned platform will follow the money, and remodels are where the money is moving.

What We Don't Know Yet

SaaS pricing is undisclosed, so no honest ROI model exists for a specific builder. Treat the 700-to-1 takeoff figure as a CEO claim with no independent benchmark, and run your own parallel takeoffs before believing it. The five-year agreement's revenue, exclusivity, and data-rights terms are all undisclosed.

Nobody has measured whether a digital home record adds resale value. Compelling as the CarFax analogy is, it is completely unproven, because used-car buyers and new-home buyers are solving different problems, one buying a depreciating asset with a verifiable service history and the other making the largest financial commitment of their lives with people whose incentives point the other way. I would rather say that plainly than let you discover it at the closing table.

Twenty years of sideways projects taught me that paperwork is where builds go to die. If someone finally builds a memory for the house, after two decades of watching critical documents vanish into glove compartments, job trailers, and the occasional landfill, that is worth paying attention to, and worth every superintendent's cautious optimism. Just make sure the memory belongs to the homeowner. Not the lumberyard.
