Your Submittals Get Rejected 35% of the Time. Each One Costs You $805.

By Frank DeLuca · September 6, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Friday, 4:47 in the afternoon. Your project engineer opens the email he has been waiting on for three weeks. Stamped. And judged. In red, in the margin: the verdict, in handwriting your PE has started to recognize the way a man recognizes the cough of a relative who never pays him back. Revise and resubmit.

That submittal, the window schedule for the second floor, was supposed to lock the rough opening dimensions last month, back when the framer still trusted the schedule and the lumber package was still wrapped in plastic and waiting. Now the framer is standing at the job trailer asking whether he can start the second-floor walls, and the honest answer, the one your PE delivers while staring at a point somewhere over the framer's left shoulder, is that nobody knows, because a stack of paper in an office forty miles away has not finished its round trip, and the round trip is the job.

In twenty years of building, I have never once heard a superintendent say the words "submittal process" with affection, and I have heard supers discuss roofing felt, inspector visits, and portable-toilet placement with something approaching warmth by comparison. Here is why they say them the way they do, with the exhausted familiarity of men who have watched the same stack of drawings circle the same chain of inboxes for longer than some of their marriages have lasted.

Two to Six Weeks, Every Time

A submittal, for those who don't live this paperwork life, is the formal proof that what the contractor plans to install matches what the architect specified. Shop drawings for the framing package. Product data for the windows. Appliance cuts. Waterproofing data sheets. On a custom home, you're looking at somewhere between fifty and eighty of them, which is modest next to commercial work but still enough that a project engineer can spend a full month of his year shepherding paper through a chain that was designed before email existed. On a large commercial project, the count runs past two thousand, and the submittal log itself becomes a full-time administrative job, a spreadsheet that needs feeding, chasing, and babysitting for the entire duration of construction.

Each one's journey reads like a bureaucratic fable. It starts with the subcontractor, who submits to the general contractor, who reviews it and forwards it to the architect, who distributes it to the engineering consultants, each of whom adds a few days while juggling three other projects that are also on fire. Industry reporting puts the full cycle at two to six weeks per submittal, with complex equipment and shop drawings running longest. Each handoff along the chain eats a day or two, and the return trip, comments coming back down the same chain in reverse, eats three to four more. A University of Wisconsin study of actual submittal logs found that contractors spend roughly eleven of those days doing nothing but waiting, which tells you that most of the timeline is not review. It is queue.

And the queue rejects people. A BuildSync survey of more than 6,000 construction professionals found that 35 percent of submittals get rejected on the first pass, and that each rejected submittal costs about $805 when you add up the reviewing, the revising, the resubmitting, the phone calls, and the hours your engineer spent doing this instead of walking the job. Scale that to the 2,000-submittal commercial project, the kind with its own trailer park of jobsite offices and a document-control specialist who does nothing else, and you get 700 rejections and $563,500 in direct rejection cost on a single job. Not overhead. Not contingency. Churn.

Each rejection, according to research compiled by Plan Academy, typically adds two to three weeks to the timeline, and submittal and design-change issues show up as a delay factor in 35 to 40 percent of projects. On the critical path, that arithmetic gets ugly fast: the rooftop unit rejected in month two pushes the ductwork, the ductwork pushes the electrical, the electrical pushes the ceilings, and the two-week rejection cycle becomes a six-week delay that touches every trade on site. An academic study in the Engineering Research Journal ranked rework due to errors as the single most severe factor in shop-drawing delay, ahead of everything else they measured. Not the weather. Not the labor market. Paperwork about paperwork.

The Robot That Reads Them For You

This is the backlog that Procore decided to hire AI for. In May, the company rolled out a suite of AI agents built on Datagrid, the vertical-AI firm it acquired in January 2026, and one of the five prebuilt agents is a submittal reviewer. It reads the submittal, compares it against the project specifications, writes a review summary, and flags the discrepancies directly inside the submittal record. Alongside it: an RFI agent that does the same trick for requests for information, and a deep-search agent that cross-references specs, drawings, and RFIs for conflicts, citing its sources. Human beings keep final sign-off, because the agents' actions require approval before they complete, which means the liability chain still ends at a desk with a human stamp, exactly where it always has.

Alain Waha, the chief technology officer at Buro Happold, said his firm is on track to cut the construction-management work around RFIs and submittal reviews by half. That is the kind of claim you usually hear from a software vendor's slide deck and almost never from the CTO of somebody else's company, which is why it caught my attention. Autodesk's Pype AutoSpecs has been attacking the adjacent problem for years, reading the spec book and generating the submittal register automatically, which means nothing that requires approval escapes onto the job unreviewed, and nothing quietly becomes a change order six months later when somebody notices the missing firestopping data sheet.

Here's the part the vendors won't put in the brochure. Every one of these tools was built for the 2,000-submittal job, not the 60-submittal house. For now, these agents are rolling out as paid, credit-consumption products aimed at enterprise accounts, which is the polite way of saying the pricing page was designed for companies with procurement departments rather than the three-person GC shop. If you are the custom builder running four projects out of a trailer, you have not been invited to this particular future. Which means the interesting question for residential was never really about what the AI does in the demo video. It is whether the math works for you anyway, on a $600,000 house with sixty submittals and one PE who is also the safety guy and the scheduler.

Nobody Published the Residential Math. So Here It Is.

All the headline numbers in this business are commercial numbers. Nobody has published what the submittal churn costs on an actual house, so I worked it out. You can check my arithmetic, and you should.

A $600,000-to-$1-million custom home generates roughly sixty submittals: window and door schedules, framing or steel shop drawings, mechanical, electrical, and plumbing product data, appliance cuts, finish selections, waterproofing systems. Apply the industry's 35 percent first-pass rejection rate and you get 21 rejections on one house. That $805 BuildSync figure is weighted toward commercial project engineers with six-figure burdened rates, so I will discount it for residential: a PM reworking a rejected submittal burns four to six hours at a $75-per-hour burdened rate, call it $300 to $450 a pop. Twenty-one rejections lands between $6,300 and $9,450 in pure rework labor on one project.

Then the schedule. Prior analysis on this site estimated carrying cost on a custom home at roughly $1,066 per month, or about $266 a week. A rejected submittal's two-to-three-week cycle adds $530 to $800 in carrying cost before you count trade-scheduling cascades or anything resembling liquidated damages. Put the two together and you get $12,000 to $17,000 per custom home burned in document churn. That's two to three percent of a $600,000 budget spent on revising things that were already drawn once.

Now look at that number and ask the only question that matters: what would you pay to cut it in half? If a submittal-review tool saves you even a quarter of that churn on one project, it has paid for itself. If your first-pass rejection rate is above 25 percent, though, the tool is not your problem. Your specs are. And that brings us to the part nobody at the vendor wants to discuss.

What the AI Cannot Fix

The review agent reads the submittal and compares it to the spec. Nobody has built the agent that asks whether the spec is any good.

On a commercial job, the spec book runs five hundred pages, written by a team that does nothing but write spec books. On your custom home, the spec book runs thirty to fifty pages, written by an architect who is also designing three other houses, and whole products simply are not addressed. A disturbing share of those 35 percent rejections are not lazy subs getting it wrong. They are correct subs colliding with a spec that was vague, contradictory, or silent. Automating the review against a bad spec does not fix the churn. It just makes the rejection arrive faster, with more confident formatting.

But the residential bottleneck is the wrong bottleneck for these tools, because it sits upstream of review, in the quiet chaos of subs who were supposed to submit six weeks ago and have submitted nothing at all. Your real problem is usually not that review is slow; it is that the electrical sub submitted the panel cuts six weeks late, or submitted a catalog cut instead of the engineered submittal the spec demanded, or never submitted at all and just ordered the thing. No reviewer agent, however clever, addresses a submittal that does not exist.

Then there is liability, sitting in the corner like an uninvited uncle. When the architect's stamp says approved but the AI pre-screen missed the mismatch between the submittal and the spec, whose insurance eats it? AIA contracts say nothing about AI review, and the exclusion clauses rolling through commercial general liability policies (we covered the ISO AI exclusions in August) suggest the insurers have opinions even if the contracts don't. Whoever files first gets an education. Until then, the architect signs, the architect owns it, and the AI is a very diligent intern whose mistakes are technically your professional liability.

Objections, at Full Strength

Stated at full strength, and I mean this the way a man means it when he tells you your foundation is cracked: this article is a commercial-software story wearing a residential costume, and the residential reader has every right to feel the costume seams. Those 35 percent and $805 figures come from commercial-weighted surveys; my residential discount to $300-to-$450 per rejection is my own estimate, built on one labor rate and one hours assumption, and on custom homes where the owner is also the GC, the whole apparatus is an Excel sheet and a relationship. These AI agents are enterprise products at enterprise prices, and telling a builder with three employees to adopt them is like telling a food truck to buy a blast chiller. An honest version of the residential recommendation is not "buy the AI." It is "steal the discipline the AI enforces," which costs nothing, and the honest skeptic will note that the vendors selling you the reviewer are the same vendors whose platforms generated the queueing problem in the first place.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The $12,000-to-$17,000 residential churn figure is a first-principles estimate, not a measured number. It assumes the 35 percent commercial rejection rate applies to residential custom homes, and it very plausibly does not: residential rejections skew toward owner-driven finish changes rather than equipment mismatches, and custom-home rejection behavior is genuinely under-studied. My sixty-submittal count for a custom home is an experienced-builder heuristic, not survey data. That $1,066-per-month carrying cost figure comes from this site's earlier long-tail analysis and assumes a 6.3-month residential timeline. And the Buro Happold 50 percent reduction figure is a vendor-cited projection from a firm that is not yet at full deployment, as of Procore's May 2026 announcement. Treat the agents' performance as claimed, not measured.

What to Do With Your Next Submittal Package

If you are a GC running $600K-to-$2M custom homes: build the submittal register before you break ground, using AutoSpecs-style spec extraction if you have it, or two disciplined hours with the spec book if you don't. A missed submittal is invisible delay, the most expensive kind. Require subs to submit six weeks ahead of need, not two; the cycle is two to six weeks and the queue is not a negotiation. Track your own first-pass rejection rate the way you track your lumber takeoffs, in a running log that nobody is allowed to round off, because the number is the diagnostic: above 25 percent, the problem is your specs and no reviewer will save you; below 15 percent, you are exactly the shop these AI tools will help most.

If you are the owner-builder, the person with the most money at stake and the least institutional protection, demand the register from your GC before a single trade mobilizes. It is a one-page document and it tells you whether the person running your project has done this before. A contractor who cannot produce a submittal register on demand, right there at the kitchen table, has just told you everything you need to know about how your schedule will go, and it will not go the way the Gantt chart says.

And if you are evaluating Procore's agents or any competitor's when they reach your market: run the number from this article against your own rejection history, not the vendor's case study. One avoided rejection per quarter, one two-week delay that never materializes and one round of PE rework that never gets billed, pays for a lot of software over the life of a project. But software never fixed a spec that nobody wrote properly in the first place. That is still a human job, and on a custom home, it is probably yours.
