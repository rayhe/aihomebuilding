Your Washing Machine Makes 60 Gallons of Free Irrigation Water a Day. Your Plumber Buried the Option Under the Slab.

By Priya Greenwood, September 13, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

Every load of laundry produces roughly 15 gallons of water that your fruit trees would love. Not metaphorically. That number comes from a study commissioned by the city of Santa Rosa, which measured laundry-to-landscape greywater systems across real households and found 15 gallons saved per person per day. (realtor.com / Greywater Corps) For a family of four, that is 60 gallons a day of irrigation water that currently goes straight to the sewer, gets treated at a plant, and comes back to you on a bill.

The waste is structural, not behavioral. Up to 60 percent of a home's indoor water use was never dirty in the first place. It came from showers, bathtub drains, bathroom sinks, and the washing machine. Plumbers call this graywater; it excludes toilets, kitchen sinks, and dishwashers. (Santa Clara Valley Water) Meanwhile about half of all household water goes to the landscape. Put those two numbers together and the mismatch is absurd: the house manufactures irrigation water upstairs while the homeowner buys irrigation water from the utility to spray on the yard downstairs.

The simplest fix is a laundry-to-landscape system. A diverter valve on the washer's drain hose, a line of tubing through the wall, and mulch basins at the outlets. No permit is required for simple systems in many jurisdictions. A handy homeowner can build one in a weekend for $150 to $700. (GreenCitizen) Installed by a contractor it runs $1,500 to $3,000, and water utilities from Santa Clara Valley to Austin to Tucson offer rebates that often cover the materials entirely. (Valley Water) Reported savings range from 4,500 to 14,500 gallons per household per year, enough to irrigate 800 square feet of trees, shrubs, and vines. (Valley Water)

That is the easy version, and the economics are the kind that make you wonder why every new house does not include one. For a deeper answer, look at what UCLA's Luskin Center for Innovation found when it priced graywater collection in Los Angeles. Their cost model treats the installation of graywater collection and distribution in new construction as essentially negligible: materials only, roughly $120 per bathroom plus the laundry machine, with labor absorbed into the normal plumbing rough-in. (UCLA Luskin) Retrofitting is a different universe. On a slab-on-grade foundation, which is how most new American homes are built, their model assigns 16 plumber-hours to the first bathroom alone, eight hours for each additional bathroom, and eight more for the laundry connection. That is $1,500 to $2,600 in labor before a single fitting is purchased, before trenching, before mulch basins.

Run the payback both ways and the timing problem is impossible to miss. Take a four-person household saving 15 gallons per person per day: 60 gallons daily, 21,900 gallons a year, about 29 hundred-cubic-feet. At a California marginal rate near $8 per CCF, that is roughly $230 a year off the water bill. A rough-in during construction costing $300 to $600 in pipe and valves pays for itself in under two years. A whole-house retrofit at $15,000 or more, which is what Valley Water quotes for complex systems, takes over 60 years to pay back. The systems last about 15 years. (Alliance for Water Efficiency) Same water. Same house. A 25-to-1 cost ratio, decided entirely by whether the pipe went in before the slab was poured.

Technology is creeping toward the indoor half of the problem. Greyter Water Systems builds a washer-and-dryer-sized unit that captures shower and bathtub water and filters it for toilet flushing, and the company has more than 100 multi-family and commercial installations. (Green Builder Media) At UC Merced, an NSF-funded team is integrating AI into solar photocatalytic panels that kill microbes like E. coli and strip chemical compounds from graywater, warming the water in the process so it doubles as thermal energy. (UC Merced) Machine learning is also improving the demand side: Cornell researchers built a predictive irrigation model combining plant physiology, soil moisture, and weather forecasts that cut water use 40 percent versus traditional schedules. (Lab Manager / Cornell)

Now the honest part, because the National Academies said it plainly and it deserves quoting: "water savings associated with gray water irrigation at the household scale have not been demonstrated with confidence." (National Academies, 2016) Their review found the maximum-potential figures "do not reflect what can be realistically achieved." Field researchers found that many homeowners who installed systems responded by planting more beds, increasing demand and erasing some of the savings. A 60-gallon-a-day irrigation credit only materializes if the yard would have been watered with potable water anyway; for a xeriscaped yard or a house in a wet climate, the marginal value drops toward zero. Graywater also comes with rules: no bleach, no boron, nothing toxic down the drain, and the mulch basins and filters need maintenance. The indoor-reuse appliances need dual plumbing, treatment, and permits, which pushes them into thousands of dollars per unit with no mass-market residential product shipping yet. The UC Merced AI system is a Phase 1 research prototype. It is not a SKU.

What we did not prove

Several things. The 37-home California study behind the 17-gallon-per-person-day figure is small and self-selected; water-conscious early adopters are not a representative sample, and their savings likely run high. (AWE) The UCLA cost factors use Los Angeles-area labor rates from 2014-era data, so current costs run higher everywhere. The payback math is rate-sensitive by a factor of ten or more across US utilities; at $2 per CCF in a low-rate city, even the cheap rough-in takes a decade. And nobody publishes itemized residential pricing for the indoor-reuse appliances, which means the toilet-flushing half of this story is still priced in "call for a quote."

What to do with this

If you are building: ask your plumber for a graywater-ready rough-in before the slab pour. A separate graywater branch, a stub-out to the yard side, a three-way diverter valve on the laundry line. It is a few hundred dollars in materials and about ten minutes of conversation, and it is the single cheapest water investment in a new build. If you are buying an existing home: laundry-to-landscape is the sweet spot, and your water utility probably has a rebate page for it. If you are a Florida builder or homeowner: the state's SB 358 tax incentive, worth up to $4,200 per residential system, sunsets in 2027. That is a calendar deadline, not a marketing one. (Greyter / FL SB 358) And one rule for everyone: do not buy a $9,000 pumped system to save $200 a year on water. The Alliance for Water Efficiency's payback tables show that professionally installed complex systems almost never pay back within their own 15-year lifespan. Keep it simple, keep it gravity, and run the pipe before the concrete.
