Your New House Is Designed, Permitted, and Built. The Grid Says Wait Until 2037.

By Elena Vasquez · July 28, 2026

Imagine a row of finished townhomes in west London. Walls painted, kitchens fitted, front doors hung and locked, every detail attended to except the one that matters most. Families have signed purchase agreements, moving trucks are scheduled, and the electricity provider says the grid connection might arrive in eleven years.

That happened. A London Assembly report found that completed housing projects in the boroughs of Ealing, Hillingdon, and Hounslow were told they could be waiting until 2037 for a connection after the local electricity grid reached full capacity. Officials quietly discussed pausing new housing in entire boroughs until the grid caught up.

What consumed all that capacity? Data centers.

Not Labor. Not Lumber. Power.

We have written about data centers siphoning electricians from residential job sites, but that story was about bodies and this one is about electrons. Even if you find the electrician, wire every panel, pass every inspection, and satisfy every code requirement from foundation to roofline, the grid itself may not have capacity to serve your development, and no amount of money will move your name higher in the queue. A labor shortage responds to wages, but a grid capacity deficit responds to nothing short of new substations, new transmission lines, and years of regulatory approval that nobody can accelerate with a bigger check.

In Nevada, NV Energy, the state's dominant utility, informed Liberty Utilities, which serves 49,000 customers around Lake Tahoe, that it will stop providing wholesale power after May 2027. NV Energy's reason was blunt: it needs that capacity for Google, Apple, and Microsoft data centers clustering near the Tahoe-Reno Industrial Center, facilities that already consumed 22 percent of Nevada's total electricity generation in 2024, a share that has doubled in three years and shows no sign of leveling off. Twelve proposed data center projects in Northern Nevada represent 5,900 megawatts of new demand by 2033, a figure that would require the equivalent of five new power plants, according to Desert Research Institute analysis.

Forty-nine thousand residential customers, competing against trillion-dollar companies for the same electrons. No recourse.

Who Pays for the Wires You Will Never Use

In Wisconsin, the question has shifted from whether data centers affect housing to who bears the cost when they do. American Transmission Company filed plans in July 2026 to build more than $2 billion in new high-voltage lines, substations, and upgrades to serve large data centers.

ATC charges those costs to retail utilities like We Energies, which passes them directly to residential and commercial customers through monthly rate adjustments that most people never scrutinize until the number is large enough to notice. Without regulatory intervention, residential ratepayers absorb the infrastructure costs for data center facilities they will never use, facilities that generate no local economic benefit for the family paying the surcharge on their monthly bill.

We Energies has earmarked $620 million in data-center-related transmission charges for 2027 and 2028, costs that will flow to the monthly bills of every residential customer in southeastern Wisconsin whether or not a single data center job materializes locally. A Marquette University Law School poll from July found that 75 percent of Wisconsin voters now view data centers unfavorably, up from 55 percent nine months earlier, a shift in sentiment that helps explain why ATC's July filing is the first attempt by any transmission operator in the country to directly assign data center infrastructure costs to the technology companies whose demand triggered them.

Every other state is watching. Whether FERC approves it will determine whether any regulatory mechanism exists to prevent residential ratepayers from subsidizing the infrastructure costs of trillion-dollar technology companies whose electricity demand triggered the need for those very lines and substations.

What This Means If You Are Building a Home

Virginia illustrates the full cascade. One in every four kilowatt-hours generated in Virginia already goes to a data center. Not a hospital. Not a factory. A server farm. Dominion Energy, which had not raised its base rates in thirty-four years, proposed adding $8.51 per month to the average residential bill in 2026, driven largely by the cost of reinforcing transmission infrastructure for the data centers that have made Loudoun County the largest such corridor on earth. Nationally, the average residential electricity rate hit 17.45 cents per kilowatt-hour in January 2026, up 9.5 percent year-over-year, and the trajectory is accelerating in every state where data centers are densely concentrated.

If you are planning a development in the PJM footprint, which spans Virginia to Illinois and serves 67 million people, the grid interconnection queue for large connections now runs three to four years. Individual residential connections carry shorter timelines, but they depend on distribution capacity strained by upstream data center load; when a hyperscaler consumes the utility's capital budget for substations, every downstream connection waits. Do this now. Request a formal grid capacity assessment from your utility before breaking ground, not after framing.

Pre-wire for solar and battery storage — not as a luxury upgrade, but as structural insurance against a grid that may not be there when you need it. In Virginia, rooftop solar paired with storage lets a homeowner partially decouple from a grid whose capacity prices have increased tenfold in two years.

A Counterargument Worth Taking Seriously

Data center advocates argue that their industry is driving long-overdue investment in grid infrastructure that will ultimately benefit everyone, including residential customers. In March 2026, Meta, Oracle, Microsoft, and others signed a White House Ratepayer Protection Pledge committing to cover all infrastructure costs.

Fair enough. But a nonbinding pledge is not a tariff, which is precisely why Wisconsin's Public Service Commission had to create a separate electric rate class for data centers and why ATC filed its own cost-assignment proposal a month later when the rate class failed to cover transmission costs. Families buying homes in 2026 will absorb rate increases for years, possibly a full decade, before any systemic benefit from grid modernization materializes in the form of lower costs or greater reliability at the meter. The families who bought homes in Ealing and Hillingdon that cannot turn on their lights are not reassured by the promise that grid modernization will eventually benefit everyone.

Limitations

Several London projects received interim connections after GLA negotiations with National Grid and Ofgem, though others in Hillingdon remain years from full service. Nevada's situation is evolving, with Liberty Utilities' emergency procurement petition pending before California regulators. Wisconsin's ATC proposal was newly filed in July 2026, and FERC has 60 days to respond; the outcome is uncertain. Most critically, individual residential service connections are not directly queued behind data centers in any utility interconnection system, meaning the delay mechanism is indirect, operating through constrained distribution capacity and deferred substation upgrades rather than through a literal first-come-first-served line. The mechanism is real, but calling it a "queue" is a simplification.
