Your Panel Is 100 Amps. Your Electrification Wish List Is 180. The Math Your Electrician Skips.

By Priya Greenwood, September 19, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

Maya is a composite, but you have met her. Heat pump in March, induction range in June, heat pump water heater in August. Her 1,800 square foot Sacramento ranch had shed its last gas appliance, and the utility bill had fallen by a third. Then she bought the EV, ordered the 32-amp Level 2 charger, and called her electrician for the permit. He ran the load calculation, looked at the number, and handed her a quote for a panel upgrade: $3,400. The charger itself was $600; making it legal cost almost six times that. Read that twice.

Maya is not an edge case; she is 48 million homes.

That is the count from Pecan Street's panel sizing whitepaper, the nonprofit research outfit in Austin that tracks this stuff with unusual rigor: roughly 48 million American single-family homes sit on 100-amp service that the National Electrical Code says cannot fully electrify, even though their own 407-home study found peak draws above 20 kilowatts are rare and driven almost entirely by resistance heat and EV charging stacking up at the same time. An EPRI analysis cited in a recent arXiv field study puts the share needing a panel upgrade at 24 percent for full electrification with an EV, 35.8 percent in the Midwest. Somewhere between those estimates, tens of millions of households will face Maya's exact quote, and most of them will pay it, because almost nobody shows them the line of the code that offers a way around it.

Blame the paper math, not the code

Every panel upgrade quote begins with the same ritual: the NEC Article 220 load calculation, the official formula that decides how much electricity a house is allowed to pull. For a single-family dwelling, the optional method, 220.82, works like this. Take 3 volt-amps per square foot of living space. Add 1,500 volt-amps for each of two small-appliance circuits and 1,500 for laundry. Pile on the nameplates of your big appliances. Take the first 10,000 volt-amps at face value, then count everything above that at 40 percent, on the theory that a house never runs everything at once. Finally, add the larger of your heating or cooling load at 100 percent.

It is conservative by design, assuming the worst realistic day. And it is the reason Maya's charger became a $3,400 problem.

Before the code's demand factors do their quiet work, the raw appetite of the wish list is worth staring at. Add up every nameplate with no discounts: 5,400 for lighting and receptacles, 3,000 for the small-appliance circuits, 1,500 for laundry, 4,500 each for the heat pump and the water heater, 9,600 for the range, 5,000 for the dryer, 1,200 for the dishwasher, 7,680 for the charger. That is 43,380 volt-amps, or 180.8 amps of desire connected to a 100-amp panel. The entire game of the load calculation is deciding how much of that 180 you are allowed to ignore.

Let me run the actual numbers on a model house, because this site shows its math and this is the whole story. Start with a model home: 1,800 square feet, all electric, a heat pump with no supplemental strip heat at 4,500 volt-amps, a heat pump water heater at 4,500, a 9.6-kilowatt induction range, a 5-kilowatt dryer, a dishwasher, and the standard lighting and laundry allowances. Run it through 220.82 and you get 22,580 volt-amps. Dividing by 240 volts gives 94.1 amps, so it fits with room to spare.

Now add the 32-amp charger at 7,680 volt-amps. With the charger, the general load jumps to 37,380 volt-amps, the demand calculation lands at 25,452, and divided by 240 you get 106.1 amps. Over by six. The permit is denied. That six-amp margin is what the $3,400 buys: not real capacity you need, but arithmetic headroom the code demands.

Only one appliance broke the budget, and it was the car charger

Notice what the calculation just proved: not the heat pump, not the induction range, not the water heater. Only the EV charger broke the panel, and it broke it by six amps on a spreadsheet, not on a hot wire in your wall. Pecan Street's data says your actual peak draw rarely approaches these numbers at all: in their 407-home sample, 1.5 percent of homes produced more than 95 percent of all above-20-kilowatt data points.

Everyone else, the 98.5 percent of homes that look like yours, barely registered, which raises the obvious question: if the charger is the only thing pushing you over, and if it mostly runs at 2 a.m. when the induction range is stone cold, why does the code get to count the charger's full nameplate against your panel as if it were blasting at noon alongside everything else?

Here is the part your electrician may never mention: it does not have to.

NEC 625.42: the escape hatch in plain sight

The National Electrical Code has had an answer to this problem since the 2023 edition. Section 625.42 says that where an automatic energy management system is used, the maximum equipment load on a service "shall be the maximum load permitted by the automatic load management system." In ordinary language: if a listed smart device caps your EV charger at 16 amps during peak hours, the inspector can count 16 amps in the load calculation instead of 32. Any such system has to be certified and fail-safe, dropping to a safe level if the control signal dies, but those products exist and utilities already approve them.

This is not a gray area or a loophole that inspectors grudgingly tolerate. It is black-letter code, and the 2026 NEC edition makes it more important, not less: per industry analysis of the new edition, EV chargers must now be calculated at 100 percent of rated amperage with no demand reduction at all, which pushes even more homes over the limit on paper. The management-system path is the code's own pressure valve.

Run Maya's house again with a managed charger capped at 16 amps, and swap the 9.6-kilowatt range for an 8-kilowatt model. With those two changes, the general load falls to 32,940 volt-amps, the demand math lands at 23,676, and divided by 240 that is 98.7 amps. It fits, with 1.3 amps to spare, and nobody touched the panel. The car still charges overnight at half rate, when nothing else is drawing, and the homeowner keeps her $3,400.

Smart EVSE units with this capability run $600 to $1,200 installed. Whole-home smart panels like Span run higher but manage every load in the house, and Canary Media documented one Span-equipped home staying under an 80-amp ceiling simply by postponing EV charging when other loads spiked. Those 100-to-200-amp upgrade quotes run $1,300 to $4,000 for a straightforward job per EngineerFix, $1,300 to $3,000 in Berks County as of 2026, and up to $4,500 or more when the meter base, trenching, or the utility's transformer gets involved, per Kalahari Electrical. Then there is the part nobody prices into the quote: weeks of permitting, a day with the power off, and the utility possibly rebuilding their side of the drop.

There is also a sustainability case that goes beyond the money, and it is the one that hooked me. Every avoided panel upgrade is a panel not manufactured, copper not mined and pulled, and a truck that never rolls. Electrification is supposed to shrink our material footprint, not mint 48 million new 200-amp boxes to make the math pretty. Load management is electrification with the waste designed out.

The honest objections

Now the other side, stated at full strength, because this column does not sell fairy tales.

That load calculation is conservative on purpose, and clever code-reading is how houses end up dark. My model home assumes a heat pump with no supplemental strip heat, a mild-climate luxury. Put that house in Minneapolis, where backup resistance heat stages on at twenty below, and 220.82 counts 65 percent of the strip nameplate, several extra kilowatts that eat the entire 1.3-amp margin and keep eating. On the worst night of the year, with the dryer running and all four induction zones on, the only thing between the family and a tripped main is the fail-safe on a smart device, and firmware updates do not inspire the same confidence as dumb copper that lasts forty years.

Future-proofing is the sharper objection, because a house that fits at 98.7 amps today has no room for a second EV, an ADU, or the 14-kilowatt pro range the next owner wants. A 200-amp service is a one-time $3,000 investment in every future this house might have. Smart panels get discontinued, apps get abandoned, and the company that made your energy manager can get acquired and sunshotted before your mortgage is half paid. It is boring, permanent, and never needs a password reset. Dumb wins sometimes.

There is a fair middle position: use the management system to avoid the upgrade now, when the money matters and the loads are known, and size the conduit and meter socket for 200 amps anyway so the future upgrade is a panel swap, not a rebuild. Copper in the ground is cheap; regret is expensive.

What this analysis did not prove

Let me flag the gaps in my own math, because the rigor rules here demand it.

First, how an EV charger enters the 220.82 optional calculation is genuinely contested. Some electricians and inspectors apply the 40 percent demand factor to it as a general appliance load, as I did, while others insist it counts at 100 percent with no reduction, which pushes the unmanaged number even higher. Your AHJ's reading controls, not mine.

Second, nameplates vary wildly. Real induction ranges run 8 to 14 kilowatts, and my 8-kilowatt pick is the smaller end of the 30-inch market. A 12-kilowatt range adds roughly 7 amps to the final number and kills the managed scenario.

Third, I used the 2023 NEC. The 2026 edition's stricter EV treatment and its lower lighting allowance (3 to 2 volt-amps per square foot) reshuffle these numbers, and most jurisdictions have not adopted it yet.

Fourth, the heat pump water heater's 4,500 volt-amp nameplate assumes resistance backup engaged. In heat-pump-only mode it draws a tenth of that, which means real-world peaks are lower than my calculation and the margin is actually better than it looks.

Finally, I did not model a cold-climate home with strip heat, where none of this works and the upgrade is simply the right answer.

The takeaway for your project

If you are electrifying and an EV charger is in the plan, do three things before you sign a panel-upgrade quote. First, ask your electrician for the actual load calculation, in writing, with the EVSE entered both unmanaged and managed under NEC 625.42. Many electricians run the standard method by habit and never price the management option. Second, right-size the big nameplates: the difference between an 8-kilowatt and a 12-kilowatt induction range can be the entire margin, and nobody tastes the difference in a weeknight stir-fry. Third, schedule the charger for overnight hours and put the management in writing on the permit, because the inspector needs to see a listed fail-safe system, not a promise to charge after midnight.

If your calculation fails even with management, if you live where strip heat is a winter reality, or if a second EV is already in the driveway plan, take the upgrade and sleep well, because copper is honest. No shame in it.

But if you are Maya, with one charger, a mild climate, and a panel that fits everything except six amps of spreadsheet, do not buy $3,400 of headroom you will never draw. Buy the $900 smart charger, show the inspector the code section, and spend the savings on the heat pump water heater that actually cuts your bill. Greenest of all is the panel already bolted to your garage wall. Start there.
