The Code Demands 6 Inches of Fall in 10 Feet. Clay Swallows 0.1 Inches an Hour.

By Elena Vasquez · October 4, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Six inches, ten feet, one sentence of building code.

Section R401.3 of the International Residential Code requires that lots "be graded to drain surface water away from foundation walls," and specifies the grade: a minimum fall of six inches within the first ten feet. It is the most consequential sentence most homeowners will never read. It is also, in a specific and measurable way, incomplete: it specifies the geometry of the dirt while saying nothing about the appetite of the dirt, and clay soil drinks water at a tenth of an inch an hour, which on a rainy Saturday is the difference between a dry basement and a $4,377 restoration invoice.

This is the gap drone LiDAR now lets us see with embarrassing clarity: a single morning flight produces a digital elevation model of a lot accurate to centimeters, and the software can paint exactly where water will stand, where the grade goes flat against a wall, and where the swale pinches shut. Three days of survey crew work compressed into a drone's morning. But measurement is not the same as remedy, and the most interesting thing the maps reveal is not where the grading failed but where it was code-compliant and failed anyway.

The water disagrees.

What the Code Promises

R401.3 is blunt. For most of the country, it is sufficient. Six inches of fall in ten feet is a five percent slope, steep enough that water cannot politely ignore it. California's Title 24 residential code carries the identical sentence. Even the exception anticipates the hard cases: where lot lines or walls prohibit the full six inches, the code accepts a five-percent slope with drains or swales to carry the water away.

Notice what the rule quietly assumes: that water arriving on the surface can be moved across the surface, and that the ground underneath will drink whatever the slope does not carry off.

For sand, that assumption is generous and true; for clay, it is a polite fiction the data refuses to support.

Soil-Deficit Math

The USDA's Natural Resources Conservation Service publishes steady-state infiltration rates, and the numbers read like a verdict. Sand absorbs more than 0.8 inches per hour. Loam manages 0.2 to 0.4. Clayey soils take in 0.04 to 0.2 inches an hour, and sodic clays less than 0.04. (Hillel, 1982, still the reference the agency cites.) NRCS adds a sentence worth framing: little or no water penetrates frozen or saturated soil at all.

Now run a storm through it, and the numbers stop being abstract. One inch of rain on a 40-by-60-foot lot is 2,400 square feet times one-twelfth of a foot: 200 cubic feet, about 1,500 gallons, roughly the volume of a full hot tub arriving all at once and demanding somewhere to go.

On sandy soil absorbing 0.9 inches an hour, roughly nine-tenths of that storm disappears into the ground and 150 gallons become surface flow; on clay absorbing 0.1 inches an hour, the arithmetic reverses, barely a tenth infiltrates, and roughly 1,350 gallons must go somewhere across the surface. In hydrologic shorthand, a code-compliant lot on clay converts a 1-inch-per-hour storm into something like 20 gallons a minute of water demanding conveyance; the identical lot on sand asks for two or three, which is why two neighbors on the same street, built to the same code, can live in entirely different flood regimes.

Nobody at the counter pulling your grading permit will mention this, because the code never asks what kind of dirt it is, and the permit file will contain a plat, a floor plan, and not one word about infiltration. Minnesota's building code is the rare exception: it waives the foundation drain requirement outright on well-drained Group I soils, sand and gravel, which is the code's own quiet admission that soil, not geometry, is the governing variable.

Same slope, same code, different planet: a clay lot and a sand lot live under identical rules and inhabit different hydrologic worlds.

Math is merciless.

20 vs. 2-3 Gallons per minute of surface water a 1-inch-per-hour storm demands from a code-compliant 40x60-ft lot on clay versus on sand. Same grade. Same code. Ten-to-one difference in drainage load.

That is the finding nobody had bothered to put in one place: the most famous number in residential grading answers a question drainage never asked, while the industry's inspection rituals are still built around the number rather than the question. So the code tells you the shape of the surface, while drainage remains a capacity problem the shape alone cannot answer, no matter how precisely the surveyor drew it. Water that cannot enter the soil still has to leave the lot, and a five-percent slope only helps if whatever the slope delivers to has room to take it.

What the Laser Sees

Enter the drones, with their awkward but honest contribution. LiDAR and photogrammetry flights now deliver centimeter-level elevation models of a lot, and the analysis software flags negative grade against walls, ponding depressions, and swales that pinch shut, with a precision no tape measure can match. A job that took a survey crew three days is a morning's flight with a two-day model turnaround, according to the drone survey firms now marketing directly to landscape architects and builders. For a production builder grading dozens of lots, weekly flights turn finished grade from a single inspection into a continuous record, which means the inspection never ends. Finished grade becomes legible at a resolution the inspector's eye never had, and a depression the tape measure missed shows up as a blue puddle on the model.

This is genuinely useful, and it is not what the vendors think it is: the real value is a complete inventory of where the ground fails the drawing, delivered before the concrete is poured. But the AI does not solve drainage; it inventories failure. It is a camera with a contour map attached, and its output is only as good as the decision the builder makes after reading it, which is to say the intelligence in the pipeline is not artificial at all. I have a rule about optimization tools that measure old problems: they are honest about the measurements and optimistic about everything else, including whether anyone acts on what they measure. Clay's physics predates the drone by half a century, and no contour map has ever changed an infiltration rate.

Dirt Keeps Moving

There is a second, slower failure the maps are only beginning to catch. Fine Home Building's classic foundation-drainage detail contains a sentence that should be engraved on every backfill: compacted backfill settles about five percent of its height, and deep, lightly compacted fill settles more. A seven-foot foundation wall backfilled to grade will lose roughly four inches of fall within a few years, which means the code-compliant six inches the inspector measured at final inspection quietly becomes two. Nothing in the code requires anyone to look again. Compliance is a snapshot; soil is a process. No enforcement mechanism exists beyond a voluntary re-survey two or three years after backfill, and it has never been anyone's job to order one, which is why settlement failures surface as insurance claims instead of punch-list items.

This is where the drone has its most defensible use case for an ordinary house: a cheap flyover at year two or three, compared against the as-graded model, would catch settlement failures while they are still a grading problem instead of a waterproofing invoice. Waterproofing runs $2,000 to $7,000; water damage repair runs $3,000 to $50,000 and up, a finished basement turns every intrusion into demolition, which is the financial argument for measuring the dirt before you finish the walls. The economics favor the buyer. Nobody sells it.

Objections, at Full Strength

All of which risks making this sound like a drone problem, when the honest hierarchy of drainage fixes starts with a downspout. Here is the single highest-ROI drainage move in residential construction: it costs a few hundred dollars and a Saturday to extend the downspouts ten feet from the foundation. Fine Home Building's guidance goes further and states it as a prohibition: never connect downspouts to footing drains, because putting roof volumes of water closer to the footings, in the magazine's words, makes no sense at all. Start there. Most wet basements are downspout failures wearing the costume of grading failures, and a deeper steelman says the entire AI-grading apparatus may be measuring the wrong thing. Exterior french drains clog with silt, demand cleanouts, and are miserable to maintain, which is why every exterior excavation quote quietly assumes a maintenance burden the brochure never mentions. On an existing home, an interior perimeter drain feeding a sump is often cheaper and more reliable than re-excavating the foundation, which is why a buyer who LiDAR-scans a lot without walking the downspouts is a doctor ordering an MRI before taking a pulse. And FHB's own detail contains a heresy worth quoting: the gravel is the main water route in a footing drain, while the perforated pipe mostly "symbolizes good practice." Fifty years of aggregate have not been improved by a point cloud.

Nor does the soil math excuse bad building. Meanwhile, the IBC's foundation-drain specification is precise for a reason: gravel or crushed stone extending a foot past the footing, six inches above it, filter membrane on top, perforated pipe on two inches of washed gravel with holes aimed down, and deviate from any of it and the capacity the geometry promised never materializes. On the drain detail, the code got it right, and on the surface grade it got it half-right, but what remains missing is the ground itself, the one variable the code never names and the inspector never measures.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The 20-gallon-a-minute framing is a simplified rational-method illustration, not a stamped hydrology report; real site drainage depends on roof area, impervious coverage, and the storm's duration-intensity curve, and the runoff coefficient used here is a planning estimate. NRCS infiltration rates are steady-state laboratory values; field absorption varies with antecedent moisture, construction compaction, macropores, and frozen ground, and the agency itself notes that frozen or saturated soil takes in essentially nothing. No independent study demonstrates that drone grading verification reduces basement water claims on single-family homes; the evidence base is commercial and land-development scale, and the residential claim is extrapolation. Cost anchors are national averages that vary two- to three-fold by market and excavation depth, and the settlement math is a ceiling rather than a forecast: it assumes five percent backfill settlement, while heavily compacted granular backfill settles far less, so four inches is an upper bound, not a prediction.

What to Ask For on Monday

If you are buying: bring a line level and a tape measure to the showing. Ten feet out from the foundation, the dirt should sit six inches lower, and after a heavy rain you should watch where the water stands. Ask where the downspouts discharge. If the answer involves the footing drain, keep walking.

If you are building: spec the drain to the soil, not the generic detail. Four-inch perforated pipe, holes aimed down, on two inches of washed gravel, six inches of gravel cover, filter fabric over the gravel rather than wrapped around the pipe, cleanouts every fifty feet, and daylighted or led to a sump. Keep every downspout ten feet from the wall. Budget a re-grade survey two years after backfill; it is the cheapest insurance in the whole drainage assembly, and no one will remind you, because no trade owns the follow-up and no code requires it.

Measure both.

The code prescribes the shape water should take, but the soil decides what it will take, and the house you buy will live inside that decision for the next fifty years. Measure the first, respect the second, and let the drone tell you when the dirt has changed its mind.
