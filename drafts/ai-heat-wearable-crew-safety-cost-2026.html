One Heat Stroke Costs $53,589. The Armband That Catches It Costs $500.

By Marcus "Steel" Washington · October 4, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

On July 21, the House Education and Workforce Committee voted to prohibit OSHA from ever finalizing its heat standard.

No committee vote ever changed the weather. That same summer, researchers at George Washington University estimated roughly 28,000 workplace injuries a year linked to hot weather, and OSHA's own estimates put annual heat deaths above 550. In construction, the math is uglier than the average. According to the Center for Construction Research and Training, fatal heat injuries across all industries rose 77.4 percent between 2012 and 2023, from 31 to 55, while construction workers died of heat at nearly four times the rate of everyone else. An industry holding 7 percent of the American workforce absorbed about 34 percent of its occupational heat deaths.

Marcus has a rule about statistics like these, which is that every one of them was a person whose foreman had to make a phone call. So consider the number that should change how residential contractors staff a summer crew. Researchers at the Workers Compensation Research Institute analyzed 29,329 heat-related illness claims and found that a quarter of them, 25.5 percent, struck workers in their first sixty days on the job. Picture the new guy. An apprentice who hasn't acclimatized, who doesn't know yet that pride is a heat-stroke risk factor, who is trying to prove he belongs by outworking the thermometer.

Acclimatization is the unglamorous science at the center of all of this. A body new to hot, heavy work needs seven to fourteen days of ramped exposure before it can regulate itself under load, and the frozen federal rule would have made those schedules mandatory. Instead, the current enforcement regime is a five-year National Emphasis Program that OSHA revised on April 10, 2026, authorizing inspectors to expand any inspection on days the heat index crosses 80 degrees, plus the General Duty Clause, which is the legal equivalent of "you should have known better." Between April 2022 and December 2024, that regime produced roughly 7,000 heat inspections, 60 citations, and more than $2 million in penalties in 2024 alone. Citations are rare. Penalties, when they land, are anything but small.

The Band That Reads Your Blood

Into this gap walked an eleven-person company from Atlanta. SlateSafety, founded by Georgia Tech engineers, sells a palm-sized armband worn on the upper arm that uses light-based sensors, the same principle as a Fitbit or an Apple Watch, to estimate heart rate, exertion, and core body temperature, then alerts a supervisor when a worker is approaching dangerous heat stress. SlateSafety says its core-temperature algorithm was validated against ingestible temperature pills to within three-tenths of a degree. Their pitch is prediction instead of reaction: the band sees the emergency before the worker feels it, because by the time a person feels heat stroke coming on, they are already in it.

For construction tech, the pricing is unusually legible. Devices cost $500 each, covering the software, the cellular data, and support, with bulk discounts and free pilots for new customers. SlateSafety's founders claim revenue between $2 and $5 million, up 65 percent year over year, and tell a striking story about their first major deployment: a U.S. Air Force base in Texas where workers in extreme heat and protective gear were suffering more than thirty serious heat-stress injuries a year before the system went in during 2020, and, by the company's account, zero since. A competitor, Kenzen, sells its own core-temp system as a per-worker monthly subscription; the Department of Homeland Security gave the women-founded company $161,600 in 2022 to develop it for border personnel.

Strip away the salesmanship and the underlying claim is genuinely interesting. Everything else on a jobsite measures the environment: the heat index on a weather app, the thermometer on the gang box. It measures the worker. On a crew where the highest-risk people are the newest and the least likely to speak up, an objective physiological signal has a kind of democratic value. A sensor array does not care who is tough.

The Math That Matters

Here is the calculation nobody in the vendor's sales deck will do for you, because it requires combining their pricing with workers' comp data they would rather not headline. A fifteen-person residential crew, outfitted at $500 a band, costs $7,500. That is the entire investment. Software, data, support, included.

Now the other side of the ledger. OH&S Magazine puts the average heat-stress incident at $53,589, and that figure excludes wrongful-death litigation. WCRI's construction-specific numbers are grimmer still: for claims involving more than seven days of lost time, the average construction claim carried $22,752 in medical payments and $16,830 in indemnity at twelve months, roughly $40,000 combined, against a $25,000 all-industry average. In California, 2023 construction claims averaged $49,947 at twenty-four months. Divide the incident cost by the device cost and one prevented heat stroke funds 107 device-years. For a fifteen-person crew, a single avoided incident pays for every band on every arm for about seven years.

107 Device-years of $500 armbands funded by one prevented heat-stress incident at the $53,589 average incident cost. For a 15-person crew: roughly seven years of coverage.

Tenure data sharpens the case further. A crew with normal residential turnover is not buying protection for a uniform population. It is buying it for the exact cohort the data says is in danger: the worker in week three who has not acclimatized and does not yet know the crew's unwritten rules about when to stop. OSHA's 2024 penalty haul, $2 million plus, spread across a small citation count, means a single General Duty Clause citation after a serious incident can dwarf the cost of the devices by two orders of magnitude. In the coldest accounting, the band is cheap insurance against the most expensive kind of phone call a contractor can receive.

None of which makes it a substitute for the free stuff. Water, rest, shade, and a written acclimatization schedule cost almost nothing, and under the current NEP they are the documented baseline an inspector will ask about on any 80-degree heat-index day. Six states, California, Oregon, Washington, Nevada, Colorado, and Maryland, already enforce heat standards stricter than anything the frozen federal rule proposed. A band supplements a heat program; it is not one.

Objections, at Full Strength

Now the steelman, because this story has a dark twin and it deserves daylight. Strip the safety branding and the armband is a surveillance device. It collects biometric data from workers, and in residential construction those workers are disproportionately young, disproportionately Latino, often the newest and least powerful people on the site, and it streams that data to the boss's dashboard. A device built to say "take a break" can become, in the wrong hands, a device that says "the algorithm says you're fine, keep framing." A contractor who has spent $7,500 on monitoring has a financial incentive to believe the green light, and the worker whose body generates the data has the least leverage to question it. Before buying, a contractor should be able to answer three questions in writing: who owns the biometric data, who sees the dashboard, and what happens when a worker declines to wear the band.

Almost the entire evidence base is vendor-reported, and the gaps matter. That Air Force base story, thirty-plus injuries a year to zero, is a CEO's anecdote in a business magazine, not a peer-reviewed result. Kenzen's published accuracy work lists paid company employees among its authors, with devices provided free. No independent study has shown these bands reducing heat incidents specifically on residential construction sites, which are smaller, more transient, and less controlled than a military base. And there is a subtler hazard the vendors never mention: risk compensation. Give a foreman a dashboard of green lights and the schedule pressure does not vanish; it gets a permission slip.

To be fair to the skeptics: their case is not that the bands fail. It is that they succeed at the wrong level. Water within arm's reach, mandatory rest cycles, shade structures, acclimatization schedules for new hires, a foreman with the authority to stop work: these are the interventions with the strongest evidence behind them, and they require no one's biometric data and cost less than one band. If a contractor buys fifteen armbands instead of fixing a broken water-and-shade program, the technology is cover for negligence with a cellular data plan.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The crew-cost model is an order-of-magnitude illustration, not a quote. SlateSafety's $500 price comes from a CEO interview, not a published price list, and bulk pricing for larger crews is undisclosed. That $53,589 incident figure comes from a 2022 industry magazine article with unpublished methodology; the WCRI $40,000 construction figure covers a different claim universe at a different development stage, so treat the comparison as a range rather than a single number. Heat fatality counts are systematically undercounted, which is why this article uses OSHA's estimated 550-plus annual deaths alongside the official ~50 rather than pretending the official count is complete. Kenzen's current subscription pricing is unpublished, so the model uses SlateSafety's device price throughout. I found no independent randomized trial of heat wearables on residential jobsites, and the "thirty to zero" base story has not been independently audited. Finally, the federal-rule framing applies to federal-plan states; the six states with their own heat standards operate under stricter, citable rules regardless of what Congress does.

What to Do on Monday

If you run a residential crew, the honest play is a pilot, not a purchase order. SlateSafety and its competitors offer free pilots; run one on the crew with the highest turnover, because that is where the 25.5 percent lives, and measure it against a control summer if your records allow. Before the bands go on arms, put the data policy in writing: the worker owns the biometric data, the dashboard is visible to the safety lead and no one else, and declining to wear the band cannot affect assignments or hours. Then do the free things first, because the NEP inspector will ask about them before asking about your technology: water within a short walk, shade for rest breaks, a written acclimatization schedule that ramps new hires over seven to fourteen days, and a buddy system that pairs every new worker with someone authorized to call a stop.

If you are buying a home, none of this appears in your contract, but it is in your schedule. Heat incidents do not just injure workers; they stop production, trigger investigations, and push closings. A builder who can describe their heat program in specifics, not slogans, is a builder who has thought about the summer your house gets framed.

Federal rulemaking is frozen. Nothing in that committee vote changed the thermometer, and nothing changed the bodies under it. Construction will keep burying a wildly disproportionate share of heat's victims until the industry treats the first sixty days as the danger zone the data says it is. A $500 band that reads blood flow cannot fix a broken safety culture. But on a crew that already has water, shade, and a foreman willing to stop work, it is the cheapest second set of eyes the industry has ever been offered. He deserves both.
