Tuesday, 9 a.m. A crew unrolls synthetic underlayment across bare plywood. They go down fast, fifty feet at a time, cap nails cracking into the deck. By Thursday, shingles cover every inch of it. Whatever happened on that roof Tuesday is now sealed under twenty-five years of asphalt. Nobody took a single photograph.

That is the underlayment problem, and it is the weirdest quality gap in residential construction, because no other trade installs a critical waterproofing layer and buries it in the same week without a single timestamped photo entering the job file. Underlayment is the roof's actual waterproofing layer, the last thing between a wind-driven leak and your living room ceiling, and it is the only major building component installed and permanently entombed in the same week with no inspection record. Your county inspector photographs the footings; typical re-roof permits don't even require a mid-roof inspection, per one regional building department's reroofing guide. The underlayment gets a glance from the ladder. Then it disappears. Gone.

What a Bad Job Looks Like

Here is what a bad underlayment job looks like, because the failure modes are weirdly specific. Courses rolled uphill, so laps face the wrong way and every seam funnels water into the deck. Horizontal overlaps of two inches where the manufacturer demands six. Ice barrier skipped on the eaves because the crew ran out of self-adhered rolls and nobody wanted to drive to the supply house. Cap nails skipped, so the first gust before shingle day turns the underlayment into a sail. Each of these is a five-minute fix on Tuesday and a five-thousand-dollar surgery in year four, because by then the evidence is buried under two layers of shingles and a manufacturer warranty denial letter.

None of this is vague, which makes the violations sting. The 2021 International Residential Code, R905.1.1, says underlayment goes down per the manufacturer's instructions. Owens Corning's instructions say synthetic underlayment gets a six-inch horizontal overlap and a four-inch end lap, fastened with cap nails or staples, while fifteen-pound felt gets two inches and thirty-pound felt gets four. On low slopes, two-to-twelve up to four-to-twelve, every course must overlap the last by half the roll width plus an inch. And R905.1.2 demands an ice barrier from the eave to at least 24 inches inside the exterior wall line wherever ice dams have a history, two cemented layers or a self-adhered membrane. These numbers are printed in every installation guide sold in America, and they just never get verified.

The Capability Is Already Sitting There

This is where the drone people enter, because photographing a flat, high-contrast, pattern-repeating surface from twenty feet up is exactly what computer vision does well. Loveland Innovations' IMGING Detect, a deep-learning model running on drone imagery, already lists "exposed underlayment" among the damage classes it finds automatically. EagleView's autonomous drone platform measures roofs and flags anomalies, and the company explicitly markets it for validating work quality after a job. SiteAware runs AI deviation detection on centimeter-accurate models and documents the whole installation sequence, not just the finished roof. None of these were built for Tuesday-morning underlayment QA. The pieces are all on the table, scattered across four different products that have never been aimed at the same Tuesday morning. Checkatrade Labs in the UK is already running the consumer version of the idea: drone flights plus AI analysis verifying roofers' workmanship, surveys under £100, roughly 75 percent cheaper than a manual survey, with human double-checks until the AI earns trust. RoofersCoffeeShop's AI series notes the same shift: AI image recognition identifying shingle types, damage, and remote measurements as standard capability now.

Break-Even Math Nobody Runs for You

Run the money like a GC would. A consumer drone survey costs under £100 in the Checkatrade program, and US residential drone tiers run $150 to $400 for imagery with analysis, so call it $250, which buys the cheapest documented evidence on a job where every other line item runs to four figures or more, and which arrives before the evidence gets entombed under shingles. Stack that against one warranty-denied leak repair, the kind where a section of shingles gets peeled back, the rotted deck section gets cut out and replaced, the underlayment gets relaid to spec, and the shingles go back on with a crew billing hourly for the privilege of fixing what the original crew got wrong. That routinely runs into the low thousands; call it $3,000 for the math and adjust to your market.

Two hundred and fifty into three thousand is twelve to one, which is the break-even: the photo pass pays for itself if it catches one defect in every twelve roofs. A production crew doing two hundred roofs a year needs an eight-percent catch rate, and the defect does not even have to cause a leak to count. Catching a skipped ice barrier is worth the photo even if the homeowner never sees water, because the warranty paperwork just got its missing page. Not heroic.

Two honest caveats ride along with it, and the first is that nobody has published what share of re-roof leaks actually trace to underlayment laps versus flashing or fasteners, which means the twelve-to-one is arithmetic, not epidemiology, and any crew chief who has torn off enough failed roofs to know the real split is welcome to correct the math. And the math assumes the photo reviewer is independent. A free drone flight bundled with the roofer's bid has the same conflict baked in as the free ladder inspection.

The Six Checks Nobody Publishes

So here is the hold-point checklist, built by crossing the 2021 IRC against Owens Corning's install instructions. Six things a photo can actually prove before the shingles bury them. One, lap width: six inches on synthetic, two on fifteen-pound felt. Two, lap direction: upper courses over lower, always shingled downhill, so water can never run into a seam from above. Three, ice barrier extent: self-adhered membrane from the eave to 24 inches inside the wall line in ice-dam country. Four, fastener spacing: cap nails along every lap, not just the field. Five, low-slope doubling: half-width-plus-an-inch overlaps on 2:12 to 4:12 pitches. Six, valley lining: 36 inches of ice-and-water in open valleys before any field underlayment goes down. Six checks, one flight, and the photos live with the warranty paperwork forever.

What the Crew Chief Gets Right

Now the rebuttal, because any crew chief worth his salt has one ready, sharpened over twenty years of salesmen and inspectors and manufacturer reps telling him how to do the job he learned before they were born, and parts of it are right. First: this inspection already exists, and it is called the foreman. A good superintendent walks the underlayment before shingle day and catches the reversed course in ninety seconds, which is entirely true on crews that still have one. Production roofing runs on piece rates and weather windows, which is where the objection gets honest: underlayment goes down Tuesday morning and shingles start Tuesday afternoon, because the crew that stops for a drone operator on a clear Tuesday is the crew standing around in Wednesday's rain with an exposed deck and a homeowner asking why the schedule slipped.

Second: a photo proves lap width, not adhesion. Self-adhered ice barrier can look perfect and peel by August if the deck was dusty. No camera can grab the membrane and pull. Third: these AI models were trained to find damage on finished roofs, hail, blisters, ponding, not to audit installation technique on a half-done deck. Nobody has published accuracy numbers for lap-error detection, not Loveland, not EagleView, not the academic literature, so today's honest product is a very good camera plus a very fast human reviewer, and anyone selling you a certified install auditor today is selling you next year's roadmap dressed up as this year's product. Fourth, the practical one: commercial drone work in the US requires an FAA Part 107 pilot, so the hundred-dollar survey still needs a licensed operator standing in your driveway.

What This Analysis Didn't Prove

A few honest boundaries apply here. We could not find a published study measuring AI accuracy on underlayment installation defects anywhere in the literature, which means every vendor claim on this page is about finished-roof damage detection, and the leap from detecting hail damage on a finished roof to auditing lap widths on a half-installed deck is ours, stated plainly so you can discount it accordingly. Lap specs in this article use Owens Corning synthetic as the reference; GAF, CertainTeed, and every felt variant print their own numbers, and your crew's paperwork is the authority, not this article. The ice-barrier requirement applies in ice-dam regions designated by local code; in much of the Sun Belt it is not required at all, which shrinks the checklist there. And the break-even math treats the $250 inspection as independent; adjust both inputs to your market before spending a dime.

Should You Buy One?

If you are signing a re-roof contract this month: yes, and write it into the contract. Add one line to the contract: "Contractor to provide dated aerial or photo documentation of underlayment installation, showing laps, fasteners, and ice barrier extent, before shingle installation begins." The crew that balks at a photo was never going to do the six-inch laps.

Do the flight the same day the underlayment goes down, not the morning after the shingles. No exceptions. Keep the images with your warranty registration. That folder is the difference between a denied claim and a paid one when the stain appears in year six.

If your roof is a simple gable under fifteen squares with a crew you have used for a decade, skip it. The foreman you trust is the inspection. But if you are a first-time buyer watching a strange crew work on your one big asset, two hundred and fifty dollars is the cheapest insurance on the whole job. The shingles get the curb appeal. The underlayment does the work. Photograph the worker.
