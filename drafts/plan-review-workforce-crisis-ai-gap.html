Your City Has Three Plan Reviewers. Two Are Retiring. The AI That Could Replace Them Costs $59 a Month.

By Catherine Chen

Dallas, Texas charges you four weeks just to prescreen your residential building plans, a preliminary check that confirms the right documents are attached and formatted correctly. Then the actual plan review starts. That queue runs ten weeks. Fourteen weeks total before anyone with authority tells you whether your house complies with the Dallas Development Code.

In Aspen, Colorado, a major residential permit requires sixteen to eighteen weeks for the first round of review, and each correction cycle tacks on eight to ten more. In Redwood City, California, fifteen minutes from the headquarters of multiple AI companies valued in the hundreds of billions, new building plans sit for four to eight weeks while a handful of reviewers works through the stack.

These are not aberrations. They are published timelines on government websites, updated as recently as July 2026, offered without apology because the departments posting them lack the staff to do better.

And they are correct about that. They don't.

A Demographic Cliff Nobody Discusses

When the International Code Council and the National Institute of Building Sciences conducted the most comprehensive survey ever attempted of America's building code professionals, polling 3,850 ICC-certified officials across all fifty states, the results should have set off alarms in every jurisdiction that issues building permits. Eighty-five percent of code professionals are over 45 years old, and barely three percent are under 35, which means the pipeline of replacements isn't thin so much as nearly nonexistent.

Bureau of Labor Statistics data shows 147,600 construction and building inspectors employed in the United States as of 2024, with the profession projected to actually shrink by one percent over the next decade while all other occupations grow by three. BLS estimates 14,800 annual openings, driven almost entirely by retirements and workers departing for private-sector jobs, a number that represents not growth but backfill the system cannot achieve.

According to a workforce survey by the International Association of Electrical Inspectors, the average Authority Having Jurisdiction inspector is between fifty-five and sixty-four years old, carrying fifteen years of inspection experience layered on top of thirty-five years in the broader construction industry. When these professionals leave, they take with them decades of pattern recognition: the ability to spot where builders cut corners, which soil conditions demand deeper footings, how a framing crew's bad habit before lunch becomes a code violation by three o'clock, the sort of knowledge that cannot be transferred in a training manual or a two-week orientation.

What makes each departure catastrophic in smaller departments, which describes most departments in America, is the multi-certification problem. ICC/NIBS found that 71% of plan reviewers also hold building inspector certifications, 66% are certified residential inspectors, 58% carry mechanical certifications, 51% plumbing, and 50% energy. A single retirement in a four-person office doesn't create one vacancy. It creates somewhere between three and six, spread across disciplines that the remaining staff may not be certified to cover.

Why Nobody Applies

A government building inspector earns a median salary of $72,120, and in some jurisdictions the average drops to $48,041. Compare that with a private-sector construction manager at $106,980 at the median, rising to $147,760 as a senior PM and $203,093 as a director, and you begin to understand the 48% pay gap that keeps skilled professionals from applying. Roughly $35,000 a year evaporates for the privilege of reviewing blueprints under fluorescent lights and explaining to the fourteenth homeowner this week that their contractor drew the egress window two inches too small.

Stability once compensated for that gap: a pension, predictable hours, solid benefits. Post-2008 austerity gutted those advantages in jurisdiction after jurisdiction. Cities laid off junior inspectors during the recession and never rehired them. An apprenticeship model that once brought young professionals into departments, pairing them with experienced reviewers for years before they took cases solo, largely disappeared along with the budget lines that funded it. Former ICC CEO Dominic Sims described the situation in characteristically careful language when he noted that state and local governments are having "a more challenging time hiring building safety professionals," which translates from bureaucratic dialect roughly as: nobody wants the job.

New York City's Department of Buildings illustrates the endgame. A 16% vacancy rate across 1,884 budgeted positions, with 287 sitting empty. Fewer inspections conducted, fewer code violations issued, fewer certified permit applications audited, all in a year when eleven construction workers died on job sites and the mayor imposed an additional 4% budget cut on the department responsible for preventing exactly those deaths.

What Doesn't Get Built

PlanCheckSolver, an AI plan review vendor with obvious commercial interest in framing the problem, but one that grounds its analysis in BLS and ICC data, estimates that 19,000 homes were not built in 2024 specifically because permit processing delays made projects economically unviable. That figure represents $8.14 billion in housing that someone wanted to build and someone wanted to buy, contributing to a total annual economic impact from extended construction timelines of $10.81 billion. Seventy-eight percent of construction projects experienced delays in that period, and 62% of those delays traced directly to staffing shortages in building departments rather than to design problems, material costs, or financing difficulties.

NAHB puts the national housing shortage at 1.2 million units while the residential construction workforce itself lost more than 41,000 jobs in 2025, and yet the professionals tasked with approving whatever manages to get built are disappearing at a rate nobody has a credible plan to arrest. More than 70% of planning professionals surveyed by the ICC cited resource and staffing shortfalls as a barrier to meeting their own mandated permitting deadlines, which is another way of saying they know they are too slow and they do not have the bodies to go faster, and the bodies they still have are, by the numbers, overwhelmingly closer to retirement than to mid-career.

A Technology Waiting at the Door

On June 3, 2026, UpCodes, a platform already used by 800,000 architecture, engineering, and construction professionals for code research, launched Plan Review: an AI feature that automatically checks project drawings against a library of eleven million locally adopted building codes. It identifies code violations, coordination errors between mechanical, electrical, and plumbing plans, and specification mismatches, all in minutes rather than weeks, for $59 per user per month at the full AI tier or $39 for the base platform. That monthly cost is less than most building departments spend on a single ream of architectural-format paper.

PlanCheckSolver published a head-to-head comparison in which AI reviewed ten residential plan sets in approximately ten minutes each and flagged 814 code compliance issues while human reviewers spent eight to ten hours per plan set and caught 93, an 8.7-to-1 ratio on issue detection and a roughly 50-to-1 ratio on speed, with the company claiming a 97% catch rate versus approximately 11% for manual review.

I want to be precise about what those numbers are: vendor-published benchmarks with no independent audit. No third-party study has verified PlanCheckSolver's methodology or reproduced its results in a peer-reviewed setting. An AI system that flags 814 "issues" on ten residential plans may well be generating false positives that waste reviewer time rather than saving it, and the framing of 97% versus 11% invites skepticism about how "catch rate" was defined and measured. But even halving those claims, even assuming the real performance is four times as many issues caught in twenty minutes rather than ten, the implications for a department running a fourteen-week residential queue with three reviewers and no hiring budget remain significant enough to demand serious examination.

Other tools are arriving alongside UpCodes. CodeComply automates compliance checks against the International Building Code, NFPA fire codes, ADA requirements, FHA standards, and local amendments. AutoReview.AI, a University of Florida spinoff named the university's Invention of the Year, pairs AI plan review with an augmented reality inspection system where an inspector holds an iPad on a construction site and the software superimposes approved plans over the actual structure to highlight deviations in real time. Even the ICC itself has launched Navigator, an AI research tool grounded in its authoritative code library, though the organization positions it carefully as an aid rather than any kind of replacement.

Three Walls Between a Tool and a Desk

Municipal purchasing cycles for new software platforms run six to eighteen months under typical conditions. Competitive bidding requirements, IT security reviews, data residency assessments, council approval for recurring expenditures, and integration testing with legacy permitting systems that sometimes predate broadband internet all contribute to a procurement timeline that can outlast the tenure of the building official who initiated the request. By the time a department identifies, evaluates, pilots, and approves an AI plan review tool through standard channels, two more reviewers may have submitted their retirement paperwork.

Liability forms the second barrier, and it is arguably the most intractable. When a human reviewer misses a code violation and a structure fails, responsibility distributes along a well-established chain: the inspector, the department, the jurisdiction, the builder, the architect of record. When an AI tool misses the same violation, that legal framework dissolves into untested questions about whether the software vendor bears product liability, whether the jurisdiction assumed risk by choosing to rely on automated output, and whether the reviewer who accepted the AI's conclusion can invoke reasonable reliance or must independently verify every finding. No court has answered these questions. No statute addresses them. Building departments exist to manage risk, and adopting a technology whose liability model is genuinely undefined represents exactly the species of risk they are constitutionally inclined to avoid.

"AI is not a replacement for the knowledge and judgment of trained professionals," the ICC states in its guidance, adding that a human must always remain in the loop, "applying knowledge, verifying information, and ensuring decisions prioritize safety and compliance." A defensible position rooted in legitimate safety concerns. Also a framework that renders AI adoption functionally optional. If the human must verify everything the AI produces, and you do not have enough humans to perform the verification, then the constraint is still humans, and the AI sits unused on a server because nobody in the building can review what it generates.

Dr. George Guszcza, CEO of the National Institute of Building Sciences, confronted this impasse directly at the Elevate 2025 Workforce Summit in Washington, held at the National Building Museum with U.S. Secretary of Labor Lori Chavez-DeRemer in the audience: "Past and current strategies and interventions have failed to provide solutions at scale." Hiring programs. Training pipelines. Technical high school partnerships. Inspector-sharing compacts between jurisdictions. All meaningful. All producing dozens of new professionals against a deficit that runs in the thousands per year.

What You Can Actually Do

You cannot fix municipal procurement from the applicant side of the counter, but you can control what you submit. Running your plans through UpCodes or a comparable pre-submission compliance tool before filing costs between $39 and $59 a month, which is less than a single hour of architect revision time. Every code violation you catch before submission eliminates a correction cycle you would otherwise trigger, and in a department where each resubmittal adds weeks or months to your timeline, catching three issues before you file can compress the process by a third.

If you are an architect or engineer filing repeatedly in a jurisdiction with published backlogs exceeding six weeks, build that delay into your project schedule from day one. Do not quote your client a timeline based on the permit application date. Quote them one based on the permit issuance date, because the gap between those two events is growing, and neither hiring trends nor procurement timelines suggest it will reverse before the end of this decade.

If you are a homeowner adding a bedroom or building an ADU, here is what the data says about the person who will review your plans: statistically, they are fifty-eight years old, hold certifications in five disciplines, earn $72,000 a year in a profession the labor market has collectively decided is not worth competing for, and carry thirty-five years of construction knowledge that will walk out the door when they retire in three to five years. They are very good at their job, and there are not remotely enough of them to keep up with the queue. A tool that could screen your plans in ten minutes for $59 a month is commercially available today, but the building department across the counter almost certainly has never evaluated it and may not know it exists.

Limitations of This Analysis

PlanCheckSolver's performance claims are self-reported, and no independent, peer-reviewed audit of any commercial AI plan review tool's accuracy in a real municipal deployment exists as of this writing. Plan review timelines cited here represent six jurisdictions selected for publicly available data and are illustrative rather than nationally representative. FMI Corporation and PlanGrid's widely cited $177 billion rework figure originates from a 2018 survey covering all construction sectors, not residential exclusively. ICC and NIBS's 3,850 survey respondents represent approximately 2.6% of the 147,600 inspectors counted by BLS, though the geographic coverage spans all fifty states. Most critically, no building department in the United States has publicly reported measurable results from deploying AI plan review at scale, which means the technology's effectiveness outside vendor demonstrations and controlled comparisons remains genuinely untested in the bureaucratic, liability-laden, and politically constrained reality where building plans actually get approved or rejected.
