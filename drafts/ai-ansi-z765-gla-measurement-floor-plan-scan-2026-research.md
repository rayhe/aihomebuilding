# Research: ANSI Z765 GLA — The Ruler That Erased Your Basement, and the AI Tape Measure

**Slug:** ai-ansi-z765-gla-measurement-floor-plan-scan-2026
**Journalist:** Elena Vasquez (Architecture & Design)
**Date:** October 5, 2026
**Kill test:** Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes. The number on the listing — "2,400 sq ft" — and the number on the appraisal can now differ by hundreds of square feet, by federal rule, and the buyer absorbs the gap. A buyer who understands ANSI Z765's below-grade exclusion, the 7-foot ceiling rule, and the AI phone-scan workflow can verify GLA during the inspection period instead of discovering the shortfall at the appraisal. A seller finishing a basement needs to know that space will never count as GLA no matter how beautiful it is.

## Thesis

In April 2022, Fannie Mae did something it had never done: it told appraisers exactly how to measure a house. ANSI Z765-2021 became mandatory for covered appraisals, and the standard is ruthless about what counts. Measure the exterior. Finished, above-grade space only. Seven-foot ceilings minimum. A level is below-grade if any part of it is below grade — so the walkout basement with the home theater and the wet bar contributes zero to GLA. Fannie Mae has said plainly that one reason it adopted the standard now is "the recent emergence of new technologies, such as phone apps, which can measure houses, generate floor plans, and calculate GLA." The AI tape measure arrived the same season as the federal ruler. Tools like CubiCasa now scan a 2,000 sq ft home in 5 minutes and claim 95-97% accuracy — while CubiCasa's own published comparison found its average GLA variance (4.0%) essentially identical to two human appraisers with tape measures (3.9%). The uncomfortable finding: machine measurement is not yet more accurate than human measurement. It is merely faster, cheaper, and — this is the part that matters — ANSI-aligned by default.

## Original contribution (the variance-to-dollars math)

Nobody has translated CubiCasa's published 4.0% average GLA variance into dollars at risk, or the below-grade exclusion into a listing-vs-appraisal gap:

- **Measurement noise, priced:** 4.0% average variance on a 2,000 sq ft home = ±80 sq ft. At an assumed $350/sq ft (labeled assumption; coastal markets run far higher), that is ±$28,000 of valuation noise attributable to measurement method alone — before condition, location, or market adjustments enter.
- **The basement haircut:** a 2,400 sq ft listing with 800 sq ft of finished walkout basement = 1,600 sq ft ANSI GLA. At $350/sq ft, $280,000 of finished space vanishes from the headline number. It is not valueless — the appraiser adjusts it on the basement line — but the per-square-foot comp math every buyer does in their head just broke.
- **The liability dodge, quantified:** CubiCasa has produced 4M+ floor plans and will not print a total square footage on them. The 4.0% variance figure comes from the vendor's own 43-property homeowner sample — not peer-reviewed, not independent.

Methodology note: $350/sq ft is an illustrative assumption, not a market claim. The 4.0%/3.9% figures are CubiCasa's self-published comparison. The basement example assumes the entire lower level is below grade per the any-portion rule.

## AI angle

- CubiCasa: phone-camera machine vision + AI point cloud to 2D plan, ANSI-Z765 Digital GLA product, UAD 3.6 GLA floor plan version; ~5-minute capture, <24-hour delivery, QA engineer review before delivery.
- Matterport: LiDAR-based 3D twins with floor-plan byproduct; $150-400/shoot; more consistent on complex layouts.
- magicplan: mobile + laser, instant capture, ANSI explainer content aimed at appraisers.
- MeasureFloorPlan: upload any floor plan image, trace exterior perimeter, get ANSI GLA in ~2 minutes ($9 per plan).
- Skepticism: CubiCasa's own study shows parity with tape measures, not superiority (3.9% vs 4.0% average variance); accuracy degrades with clutter, angles, poor light (vendor's own FAQ); no independent audit of any vendor's accuracy claims; the liability dodge (no total sq ft printed) tells you what the vendor believes about its own numbers; Fannie Mae requires appraiser independence — an AI scan does not replace the appraiser's exterior measurement for covered assignments.

## Strongest counterargument

ANSI Z765 does not set value. It sets a reporting convention. The finished basement is not worthless — it is reported and adjusted on a separate grid line, and in markets where daylight basements are the norm, appraisers and buyers already price them. The pre-2022 chaos (every appraiser measuring differently, MLS and tax records disagreeing with everyone) was arguably worse than a strict standard with known blind spots. And the AI tools genuinely solved the hand-drawn-sketch problem Fannie Mae cared about: computer-generated, dimensioned, repeatable plans. The ruler is rigid; the market was never going to be.

## Limitations

- CubiCasa accuracy and variance figures are vendor-published (n=43 homeowner scans); no independent or peer-reviewed verification exists.
- $350/sq ft is an illustrative assumption; $/sq ft varies enormously by market and the basement adjustment rate is not 1:1 with above-grade GLA.
- Cost figures ($15-50 CubiCasa, $150-400 Matterport, $9 MeasureFloorPlan) are vendor/marketplace published and change.
- Fannie Mae's ANSI requirement applies to covered appraisal assignments (interior/exterior inspections for loans sold to Fannie Mae); desktop appraisals are encouraged-not-required, and state law can supersede.
- No public dataset quantifies how often GLA discrepancies actually kill or reprice deals post-April 2022.
