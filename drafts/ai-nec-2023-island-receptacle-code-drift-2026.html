Your Kitchen Island's Outlet Is Optional Now. Your Inspector's Memory Might Not Be.

By Catherine Chen · October 4, 2026

Picture an electrician in Madison, Wisconsin, cutting a rectangle into the side of a brand-new kitchen island cabinet. He mounts a GFCI receptacle eight inches below the countertop overhang, wires it cleanly, bills $385, and nothing about the workmanship is wrong: wire right, box right, device right. But Wisconsin adopted the 2023 National Electrical Code this fall, stepping off the 2017 edition, and under the code now in force, the thing he just installed with twenty years of muscle memory is a violation, which means he built, at full price, the exact outlet the code had just outlawed.

For decades, Section 210.52(C) of the NEC required at least one receptacle for every kitchen island and peninsula, with more for bigger surfaces under the old 9-18-18 square-foot formula, and electricians wired them into cabinet faces as a matter of course. In the 2023 edition, that requirement vanished outright: outlets on islands are now optional. Install one anyway and it cannot go below the countertop, while skipping it entirely means leaving provisions, a raceway or a cable run to an accessible box, so a future owner can add one without opening finished cabinetry, which sounds simple until the cabinet shop, the countertop fabricator, and the electrician each discover they have a different idea of what counts as a provision. Three sentences of code text, and half the kitchens permitted in America changed.

CMP-2, the code panel responsible, did not do this on a whim. During the 2023 revision cycle it was shown data from the Consumer Product Safety Commission: 45 anecdotal reports of burns and other injuries between January 1991 and 2020, an estimated 9,700 burns treated in hospital emergency departments, many of them second- and third-degree, and 10 deaths. Nearly always, the mechanism was the same, a small child pulling the power cord or a passerby snagging it, sending a slow cooker full of boiling liquid off the counter. A below-counter outlet puts the cord exactly where small hands live, and the panel decided the outlet had to go, which is how a requirement that had stood for decades, wired into millions of kitchens without a second thought, vanished in a single code cycle because of a cord.

Note the scale honestly, because the panel's critics certainly did: roughly 9,700 injuries over thirty years is about 320 a year, in a country with tens of millions of kitchens. Rare. Horrific won anyway. The panel weighed rare against horrific and chose horrific. Reasonable people disagreed, loudly, and the disagreement is the interesting part of this story, which we will get to.

Six states have already adopted the 2026 NEC, which tidied the new language further: "if installed" became "if provided." Receptacles below a countertop are now prohibited within 24 inches of it, a bright line where the old rule had a debatable one, and a receptacle inside a drawer is permitted but does not count as the serving outlet. Every revision points the same direction, power goes on the counter or in it, never under it, because somewhere along the way the code developed opinions about cords, and those opinions now carry the force of law in nineteen states and counting.

Here is where the calendar turns the rule into a trap. Every three years the NEC revises itself and each state adopts on its own schedule, sometimes years behind, sometimes with local amendments layered on top. As of August 2026, nineteen states had adopted the 2023 edition in some form and six had moved to 2026, while Connecticut, Florida, Indiana, Maryland, Montana, South Carolina, Vermont, and Virginia were still working through 2023 adoption. So a kitchen plan drawn perfectly to the new rule, no island outlet, provisions roughed in, fails inspection in a state still enforcing 2020, while a plan drawn to the old rule, with the classic below-counter cabinet-face outlet, fails inspection in a state on 2023. Same PDF. Two verdicts. Nothing changed except which side of a state line the house sits on.

This is also where the artificial intelligence enters, and where it earns its keep as a cautionary tale. In June, UpCodes launched an AI-native Plan Review that reads drawings against a library of 11 million adopted code sections across more than 6,000 jurisdictions. Someone sets the jurisdiction, the code year, and the building type once per project, and every finding flows from those settings, which makes that single dropdown the most dangerous field in the workflow for this rule, because the rule's polarity flips between editions. Leave the year on 2020 for a 2023 project and the machine flags your compliant omission as a missing required outlet. Leave it on 2023 for a 2020 project and it blesses the below-counter outlet your inspector is about to red-tag. About the code itself, the software is not wrong. It is wrong about which code, which is worse, because it delivers the wrong answer with the full confidence of the right one. Confidence without context.

Start with the money, which is straightforward: a conventional island outlet, new homerun from the panel, GFCI device, cabinet cut, coordination with the cabinet shop, runs $200 to $500 installed. Under the new rule the required work shrinks to provisions at rough-in, a raceway or cable to an accessible box inside the island, which is the cheap part of the job. What costs real money is the listed pop-up countertop assembly, $351 to $377 for the hardware alone, which becomes a purchase you make later if you ever want it rather than a permit-line item you must install now. A builder doing forty kitchens a year in a 2023 state who keeps wiring islands the old way is spending $8,000 to $20,000 a year on work the code no longer asks for, before a single inspection argument, because old habits invoice monthly.

Now the disagreement, stated at full strength, because their case deserves a fair hearing. Filed in response to the change, the National Association of Home Builders submitted suggested amendments asking to reinstate the 2017 requirement: at least one outlet per island or peninsula, with an accessibility exception for below-counter placement. Buyers expect island power, and a provisions-only island is a small daily annoyance the owner fixes later at retrofit prices, which are always worse. And the CPSC record is 45 anecdotal reports across three decades, a thin reed for deleting a convenience feature used every day in millions of kitchens. On this telling, the code punished every kitchen for injuries that mostly involved unsupervised small children near hot appliances, and the panel got the trade backwards. Maybe they did. Note what the amendment reveals: the people who build the houses wanted the old rule back, while across the table, the people who write the code said no. When builders and the code disagree about your kitchen, you live with both of them.

A few honest limitations before the takeaways. Adoption counts move monthly, and your local inspector can amend, delay, or simply ignore what the state adopted, so confirm with the authority having jurisdiction before acting on any of this. My savings math on provisions versus a finished outlet is estimated from the character of the work, not from a bid database, and your electrician's number is the one that counts. Nobody has published a measured false-positive rate for AI plan reviewers on this specific rule; the failure mode I described is reasoned from how these products are built, a single project-level code-year setting driving every finding, not from observed errors. And the CPSC figures come through the code panel record as reported by the trade press, not from a primary CPSC release I reviewed directly. The direction of every claim is right; the decimals belong to the industry, not to me.

If you are building or remodeling, here is what to do. In a 2023-NEC jurisdiction, you may skip the island outlet, but make the electrician rough in provisions, a raceway or cable to an accessible box, before the cabinets go in, because adding power later without it means opening finished cabinetry, which is how a $300 decision becomes a $900 one. In a 2020-or-earlier jurisdiction, the outlet is still required, and if your plans show it on the cabinet face below the countertop, get a redesign before the inspector gets involved. If anyone runs your plans through an AI code checker, verify the code-year setting against your inspector's adopted edition, since on this rule a wrong setting manufactures violations out of thin air. And if you want the outlet regardless, listed pop-up assemblies run $351 to $377 for the hardware, and the countertop cutout has to be planned before fabrication, not discovered after.

Feelings, muscle memory, the way you have wired islands since the nineties: the code cares about none of it. It cares which edition your state adopted and whether the person checking your work, human or machine, is reading the same one. Ask which edition before the drywall goes up. It is the cheapest question on the entire job. Ask it.
