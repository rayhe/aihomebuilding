Fourteen Rules Govern the One Pipe on Your Water Heater Nobody Looks At.

By Catherine Chen · September 22, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

On January 9, 1982, a water heater exploded at Starr Elementary School in Spencer, Oklahoma, killing six children and a teacher. Investigators described the force as equivalent to two pounds of dynamite, and traced the blast to a malfunctioning temperature and pressure relief valve, the small brass device on the side of the tank that is the only thing standing between 40 gallons of superheated water and a rupture. When it failed, nobody in the room had a chance. (GLH Home Inspections)

You would think an industry would change its habits after that; it changed its paperwork instead.

What the code says: fourteen rules for one pipe

Section P2804.6.1 of the International Residential Code is the law's answer to decades of relief-valve failures: fourteen distinct requirements governing nothing but the discharge pipe, the short run of pipe that carries the valve's emergency output somewhere safe, not the valve itself, not the tank, just the pipe, all fourteen rules of it.

Condensed to what a plumber can carry in their head: the pipe cannot connect directly to the drainage system; it must discharge through an air gap in the same room; it cannot be smaller than the valve outlet and must stay full size to the air gap; it serves one relief device and one only; it discharges to the floor, the heater pan, a waste receptor, or outdoors; the termination must be visible to the occupants; it cannot be trapped; it must drain by gravity; it must end between two pipe-diameters and six inches above the floor; the end cannot be threaded; no valves or tees in the run; the material must be rated for the job; and if insert fittings are used, the pipe gets one nominal size larger than the valve outlet. (IRC 2021)

In one respect the Uniform Plumbing Code is stricter than the IRC: relief drains must run to the outside of the building, terminating between six inches and two feet above grade, pointing downward. (UPC 608.5) The federal government, through HUD's NSPIRE inspection standard, calls a bad TPR discharge pipe a Severe health and safety deficiency, the same category as exposed wiring and gas leaks, with a 24-hour correction clock. (HUD NSPIRE)

Home inspectors will tell you, privately, that improperly installed TPR valves are among the most common defects they flag, and also the defects most likely to be shrugged off as maintenance items. Your feelings are not the code's concern. It cares that the termination point is observable, the pipe drains by gravity, and nobody threaded a cap onto the end of it.

This matters because AI inspection software is arriving at exactly this granularity. Spectora, the dominant home-inspection report platform, shipped AI Report Assist in June: inspectors speak observations and snap photos, and the model matches what it sees to pre-approved report comments, cutting roughly 25 percent of the time per inspection. (Business Wire) Those models are being trained on millions of tagged inspection photos, and TPR discharge geometry is some of the most consistently labeled data in the corpus because it is one of the most commonly cited defects. A vision model that reliably flags a threaded terminus or an uphill run is not science fiction; it is the next feature release.

What the camera cannot see

The audit has hard limits, and the honest version of this story names them. A photograph cannot distinguish PVC from CPVC, and HUD's NSPIRE standard explicitly disallows PVC in discharge piping, so the material check stays human. A photograph cannot tell whether the valve itself is stuck closed, the failure mode that actually kills people, and no photograph can pressure-test anything. Geometry yields to a photograph; the mechanism does not.

Why the skeptics might be right

Now the case against everything above, because it may be the better read of the situation. A tank rupture requires three failures at once: the thermostat stuck on, the high-limit cutoff failed, and the relief valve stuck closed or capped. Modern tanks ship with redundant safeties, and properly maintained ones essentially never hit all three. Inspectors who file TPR discharge under maintenance instead of emergency are not ignorant of the code. They are making a base-rate judgment: the fourteen requirements are belt and suspenders for an event that, in a maintained tank, may never arrive in the life of the building. A plumber who has set a thousand heaters and never seen one lift is not wrong about the frequency. He is wrong only if he treats a rare tail as zero.

Even granting the skepticism, the arithmetic still lands. Up to 30 percent of water heater failures trace to improper installation, and insurers routinely deny water-damage claims when the install violated code. (DRF TPS) the fourteen rules are cheap to satisfy, while the tail event is not.

What the fix costs versus the failure

Break-even on one failed final: correcting a discharge pipe at rough-in costs a plumber roughly $150 to $300 in labor plus $20 to $40 in CPVC or copper. Fail the final inspection over it and the AHJ charges a re-inspection fee, typically $100 to $200, plus three to seven days waiting for the next inspection window. A two-person crew idled at roughly $800 a day fully loaded turns one afternoon of rescheduling into $400 in dead wages. Total cost of one failed final: around $550. That $150 fix pays for itself 3.6 times over against a single re-inspection, before counting the schedule slip that pushes every downstream trade. For homebuyers: the same $150 to $300 repair negotiated at seller expense before close is free money, because after close the deficiency and the insurer's right to deny a related claim are both yours.

What the camera cannot adjudicate

The audit matrix checks geometry against law, but compliance is not the whole story. PVC versus CPVC is a material question that photos fumble, stuck-closed valves are invisible to every lens, and no model has been validated specifically on TPR discharge detection. Spectora's 25 percent time saving covers the whole report workflow, not this defect class, and no vendor has published precision or recall numbers for relief-valve geometry. A camera is a triage tool, not a licensed inspector, and in most jurisdictions the law agrees: software is not a competent person, and a photo is not an inspection.

What to actually do

If you are a GC: photograph every water heater at rough-in with the discharge pipe, termination, and a tape measure in frame, and run the fourteen-point checklist before the inspector does, because a $150 correction now beats a $550 re-inspection later. If you are buying a home: when the inspection report flags the TPR discharge pipe, treat it as a seller repair, not a maintenance note; the fix is cheap, the code citation is ironclad, and the insurer is on the code's side. If you manage rental property: HUD's NSPIRE standard gives you 24 hours on a Severe TPR deficiency, and the federal inspector's checklist is exactly the photo audit above, so pre-check every unit before the inspection cycle finds them for you. And if you are the homeowner staring at a dripping valve: never cap it. That drip is the valve doing its job. A cap turns a $15 replacement into the experiment MythBusters ran at 335 psi, and the tank won. (InspectionNews)

Fourteen rules for one pipe is the code at its most obsessive, and the photo audit is proof that obsessive can also be cheap to verify.
