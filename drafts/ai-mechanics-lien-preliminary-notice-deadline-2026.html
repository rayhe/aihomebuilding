You Have 20 Days to Protect Your Right to Get Paid. 56% of Subcontractors Missed It.

By Catherine Chen · September 11, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Twenty days. In California, that is the entire grace period a subcontractor gets to announce, in writing, to the property owner, the general contractor, and the construction lender, that they exist and expect to be paid. Miss the window and the state's most powerful payment protection, the mechanics lien, starts leaking. Miss it by enough and it is gone entirely. Statutes do not care that your crew was busy hanging the actual building.

Elaine runs the office for a California specialty contractor, and for years her preliminary notices went out the way most of them do in this industry: remembered on a Friday, assembled from a manila folder, mailed on hope. Then the company plugged its accounting software into a lien management platform. Job details flowed in automatically. Her notices populated themselves. When the legal property description was missing, which it usually was, the platform's research team dug it out of county records. What used to eat an afternoon now takes a few clicks, and when a deadline gets close, Elaine hears about it within 24 hours instead of discovering it after the fact. (Levelset)

She is the exception. A 2026 survey of trade contractors by Siteline found that 56 percent of subcontractors missed a critical mechanic's lien deadline in the past two years, putting one of their strongest payment protections at risk. Ninety-two percent floated payroll last year. Two-thirds spend eleven or more hours a month preparing, submitting, and tracking pay applications, roughly a part-time job devoted to the paperwork of getting paid for work already done. (Contractor Magazine)

The Most Expensive Paperwork in Residential Construction

Slow payment is the industry's chronic condition, and the numbers keep getting worse. PYMNTS Intelligence put the cost of slow payments at $273 billion in 2023, nearly 14 percent of total construction costs, up from 12 percent the year before. Seventy-two percent of subcontractors reported waiting more than 30 days to be paid, up from 49 percent in 2022. (PYMNTS) An older ENR survey of 640 subcontractors found more than 30 percent of payments arriving late, with an average delay of 36.4 days. Every state has a prompt-pay statute. None of them has fixed it.

Against that background, the mechanics lien is the sub's leverage: a claim against the property itself, which clouds title and gets the owner's attention in a way invoices never do. But the lien is a creature of statute, and statutes are fussy. Before you can file one, most states require a preliminary notice, sometimes called a pre-lien or notice of furnishing, served within a set number of days after you first set foot on the job. Details vary wildly, and that variance is the trap. (Levelset)

Read that table as a residential sub working across a metro area that straddles a state line, or a supplier shipping to jobs in three states. One spreadsheet, one memory, one office manager cannot hold six different regimes, six different recipient lists, and six different service methods. In California you must notify the lender, which means first finding the lender, a task Levelset's own case studies describe as one of the hardest parts of the job. (Levelset) In Montana you must file with the county, not just mail. In North Carolina the clock is 15 days and it does not bend.

The 20-Day Trap, With Math

California's rule is the forgiving one, which makes it the perfect illustration of how the trap works. A late preliminary notice is still effective, but only for labor and materials furnished within 20 days before the notice was served, plus everything after. Work performed earlier falls outside the lien's protection.

Take a residential HVAC sub burning through $8,000 a week in labor and materials on a custom home, a plausible burn rate for a two-person crew with equipment costs. Picture the office sending the preliminary notice on day 30 instead of day 20, ten days late, an ordinary slip during a busy month. Do the arithmetic: $8,000 divided by 7 is about $1,143 per day, and 10 unprotected days means roughly $11,400 of performed work now sits outside the lien. If the GC's payment goes sideways, that $11,400 is recoverable only through a lawsuit, slower, pricier, and uncertain, instead of a lien that clouds the property's title. Ten days of inattention. Five figures of exposure.

That calculation assumes a steady burn rate and a single trade package; real jobs are lumpier, and some trades burn far more. That point survives the lumpiness. Every day past the deadline converts performed work from secured to unsecured, silently, while the crew keeps working.

What the Software Actually Does

The current generation of lien management tools, Levelset (now part of Procore), Handle, and embedded options like Bectran's lien tracking integrations, attack the problem in three places. First, state law is encoded as rules: enter a job, and every notice and lien deadline populates automatically with reminder emails as dates approach. Second, document generation is templated to each state's forms, with mailing handled to the statutory standard, first class, certified, or return receipt as required. Third, and most consequentially, the platforms research the job itself. (Levelset; Bectran)

That third piece is where the "AI" label gets complicated, and honesty requires saying so. Levelset's Scout is described by the company as a research operation that combines proprietary databases, county assessor records, and CoreLogic data with human investigators who make phone calls and send emails. Its own marketing says plainly that Scout is a team of people, not just a software program. Automation is real: job data syncs from accounting software, deadlines compute themselves, documents assemble in clicks, but the hardest lookups still run on human judgment pointed at good data. One insulation contractor ran 159 documents through the platform in a single month; Scout verified or corrected the project information on every one, securing more than $500,000 in receivables. (Levelset)

There is also a quiet second-order benefit buried in the statutes. In California and Arizona, an owner who records a notice of completion can shorten everyone's lien-filing deadline, but only after serving a copy on every party that sent a preliminary notice. File the prelim and the owner cannot shrink your clock without telling you. Skip it and the deadline can move while you are not looking. (Levelset)

The Case Against, at Full Strength

Start with the bluntest objection. Software cannot file a notice for a job it does not know about. If the field crew starts work Monday and the office enters the project on day 25, every deadline engine on earth will cheerfully compute a deadline that has already passed. Garbage in, garbage out, except here the garbage is a forfeited property right. That 56 percent miss rate measures an industry where jobs start before paperwork does, and no algorithm reaches back in time.

Then the legal fragility. A preliminary notice with the wrong legal property description, the wrong owner name, or service by the wrong method can be void, and courts are inconsistent about forgiveness. Illinois judges have excused minor service defects where the owner actually received the notice, but drew a hard line at fax transmission, which the statute does not authorize. (Levelset) A templated document is only as good as the data in its fields, and no platform's terms make it your construction attorney. When $40,000 is on the line, "the software said it was fine" is not a legal defense.

Deeper still, the software treats a symptom of a power imbalance, not the imbalance. Prompt-pay statutes exist in all 50 states and late payment got worse anyway. Subcontractors front labor and materials, pay weekly payroll, buy on 30-day terms, and wait 60 to 90 days to collect, a dynamic one CEO accurately called being the industry's involuntary bank. A deadline tracker does not change who holds the money or for how long. It changes whether the sub keeps a weapon for the eventual fight.

Finally, the economics are thinner for the smallest shops. Lien platforms price by subscription or per document, and public pricing is scarce, which itself tells you something. A two-person sub running ten jobs a year faces a different ROI calculation than a supplier running hundreds. And the headline statistic deserves its asterisk: the 56 percent figure comes from a vendor survey of trade contractors, directionally alarming rather than a randomized study. Treat it as a smoke alarm, not a census.

What This Analysis Could Not Prove

Limits, stated plainly. I could not verify current per-document or subscription pricing for lien management platforms from public sources; vendors keep pricing behind sales calls, so the break-even math below uses staff time, not software cost. The 20-day trap calculation assumes a steady $8,000-per-week burn rate that will not match every trade or market. The 56 percent missed-deadline figure comes from Siteline's 2026 survey, a vendor study of its audience, not independent research. State law summaries here are simplified sketches of complex statutes; lien law varies by project type, tier, and public versus private work, and nothing here substitutes for a construction attorney in your state. The $273 billion slow-payment cost is PYMNTS Intelligence's 2023 estimate, and methodologies for such figures are never fully transparent.

What to Do Before the Next Job Starts

Four moves, depending on where you sit.

If you run a residential subcontracting shop, run the staff-time math first. Two-thirds of your peers spend eleven or more hours a month on pay applications; at a $45-per-hour loaded office cost, that is roughly $6,000 a year of staff time spent on the paperwork of getting paid. A lien management platform has to beat that number plus the probability-weighted value of the lien rights themselves, and for most multi-job shops it clears the bar easily. But the software only works if job entry happens on day one, not day twenty-five. Make first-day entry a rule with someone's name on it, or you have purchased an expensive calendar.

If you are a general contractor, understand that your subs' preliminary notices are also your early warning system. A sub that files properly is a sub you can track, communicate with, and pay before things harden into liens. Platform GC payment-behavior ratings cut both ways; your subs are rating you too.

If you are a homeowner or owner-builder, the preliminary notice is not your enemy. It is the document that tells you a subcontractor exists before a lien appears on your title. When your GC pays a sub, collect the signed lien waiver with the check, every time, for every tier you know about. A signed waiver is the receipt that matches the notice, and the pair of them is what keeps your property's title clean.

And if you are already past a deadline, do not assume the game is over. California's soft rule means a late notice still protects recent and future work. Some states allow alternative recovery paths, breach of contract, unjust enrichment, prompt-pay penalties, that survive a lost lien. Call a construction attorney in your state before you write off the receivable. Statutes do not care that you were busy, but they sometimes leave a side door open.
