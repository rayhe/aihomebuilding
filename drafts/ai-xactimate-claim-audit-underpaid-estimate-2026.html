Your Adjuster's Software Says $18,000. Your Contractor Says $31,000. The Software Is Negotiable.

By Frank DeLuca · September 23, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

I have watched this scene play out a dozen times, and it never gets less painful: a homeowner spreads two estimates across the kitchen table, one from the insurance company that collects the premiums and one from the contractor who will actually swing the hammer, buy the drywall, and pay the crew, both describing the same burst pipe, the same four feet of ruined drywall, the same buckled oak floor, thirteen thousand dollars apart. All you get is a shrug. The software doesn't lie.

That software is called Xactimate, and your carrier almost certainly uses it. More than 80 percent of North American insurers write estimates in it, which means faster approvals and payment cycles for everyone in the system (2026 restoration industry guide). He's right about one thing: it doesn't lie. It answers the exact question it was asked, using price lists chosen by the people who hired the adjuster, configured with settings you will never see. That is not lying so much as bidding, which is something more useful to understand.

What the software actually is

Xactimate is a construction estimating program owned by Verisk. It turns a sketch of damaged rooms into line items with quantities, labor, and material prices, the way any estimator would, only faster and with better formatting. Where it gets interesting is what the program assumes about your house. United Policyholders, the nonprofit that has guided homeowners through claims for decades, puts it plainly: the software is generic and best suited to tract homes, and for custom-built, historic, or high-value-area homes its numbers "will likely not be sufficient to reimburse you for the actual cost of repairing and replacing your specific home" (Xactimate Demystified). Improper use of it, they note, has "factored into many claim disputes and lawsuits, particularly in the aftermath of Gulf Coast hurricanes."

Most general contractors do not use Xactimate at all. They price from subcontractor bids and job knowledge, the way the trade has priced work since before computers. So when the adjuster hands you a forty-page Xactimate scope and your contractor hands you a two-page bid, you are not comparing two estimates. You are comparing two languages, and yours is the one without a translator. United Policyholders' advice is blunt: get an independent estimate from a licensed construction professional who is trained in Xactimate, even if they never use it themselves, because that is the only way to argue line item against line item on the carrier's own turf.

Then there is the price list problem, and this one is structural. Buried in Verisk's own end-user license agreement, Section 12.3, sits an admission most homeowners never hear: Xactimate pricing data is already 30 days old when the adjuster opens it and is deemed "historical," with no warranty of accuracy (ICCOA hearing materials, April 2026). Thirty days is a rounding error in a calm market; after a hurricane it is a haircut. Adjusters on catastrophe forums talk openly about uplifting Xactimate pricing 25 percent for a Category 1 storm and doubling it for a Category 4 or 5, because labor floods in from out of state while the price list naps (LJB Legal damage-estimate guide). On top of that, insurers run their own customized price lists that sit below the public lists contractors see. Your estimate was not just generated by software. It was generated by your carrier's version of the software.

After a major storm, adjusters routinely uplift prices 25 to 100 percent above the list. Your carrier's customized list may start lower still.

The $10,000 line that vanishes

Now the line item that decides more claims than any other, and the one adjusters omit most hopefully: overhead and profit, the 10 percent overhead plus 10 percent profit that is standard markup whenever a general contractor runs a repair, which works out to $10,000 of real money on a typical $50,000 water loss. Carrier estimates frequently show zero next to it. Zero.

In most states, the law is not on the carrier's side. Start with the Gilderman decision against State Farm, which holds that where repairs will likely require more than one trade, it is "reasonably likely" a general contractor is needed, so the insurer must include overhead and profit in the payment even if you do the work yourself, hire a handyman, or never repair at all. A Pennsylvania appeals court reached the same conclusion in the Mee decision against Safeco, reversing a trial court that had let the carrier off the hook (Property Insurance Coverage Law Blog). In the trade this is called the three-trade rule. Drywall, paint, and flooring on your scope means three trades. Three trades means a GC is reasonably likely. Reasonably likely means the 20 percent is owed.

Regulators have said it even more plainly: in 1998 the Texas Department of Insurance declared that deducting prospective contractors' overhead and profit when calculating actual cash value "is improper, is not a reasonable interpretation of the policy language, and is unfair to insureds." Colorado's Division of Insurance wrote the same thing to State Farm the same month (Adjusters International, Adjusting Today). Even the industry's own interpretive body, the Property Loss Research Bureau, agrees that contractor overhead and profit are part of replacement cost.

The line keeps disappearing because carriers have quietly moved the goalposts from counting trades to judging "complexity," a word with no definition and no appeal process. A claims manager for American Reliable testified in 2022 that inside the company, "the rule" was still three trades or more, but the public-facing standard has dissolved into vibes (C&R Magazine). Complexity is whatever the adjuster says it is, so count the trades yourself: that rebuttal fits in one sentence, and it is the strongest sentence in this article.

$10,000 The overhead and profit (10% + 10%) owed on a typical $50,000 repair when three or more trades are involved, under the rule applied in Gilderman v. State Farm and Mee v. Safeco. It is the single most commonly omitted line on adjuster estimates, and the easiest to challenge.

The five things the sketch always forgets

Beyond overhead and profit, rushed scopes bleed money in the small lines, the unglamorous protection and prep work that never photographs well but bills honestly and keeps the rest of your house livable while the work happens. Restoration estimators keep a litany of the items adjusters most commonly leave out, and every one of them is real work with a real cost: dust-control barriers with poles and zipper doors sealing the work area. HEPA-filtered air scrubbers running while drywall dust fills the house. Carpet masking or ram board protecting the floors the crew walks across for two weeks. Resetting or replacing every outlet cover the painters remove. And drywall texture, where the adjuster prices a patch but a patch never matches, so the honest line item is heavy hand texture blended across the entire wall surface (C&R Magazine estimating guide).

Individually these are hundreds of dollars, but together, trade guides put the routinely missed items at $400 to $1,200 per job before anyone argues about the big lines. They are also diagnostic: an estimate with rooms sketched as disconnected boxes, no labeled photos tying pictures to line items, and none of the protection lines above was written fast, not written well, and speed is the tell. A careful scope photographs everything and labels it. A fast one hopes you will not notice what is missing.

The roof argument: matching

Hail chews up one slope of your roof while three slopes stay fine, and their offer is to replace only the damaged slope. You point out that twenty-year-old shingles have faded in the sun and the new slope will look like a patch on a good suit.

Courts have wrestled with this for years, and the law is genuinely split in a way homeowners should understand before they argue. In the Greene decision against USAA, a court called full-roof replacement for one damaged slope an "absurdity," holding that "the replacement cost of that part of the building damaged" required at most the damaged slope, and blessing the "line of sight" test: what must match is what you see in a single view. But the same body of law holds the door open where it matters: where damaged materials cannot be replaced with equivalents similar in function, color, and shape, the matching principle supports going further (United Policyholders matching memo). Discontinued shingle lines, faded colors, changed manufacturing: these are factual arguments, and they are won with photographs of the undamaged slopes, not with outrage. Take the photos before the adjuster arrives, because after the tear-off there is nothing left to compare.

The sixty-second audit

This is where the AI part of the story arrives, and it comes from an unexpected direction: the contractors. A new generation of tools ingests an adjuster's Xactimate export and audits it against building codes, manufacturer specs, and the carrier's own playbook, flagging missing line items and drafting the supplement letter. One such tool, RoofGenius, advertises that its AI scans an estimate in 60 seconds, finds missed line items, and generates supplement letters it says recover $3,000 to $15,000 per claim, with an average recovery of $4,820. On the auditor side, Nomad Data's Doc Chat reconciles Xactimate scope lines against vendor invoices and photo evidence, automating what fraud investigators and claims auditors used to grind through by hand (Nomad Data).

Read those numbers the way a foreman reads a bid: one eye on the math, one eye on the motive. That $4,820 figure is vendor marketing, unaudited by any third party, and a tool that finds missing money on every estimate is a revenue machine for roofing contractors, not a neutral arbiter. I would not cite it in a demand letter. But the underlying capability is real, and it changes the homeowner's leverage in a way worth understanding. What used to require hiring a public adjuster at ten percent of the claim can now begin as a sixty-second scan. What standardized the carrier's low bid is now standardizing the rebuttal, and that symmetry is the most interesting thing to happen to property claims in twenty years.

The strongest case for the carrier

Fairness requires stating the carrier's case at full strength, because it is not nothing. Before Xactimate, every claim was a bespoke negotiation, and bespoke negotiations after catastrophes are where price-gouging lives. Standardized pricing across thousands of adjusters keeps premiums lower for everyone, including you. Software didn't invent the incentive to trim scopes; humans did that with pencils for a century. And the courts have largely agreed with the carriers on the principle. In 2025 a federal judge dismissed a Pennsylvania class action alleging State Farm undervalued claims through Xactimate's price settings, ruling the carrier has no contractual duty to use any particular computation method (Claims Journal). In Belotti, the plaintiffs sat roughly $200,000 apart from State Farm on replacement cost, and the court still would not dictate the method.

That ruling is the most useful thing in this article, and not for the reason the carriers think. How they compute it is the carrier's prerogative; what they pay is negotiable, which means the fight was never about the software at all but about the number it was told to produce. Stop fighting the software and start auditing the estimate. Everything in the preceding two thousand words is just elaboration on that distinction.

What we did not prove

Honest accounting, as always. Overhead-and-profit entitlement and matching rules are state-specific; this article leans on Pennsylvania case law and the Texas and Colorado insurance departments, not a fifty-state survey, so check your own jurisdiction before citing Gilderman at your adjuster. A $4,820 average recovery is a vendor's marketing number with no independent verification, treated here as a claim rather than a fact. Characterizations of Xactimate price lists as below-market come from policyholder-side sources, not from Verisk, which does not publish its pricing methodology. No independent test of the AI audit tools was performed for this article; their capabilities are described from public materials, and tools sold to contractors have incentives that rhyme with the carriers' incentives in ways worth remembering.

The call

If you have an open claim right now, here is the sequence, in order, the way I would run it on any job that started going sideways.

  • Get the bilingual estimate: hire or borrow a contractor trained in Xactimate so both documents speak line items. Arguing a lump-sum bid against a line-item scope is bringing a handshake to a spreadsheet fight.
  • Check overhead and profit first: count the trades on the adjuster's scope. Three or more means the 20 percent is your opening supplement in most states. If the line is missing, that is not an oversight to forgive; it is money to ask for.
  • Run the five-item checklist: dust barriers, HEPA scrubbers, floor protection, outlet cover resets, texture blending across the full wall. Missing three of the five means the scope was written fast.
  • Photograph before the tear-off: undamaged roof slopes, adjacent rooms, every wall the crew will touch. Matching arguments and supplement fights are won with before photos, and after demolition there is nothing left to compare.
  • Run an AI audit before you sign: a sixty-second scan costs nothing and hands you a line-item discrepancy list. Treat its output as a starting point for negotiation, not gospel, because the auditor's incentives deserve the same skepticism you now apply to the adjuster.
  • Know when to escalate: if the gap exceeds about $10,000 or the carrier will not supplement, price a public adjuster against the expected recovery before hiring one. In many states their fee is capped around ten percent of the claim, which means the math has to clear that hurdle to be worth it.

Never accept "the computer says" as a final answer. That computer was configured by the company that owes you money, using price lists a month out of date, with settings chosen to produce the number you are looking at. An estimate is a bid. Bid back.
