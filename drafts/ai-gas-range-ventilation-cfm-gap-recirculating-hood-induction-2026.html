Your Builder Hung a 200-CFM Hood Over a 60,000-BTU Gas Range. The Air Math Doesn't Work.

By Priya Greenwood, September 15, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

Walk the final inspection of a brand-new home and the kitchen looks finished, the gas range gleaming under its hood. Overhead, the under-cabinet unit hums politely, all 200 cubic feet per minute of it, while the burners beneath can throw off 58,000 BTU of combustion byproducts that have to go somewhere, and somewhere, it turns out, is mostly back into the room.

Nothing here is broken, and nothing violates code, which is precisely the problem.

Read that twice.

What the flame actually makes

Your kitchen flame is a small chemical plant. Every blue flame produces nitrogen dioxide, carbon monoxide, formaldehyde, and ultrafine particles, while the fuel arriving through the gas line carries trace hydrocarbons that escape the burner unburned.

A Stanford team led by PhD candidate Yannai Kashtan measured over 100 American homes and reported in Science Advances in 2024: a gas or propane stove adds about 4 parts per billion to a household's annual NO2 exposure, roughly 75 percent of the World Health Organization's long-term guideline. "In other words, just by using gas stoves regularly, you're eating up three-quarters of that guideline," Kashtan said, and concentrations breached health benchmarks in bedrooms within an hour of cooking, lingering for hours after the burners went cold.

Then there is benzene, a known leukemogen, and the Stanford results on it are the ugliest of the set. Stanford's Eric Lebel and Rob Jackson found in a 2022 Environmental Science & Technology study that one burner on high or an oven at 350 degrees can push indoor benzene above average secondhand-smoke levels, and the contamination migrates to bedrooms. That benzene came from the gas, not the food: pan-frying salmon and bacon added nothing, which means you cannot cook your way out of this one.

Combine the pollutants and the Stanford team's estimates climb: nitrogen dioxide alone may account for about 50,000 current pediatric asthma cases in the United States; all pollutants plus short-term NO2 spikes may account for up to 200,000, roughly a tenth of the asthma burden from all road traffic. Long-term NO2 from stoves may contribute to up to 19,000 premature adult deaths a year. An earlier RMI-led analysis attributed 12.7 percent of U.S. childhood asthma to gas cooking, a figure comparable to secondhand smoke, though a critical review in PMC flagged its reliance on a 2013 meta-analysis, and the 2024 paper's central estimate runs lower. On direction, the evidence is not in dispute. Only the size of the number is, and that is a debate about how many thousands of children are affected rather than whether the kitchen fills with pollutants every time you sear a steak.

A worksheet nobody ran

Here is the calculation your builder skipped. Industry's rule of thumb for gas ranges says 100 CFM of exhaust for every 10,000 BTU of burner output, a ratio carried through decades of hood manufacturer guidance, and it has survived because the physics behind it is stubborn. A standard 30-inch, four-burner gas range totals roughly 50,000 to 60,000 BTU, so do the division: that range wants 500 to 600 CFM of exhaust, minimum. Simple division. Ugly answer.

Now open the spec sheet for a typical production home, where the under-cabinet hood manages 160 to 250 CFM against burners that can demand triple that on a holiday cooking marathon. It delivers somewhere between a third and a half of what the physics asks for, assuming it is vented to the outside at all, which it often is not.

Here is the part builders hope you never ask about. Ductless, recirculating hoods pull air through a charcoal filter that catches grease and return everything else, nitrogen dioxide, benzene, carbon monoxide, methane, straight back into the kitchen. ASHRAE Standard 62.2 is explicit: a recirculating hood cannot satisfy the ventilation requirement unless it is paired with a separate exhaust system delivering at least five air changes per hour of kitchen volume.

The code itself lowballs the problem, too: ASHRAE 62.2's fallback math says a 12-by-12-foot kitchen with 9-foot ceilings, 1,296 cubic feet, needs five air changes an hour, about 108 CFM. The code number says 108 while the BTU rule says 500-plus, and builders spec to the smaller number because the smaller number is the one the inspector checks, leaving the gap for your family's air to fall through. Five to one. The code loses.

One more detail worth knowing: industry convention says don't trust the rule of thumb too hard either. Green Building Advisor's analysis notes the sizing rules lack analytic rigor, and capture efficiency, the hood's depth, its height above the burners, cross-drafts from an open window, matters as much as raw CFM, which means a shallow 200-CFM hood mounted 30 inches over the range is decoration with a fan.

Models exist. Nobody runs them.

Here is where the AI in this publication's name earns its place. Design-stage modeling tools, cove.tool, IES VE, and their competitors, can simulate airflow, contaminant accumulation, and ventilation effectiveness room by room before a single stud goes up, which means the NO2 pooling and benzene migration the Stanford team spent months measuring in real homes could have been predicted on a laptop during design development.

Underneath those tools, pollutant models derived from Lawrence Berkeley National Lab research predict exactly what the Stanford team measured: NO2 pooling in bedrooms, benzene migrating down the hall. Builders do not run these models before locking the hood spec, because a purchasing department optimizing a $90 appliance line item makes the decision, not any calculation about the air a family will breathe for thirty years. The technology to check the work exists, and nobody uses it. Ever.

What to actually do about it

If you are building, buying, or renovating, three decisions matter, in order of cost. First, demand a vented hood sized to the burners: add the BTU of every burner, divide by 100, and that is your minimum CFM, so a 58,000-BTU range wants 580. Make sure the duct runs to the outside, not into the attic, and budget for makeup air, since codes generally require it above 400 CFM and a powerful hood in a tight house will backdraft the water heater.

Second, wire for the exit even if you love gas. Have your electrician run a 240-volt, 50-amp circuit (NEMA 14-50 receptacle, 6-3 cable) to the range location during rough-in, before the drywall closes the walls and turns a two-hour wire pull into a two-day fishing expedition through finished framing. At framing, that is a breaker, a run of wire, and a box, a marginal cost in the low hundreds that buys you the option to rip out the gas range in ten years without opening a single wall. After drywall, fishing that same circuit through finished walls, patching, painting, and possibly upgrading the panel runs $1,500 to $3,000 or more, per Angi's 240V installation data and 2025 contractor pricing surveys. Builders who haunt new-construction forums say the same thing: go 50 amps now, because you cannot upsize wire after the fact, and the option is worth ten times its cost.

Third, use the hood you have, every single time you cook, because ventilation only works when it runs, and the studies keep finding that the fans exist in most kitchens but stay switched off through the very meals that need them most. Only about a third of people with exhaust hoods report using them regularly, which means two-thirds of America's installed range hoods sit idle while the evidence for what they are supposed to remove keeps getting stronger. If your hood is recirculating, know that it is a grease filter, not air quality equipment, and open a window while you cook.

Hearing the other side at full strength

The American Gas Association has not been quiet about this research, and its objections deserve a fair hearing. Its review of the Lebel benzene study argues the researchers modeled indoor concentrations under extreme, beyond-worst-case scenarios rather than measuring them; that most median simulations fell below California's 8-hour health screening level for benzene; that odorants in the gas would alert occupants before dangerous concentrations; and that gas stoves have cooked in homes for a century with episodic rather than chronic exposure. The association's policy conclusion: the sensible intervention is proper vented exhaust, not tearing out gas infrastructure or punishing households that cannot afford electrical upgrades.

Part of that critique is fair. Concentration modeling does depend on assumptions, and the 12.7-percent asthma figure rests on older epidemiology that the field has since improved on. But the 2024 Stanford paper answers the strongest version of the objection by measuring real homes, over 100 of them, and still finding bedroom-level exceedances hours after cooking. And notice something: the industry's own proposed solution, vent it properly, is exactly what this article asks builders to do. We agree on the fix. We disagree on whether the current code-minimum spec counts as doing it, and that disagreement is the entire ballgame.

What this analysis did not prove

A few honest limits before the close. That 100-CFM-per-10,000-BTU rule is industry convention, not derived physics; hood geometry and capture efficiency can make a well-designed 400-CFM hood outperform a sloppy 600-CFM one, which is why the number in this article is a floor rather than a target, and why a deep, properly mounted 400-CFM hood can beat a shallow 600-CFM unit fighting cross-drafts from the kitchen window. Those 19,000 premature deaths are extrapolated from outdoor NO2 exposure studies, and the Stanford authors themselves note the indoor applicability is unclear. That 12.7-percent asthma attribution is contested, with the newer paper's central estimate at 3.8 percent. Recirculating-hood prevalence in tract construction is widely reported but has no national dataset behind it, so this article claims no specific percentage. Circuit cost ranges are national averages; in high-cost markets the retrofit number can double.

What survives all of that: a vented hood sized to the burners is cheap insurance at rough-in, a ductless hood is not ventilation, and the modeling tools that could prove any of it before the drywall goes up sit unused on engineers' desktops. Your kitchen's air was decided by a purchasing spreadsheet. It did not have to be.
