Florida Inspected 109,000 Homes for Hurricane Discounts. Only 12% Saw Their Premium Drop.

By Jake Kowalski, September 13, 2026, Construction Technology

On April 1, 2026, Florida threw out the form that decides your hurricane insurance discount and replaced it with a new one. Same form number, OIR-B1-1802, but the first real revision since 2012. More photo documentation. New fields for wind zone and roof slope. A whole new section for FORTIFIED designation. Permit records and product approval numbers now required for credit categories that used to sail through on an inspector's say-so. (Wescon Construction) If your inspection was done on the old form before the cutoff, it is still good at most carriers until its five-year clock runs out. If it was done after, welcome to the new paperwork.

Five weeks later, an insurance software company launched an AI to read the thing. Exzeo Group, a Tampa outfit trading on the NYSE, rolled out WindForm Pro on May 5, an AI data-extraction tool built specifically for the stricter documentation demands the state just imposed on carriers, inspectors, and agents. (Business Wire, May 2026) Read between the lines of that press release and the message is blunt: the manual pipeline was already drowning, and the new form just added weight.

So here is the machine as it stands in late 2026: the state made the paperwork harder, the industry built a robot to read the harder paperwork, and the credits the paperwork exists to deliver are mostly not arriving.

That last claim is not vibes; it is arithmetic, straight from the state's own testimony.

In February 2025, the director of the My Safe Florida Home program presented results to the state Senate's Banking and Insurance Committee. The program had completed 109,443 free wind mitigation inspections. It had awarded 25,530 grants and reimbursed $240 million. And of the grant recipients, 12,570 reported their insurance premiums actually dropped. (WFTS/Tampa Bay 28) Do the division: 12,570 drops against 109,443 inspections is 11.5 percent. About one in eight inspected homes got the payoff the whole apparatus exists to produce. Another 7,065 owners reported premiums stayed flat. And 4,190 reported their premiums went up anyway, hardening be damned.

Run the money side and it gets uglier. $240 million reimbursed across 25,530 grants works out to roughly $9,400 of public money per hardened home. The legislature has now appropriated $576 million for grants plus $28.5 million for 178,184 inspections. (Florida Senate memo, March 2025) That is a serious pile of cash aimed at a conversion rate that would get a marketing intern fired.

None of this means the credits are fake. Florida law is explicit: every residential property insurer in the state must offer discounts when qualifying wind-resistant features are documented on the 1802 form. (NEXGEN Roofing, citing FL Statute 627.0629) The form scores seven categories: building code era, roof covering, roof deck attachment, roof-to-wall connection, roof geometry, secondary water resistance, and opening protection. Credits apply to the windstorm portion of your premium, which in Florida typically runs 30 to 70 percent of the total bill. The state emergency management booklet puts the theoretical ceiling at 88 percent of the windstorm portion. (FL Division of Emergency Management) The Office of Insurance Regulation's 2024 wind-loss study found homes with comprehensive protection see 20 to 30 percent off their total annual premium. Program data shows owners who completed qualifying improvements averaging over $900 a year in savings.

$900 a year. For $75 to $150, the inspection documents it, and the report stays valid five years. (Wescon; Anchor and Atlas Insurance) Say $125, and say you capture even half the average savings. It pays for itself in under two months, then keeps paying for five years. On pure ROI, there is no better deal in Florida homeownership. Not the impact windows. Not the roof. The piece of paper that says you already have them.

Which makes the 12 percent conversion rate a scandal of plumbing, not physics: the features work, but the paperwork never reaches the premium.

Part of the problem is that the form is brutal in ways homeowners never see. Credits are not cumulative: when one applies, others get reduced, and only homes with full opening protection plus comprehensive roof credits approach that 88 percent ceiling. Opening protection is all or nothing. Every window, door, skylight, and garage door has to be protected, and a single unprotected opening torpedoes the rating. Impact glass on every window except the garage? Not full credit. Hurricane shutters stacked in the garage instead of mounted? Zero. The inspector photographs everything, including the label on the impact-rated glass and the exact nail pattern in the attic decking, because the difference between 6d nails and 8d nails at six-inch spacing moves real money. (LegalClarity) This is a form where a toenail versus a clip at the roof-to-wall connection is a line item. It rewards the paranoid and punishes the almost.

Under the 2026 revision, the bar rises further. Inspectors now have to document wind zone and roof slope, attach more photos, and produce permit records and product approval numbers for categories that previously ran on visual verification. A FORTIFIED Home section now sits on the form, and IBHS issued a technical bulletin in March 2026 explaining how FORTIFIED documentation can satisfy certain field-verification requirements on the new form starting in April. (IBHS Technical Bulletin FH 2026-02) IBHS's FORTIFIED program just crossed 100,000 designations nationwide, with more than 20,000 issued in 2025 alone. (IBHS, May 2026) An Alabama insurance-department study of Hurricane Sally claims found FORTIFIED homes outperformed even houses built to equivalent supplemental codes but never IBHS-inspected, which in turn beat code-minimum construction, across 40,195 policies and $17 billion of insured value. (Alabama DOI) The engineering is settled, and verification has become the product.

That is the opening the AI vendors are walking through. Exzeo's tool sits on the carrier side, extracting and structuring data from the forms so underwriters stop re-keying PDFs by hand. The inspection software shops had to ship new templates the week the form changed. HomeInspections.com launched statewide wind-mit reports starting at $125 in May 2026, betting that easier scheduling plus the new form equals volume. (CB Herald, May 2026) Everybody in this chain agrees on the diagnosis: the bottleneck was never the hurricane straps. It was the form about the hurricane straps.

Now the part the industry would rather skip. Former state senator Jeff Brandes looked at the grant bureaucracy and suggested the simpler move: just eliminate the sales tax on qualifying roofs, windows, and exterior doors. (WPTV, August 2025) He has a point. A program that inspects 109,000 homes, spends $240 million, and gets 12 percent of them a premium drop is a machine for generating inspections, not savings. And the 4,190 owners whose premiums rose after hardening are the rebuttal to the whole premise: insurers price the market, not your house. Reinsurance costs, litigation, and a decade of assignment-of-benefits abuse moved rates faster than any nail pattern could offset. Your 8d nails at six inches do not care about the reinsurance market. The reinsurance market does not care about your 8d nails.

Here is the playbook anyway, because the credits are real even if the system is leaky. First, get the inspection, especially if you have never had one or yours is older than five years. $75 to $150, about an hour, valid five years. Second, if you re-roofed, get a fresh inspection immediately: a new roof usually upgrades the covering, the deck attachment, and possibly the secondary water resistance in one shot, and none of it counts until the form says so. Third, audit your openings before the inspector arrives, because the all-or-nothing rule means one unprotected skylight can nuke the biggest credit on the form. Check the garage door glazing specifically. Fourth, dig up your permit records and product approval numbers now; the 2026 form demands them and "the roofer has them" is not a filing system. Fifth, if you are buying in Florida, make the seller's current 1802 part of the offer paperwork. It turns the insurance quote from a guess into a number, and in a market where the premium can kill the deal, that number is leverage.

What this analysis could not establish: the true capture rate beyond one program's self-reported data. The 12 percent figure comes from grant recipients who answered the state's survey; 1,705 gave no premium information at all, and homeowners who skipped the survey may differ systematically from those who answered. That $900 average-savings figure comes from program reporting via an industry site, not an audited dataset. Carrier-specific credit factors vary by territory and are proprietary, so no universal dollar value per feature exists. And the AI form-reading tools are too new to have any public accuracy or adoption data; Exzeo's launch claims are untested by third parties.

Nobody comes out of this summary looking good. Florida built a $576 million machine to document hurricane resistance and forgot to check whether the documentation reaches the bill. Insurers built AI to read the new forms faster, which solves the carriers' paperwork problem, not yours. Underneath it all, the engineering genuinely works: straps and nails and impact glass, with the IBHS data on 100,000 FORTIFIED homes as the strongest evidence in the whole story. But between the roof and the premium sits a form, and between the form and the discount sits a system that converts about one time in eight. Get the inspection. Keep the permits. Photograph the labels. Florida's cheapest hurricane protection was never the shutter. It was the paperwork proving you already bought the shutter.
