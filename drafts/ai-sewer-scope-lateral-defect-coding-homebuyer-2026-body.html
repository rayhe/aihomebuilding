Three hours on the house. He walked the roof, opened the panel, ran every faucet, crawled the attic with a flashlight, and wrote up seventeen pages about a missing GFCI and a drip at the guest bath trap. Then he packed up, and nobody ever looked at the most expensive pipe on the property. Not because he was lazy. Because his contract told him not to.

A general home inspection is a visual exam of accessible systems. Buried infrastructure sits outside its scope, in the same bucket as radon, mold, and termites. That means the sewer lateral, the pipe carrying everything from your house to the city main, gets a pass. In Minneapolis, the city spells out who owns that pipe: you do, all the way to the main, including the wye connection under the street. Average replacement cost, per the city's January 2026 homeowner guide: $13,000. Documented range: $2,150 to $62,000. Seventeen pages, and not a word about the lateral.

Your pipe has a birthday, and it matters

Laterals fail on a schedule set by their material, and the material is set by the era. Homes built before or around the 1960s usually sit on clay tile, brittle stuff that cracks at the joints and invites tree roots through every seam. Cast iron arrived in the 1970s and corrodes from the inside out. Plastic showed up in the 1980s and is the best of the three. Then there is Orangeburg, a bituminized fiber pipe used from the 1940s through the 1970s that deforms under soil pressure like a straw in a fist. If your house wears any of the first three, you own a maintenance item. If it wears Orangeburg, you own a countdown.

For decades the defense was a sewer scope: a plumber pushes a camera down the cleanout and narrates what he sees. It costs around $250 to $400, and it works, in the same way that reading a book works. What changed is that the camera footage now gets read twice. Once by software trained on millions of pipe defects, once by a human who checks the software.

Two hundred ways to say "crack"

NASSCO, the trade group that writes the inspection standard for American sewers, publishes a coding language called PACP. A certified operator logs every observation at the distance the camera traveled, adds a clock position for where it sits around the pipe, and estimates how much of the cross-section is lost. There are more than 200 coding variations. NASSCO's own position paper admits the obvious: subjective interpretation by operators influences coding decisions. Two humans can watch the same crack and code it differently.

That subjectivity, plus roughly 1.3 million miles of pipe to look at, is why automated defect recognition moved from research to revenue. Today's commercial workflow is AI-first, human-second. Software flags every crack, fracture, offset joint, root intrusion, and deposit frame by frame. Then a certified PACP coder reviews the machine's homework. CUES, a major inspection hardware vendor, runs exactly this pipeline in its GraniteNet cloud service. WinCan has offered AI-powered defect coding as a service since 2021.

A Walnut Creek company ate the pilots

SewerAI, out of Walnut Creek, California, built its models on 135 million feet of inspected pipe and sells a platform called PIONEER with an autocoder named AutoCode. Their pitch is boring in the best way: upload the video, get back coded defects, plan the repairs. More than 2,000 cities and a national contractor network, the company claims, and in June 2026 it took a strategic investment from JMI Equity to keep scaling.

What caught my eye comes from Phoenix. In 2022 and 2023 the city ran five AI vendors through the same 30 pipe videos, about 1.7 miles, and scored them on PACP coding accuracy and cost. SewerAI won on both: highest accuracy, lowest net cost per linear foot. Phoenix expanded the program to 107 miles. That is municipal work, not residential, but the coding discipline is identical. A root intrusion is a root intrusion whether it sits under Main Street or under your driveway.

The case against the hype, at full strength

NASSCO has drawn a clear line: no AI system is certified to recognize every PACP observation. Per the standard, machine coding is a first pass, not a verdict, and trained operators are required to do the quality control, because that is where errors scale badly. Any vendor telling you the human is optional is running ahead of the standard.

Also true: garbage in, garbage out. Camera speeds and lighting are spelled out in the coding standard, and a tech who races the push reel through a murky line produces footage no model can rescue. A scope is only as good as the operator holding the cable, and the AI does not fix that.

And not every flagged defect means excavation. Roots can be cut for a few hundred dollars. A belly, a low spot holding water, can be monitored. Deformation at depth is the one that summons the excavator. Software finds. Judgment decides. That distinction is worth money, because the cheapest outcome is paying $300 to learn the line is fine, and the second cheapest is paying $250 to cut roots before they become $13,000 of open trench.

What this analysis could not prove

Limits, stated plainly. Those Minneapolis numbers, the $13,000 average and $2,150 to $62,000 range, come from homeowner project data in a cold-climate city with old clay stock, so your market may run lower. That "around $300" scope price comes from a sample 2023 inspection report, not a national price survey, and metro pricing varies. I could not independently verify SewerAI's 135-million-feet training figure or the 2,000-city count beyond company materials and trade press; the Phoenix pilot results come from Trenchless Technology's reporting, not the city's raw scoring sheets. A Palo Alto inspector quoted in local press is the source for the material-era timeline (clay pre-1960s, cast iron 1970s, plastic 1980s), and regional building practices shift those dates by a few years.

What to do with this

If you are buying, order a sewer scope inside your contingency window. Budget $250 to $400, and treat it like radon and termites: a separate service your general inspector was never going to perform. Demand the footage with distances and material callouts. "Pipe looks fine" is a vibe, not a report. A defect at 8 feet and a defect at 46 feet are completely different excavations, and only the video tells you which one you are buying.

If you own an older home, scope once to establish a baseline. Clay from before the 60s means budgeting for root management. Cast iron from the 70s means checking for internal scaling every few years. One scope now turns every future slow drain from a mystery into a data point.

If you build, camera the lateral before backfill. A Colorado plumber's writeup I found tells the whole story in one line: brand-new PVC, crushed by construction equipment before the trench was closed. A ten-minute camera run catches the crush your crew just buried. The pipe you cannot see is the one you will pay for twice.
