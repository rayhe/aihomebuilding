# Research: Your Agent Is About to Start Talking to Other Agents

**Slug:** muse-agent-to-agent-mcp-a2a-2026

**Date:** November 16, 2026
**Status:** RESEARCH complete, ready for DRAFT

## Topic
Muse works alone today. It reads your email, books your table, and drafts your replies, all inside Meta's walled garden of about forty connectors. But the job you actually want done — book the flight, find the hotel that fits the flight, pay for both, tell your calendar — is a relay race between agents that belong to different companies. Two open protocols now exist to run that race: MCP, which connects an agent to tools and data, and A2A, which connects an agent to other agents. Both landed under the Linux Foundation within the last year, both are in production, and Muse already speaks one of them through the back door: ask it in chat to connect to an MCP server and it writes its own client on its VM in under a minute. The other one, A2A, Muse does not speak natively at all. This article maps the four-layer stack (MCP, A2A, payments, commerce), counts the trust boundaries a single multi-agent errand crosses, and gives readers the one test that tells them whether an agent-to-agent handoff is safe to approve: who vouches for the agent on the other end, and what exactly did it promise to do.

## Kill test
PASS. A reader deciding how much of their life to hand to Muse needs to know what happens the moment their agent starts delegating to agents they did not choose, cannot see, and did not approve by name. That is a use-it-better / trust-it-wisely decision, not a press-release rewrite. No article on this site covers MCP, A2A, or agent-to-agent delegation yet.

## Primary sources (verified)
1. **Anthropic / Model Context Protocol, modelcontextprotocol.io** — MCP announced November 25, 2024 as an open specification for connecting AI applications to tools and data over JSON-RPC 2.0. Donated December 9, 2025 to the Agentic AI Foundation, a directed fund under the Linux Foundation co-founded by Anthropic, Block, and OpenAI. Specification 2026-07-28 (final July 28, 2026) made the core stateless: no initialize handshake, no session IDs. By December 2025 Anthropic counted 10,000+ active public MCP servers; by July 2026 it reported roughly 400 million monthly SDK downloads (4x growth over the year) and 950+ connectors in Claude's directory. Adopted in ChatGPT (March 2025), Gemini, Microsoft Copilot, Cursor, VS Code, n8n, Zapier. URL: https://modelcontextprotocol.io (via https://kanerika.com/blogs/model-context-protocol/ and https://zylos.ai/research/2026-07-29-mcp-2026-spec-overhaul-stateless-oauth-tasks-apps/)
2. **Google Developers Blog, "Announcing the Agent2Agent Protocol," April 9, 2025** — A2A announced with 50+ launch partners. Agents publish a machine-readable Agent Card at `/.well-known/agent-card.json` describing skills, endpoints, and auth; communication runs over HTTP, JSON-RPC 2.0, and Server-Sent Events. Donated to the Linux Foundation June 2025. Version 1.0 shipped March 2026 with signed agent cards (cryptographic signature verifying the card came from the domain owner); v1.0.1 followed May 28, 2026. By April 2026 the Linux Foundation reported 150+ supporting organizations including AWS, Microsoft, IBM, Salesforce, SAP, Cisco; 22,000+ GitHub stars. A2A joined the Agentic AI Foundation as a Growth Stage project August 27, 2026. IBM's Agent Communication Protocol merged into A2A August 2025. 3. **Parallel, "Meta Muse Custom Integrations: Connect Any API or MCP," November 2026 (hands-on test)** — Muse has no "add MCP server" setting. Asked in chat to connect to an MCP server, it said so directly, then built a small bridge on its VM: wrote an MCP client using the official SDK, connected over streamable HTTP, and saved the integration as a reusable skill. Test connection took one message and under a minute. Meta's safety write-up: "Muse can also write its own custom connectors for other services you care about if they have their own APIs or CLIs." Meta Help Center: keys go into the Secure Credentials Store, and Meta does not review custom connectors — "you are trusting the service provider directly." Built-in connector code runs in a privilege-separated worker outside the agent's runtime, so the agent never touches the OAuth token. URL: https://parallel.ai/articles/meta-muse-custom-integrations

## Facts established
- MCP (agent to tool) and A2A (agent to agent) are complementary layers, not rivals. MCP: Anthropic, Nov 2024, donated Dec 2025 to the Linux Foundation's Agentic AI Foundation. A2A: Google, April 2025, donated June 2025, v1.0 March 2026, joined AAIF August 2026.
- Scale: 10,000+ public MCP servers (Dec 2025), ~400M monthly SDK downloads (July 2026), 950+ connectors in Claude's directory; A2A 150+ supporting organizations, 22,000+ repo stars, production deployments in customer service, marketing automation, security operations, IT support.
- Payments and commerce got their own layers in January 2026: AP2 (Google, with Mastercard/PayPal/Amex/Salesforce) and UCP (runs over MCP, A2A, or REST). A2A deliberately does not handle money.
- Muse's interop position: no one-click MCP add and no native A2A client. It reaches other systems two ways: built-in connectors (~40, Meta-reviewed, tokens held outside the agent runtime) and custom connectors it builds itself in chat (MCP over streamable HTTP, saved as a skill, unreviewed by Meta, keys in the Secure Credentials Store). A local MCP server cannot be reached from Muse's cloud VM.
- The trust gap is documented, not theoretical: NSA May 2026 guidance says proliferation outpaced the security model; MCPInspect found 833 vulnerable servers; OX Security found a systemic SDK flaw; Ars Technica (Oct 5, 2026) reported malicious instructions hopping agent to agent at major companies; OWASP lists insecure inter-agent communication (ASI07) in its 2026 agentic top 10.
- Gartner (August 2025 forecast): more than 40% of agentic AI projects will be canceled by end of 2027 due to costs, unclear ROI, or inadequate risk controls.

## Original contribution (novel analysis for this article)
1. **The four-layer stack, counted, with Muse placed on it.** Nobody in the sources lays out all four layers in one place with Muse's position marked: (1) MCP — agent to tool, Muse speaks it via custom connectors; (2) A2A — agent to agent, Muse has no native client; (3) AP2 — agent payments, Muse has no published support; (4) UCP — agent commerce negotiation, Muse has no published support. That is 1 of 4 layers Muse speaks natively-ish, and the one it speaks is the one Meta does not review. The article's core diagram is a stack with Muse's checkmarks and gaps.
2. **The trust-boundary count for one errand.** Take "book my flight and a hotel that fits it, then pay": Muse asks an airline agent for options (boundary 1: Muse to airline agent), the airline agent hands the booking to a payment agent (boundary 2: agent to agent, invisible to you), the payment agent returns a confirmation Muse relays to your calendar (boundary 3: result back across the chain). Three hops, three places an injected instruction can ride along, and only the first hop is something you approved by name. The Ars Technica case is the proof that hop 2 exists in the wild. The honest advice follows: approve the errand, not the chain, and ask Muse to name every agent it delegated to before it spends anything.
3. **The "who vouches" test.** A2A v1.0 signed agent cards verify the card came from the domain owner — they authenticate the address, not the behavior. MCP custom connectors in Muse are unreviewed by Meta. So in both protocols, nobody vouches for what the other side will actually do with your request. The article gives readers a three-question test before any agent-to-agent approval: who built the other agent, what is it allowed to do with my data, and what happens if it lies about the result. If Muse cannot answer all three from the agent card or connector description, the answer is no.
4. **The governance surprise.** Both protocols ended up under the same Linux Foundation fund within a year — Anthropic gave up MCP, Google gave up A2A — which is the strongest signal that interop is real infrastructure and not a vendor land grab. That is the optimistic half of the story, and it is why the article recommends learning the stack now rather than waiting: the plumbing is being standardized in public, and the first agents to use it well will be the ones whose users understand the handoff.

## Limitations / what we could not verify
- No hands-on test: no Muse account was used, so the under-a-minute MCP custom connector build is Parallel's test, not ours, and we did not verify Muse's current connector count or whether any A2A support has appeared since the November 4 crosswalk check.
- Meta has published no statement about adopting A2A, AP2, or UCP for Muse; the "no native support" characterization is an absence in public documentation, flagged as such.
- Adoption numbers (10,000+ servers, 400M downloads, 150+ organizations) are vendor- or foundation-reported, not independently audited.
- The Ars Technica / Mohiuddin findings are reported secondhand here (via Servicta's summary); the underlying disclosures were not read in full.
- No independent audit of Muse's Sentinel gating or of how custom-connector skills store and reuse credentials over time.

## Strongest counterargument
The cautious case mistakes youth for danger. MCP and A2A are the boring infrastructure the web needed: open specs, neutral Linux Foundation governance, signed agent cards, OAuth 2.0, mTLS, and OpenID Connect at the inter-agent layer. The vulnerabilities found so far — SDK command injection, SSRF in one vendor's toolbox, misleading tool descriptions — are implementation bugs being fixed in public, the same way the early web fixed its own. Refusing interop does not make Muse safer; it keeps every integration bespoke, which only large vendors can afford, and leaves users copying and pasting between agents by hand — which is itself an error-prone, unlogged handoff with no audit trail at all. The protocols are the fix for the chaos, not the cause of it. The article must give that argument its full weight before recommending caution.
