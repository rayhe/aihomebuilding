43 Million Americans Drink Water Nobody Is Required to Test.

By Priya Greenwood · October 8, 2026

They bought the farmhouse in 2019 for the meadow and the quiet, and the well came with it, a concrete casing behind the barn that had been delivering water since before the road was paved. Nobody at the closing table suggested testing it. Six years later a neighbor's kid got a blood test for an unrelated school screening, the numbers came back wrong, and the county health department knocked on doors up and down the road. Their well read 14 micrograms of arsenic per liter, forty percent over the federal limit that governs every public water system in the country and exactly nothing about the water coming out of their kitchen tap. That opening is a composite built from the testing patterns in the sources below, but the ending is not unusual: a $300 lab test would have found it before the offer, and instead six years of morning coffee went unmeasured.

Arsenic has no taste, no odor, and no color in water, which is precisely why the testing question matters and precisely why almost nobody answers it, because a contaminant you cannot detect with your senses gets treated by the human brain as a contaminant that does not exist. An estimated 43 million Americans draw drinking water from private wells, roughly 15 percent of the population, and the federal Safe Drinking Water Act simply does not cover them, a carve-out written into the law's definition of a public water system, which starts at 15 service connections or 25 people served, a threshold that leaves every single-family well and most small shared wells outside federal protection entirely. EPA says this plainly on its private wells page: these wells are not regulated by EPA and are not required to follow EPA's standards, and the owner is responsible for testing and treatment. Guidance exists. The EPA recommends annual testing for coliform bacteria, nitrates, total dissolved solids, and pH. Nobody enforces the recommendation, nobody sends a reminder, and nobody fines you for skipping it. A public utility that skipped its testing schedule would face the state within weeks. A household can skip it for decades and the only consequence is not knowing. That is the whole system.

What the USGS Found When It Looked

Because nobody is required to test, the best picture of what's in American well water comes from scientists who went looking on their own. A USGS national retrospective of self-supplied domestic wells found arsenic above the 10 microgram-per-liter federal limit in roughly 11 percent of the 7,580 wells evaluated, nitrate above the limit in about 8 percent of 3,465 wells, and uranium above its limit in around 4 percent. A separate USGS sampling program found that about 1 in 5 private wells exceeded a human-health benchmark for at least one contaminant, with radon and arsenic the most frequent offenders, both of them natural, both of them geology's doing rather than anyone's fault.

1 in 5 private wells sampled by the USGS exceeded a human-health benchmark for at least one contaminant, most of them natural and undetectable by taste, smell, or sight.

None of this is evenly distributed, which makes it worse rather than better. Arsenic clusters in New England bedrock, the Great Lakes, the Pacific Northwest, and the West. Nitrate follows intensive farmland through the Midwest Corn Belt and California's Central Valley. Ayotte's USGS work estimated 2.1 million Americans drink well water above the arsenic standard, with hot spots like eastern New England where one in five wells exceed the limit and parts of Maine reaching 45 percent. Your neighbor's clean test tells you nothing about your well, because aquifer chemistry can shift across a single property line and the USGS scientists who mapped this country well by well found that depth offers no refuge either, with arsenic mobilization that can worsen the deeper you drill. Aquifer chemistry can change across a single property line, and a 2017 Scientific American piece quoted USGS scientists noting that deeper wells are not safer, since arsenic mobilization can worsen with depth. Proximity is not protection. Time is not protection either, because aquifers shift with drought, pumping, and land use.

New Jersey Ran the Experiment Nobody Else Would

One state refused to leave this to memory and occasion, and New Jersey's Private Well Testing Act has required since 2002 that every contract of sale for a property on a private well include testing as a condition, with closing barred until buyer and seller have both received and reviewed the results, plus five-year retests for landlords. New Jersey's Private Well Testing Act, in force since 2002, requires that every contract of sale for a property on a private well include water testing as a condition, and closing cannot occur until the buyer and seller have both received and reviewed the results. Landlords must test every five years and hand tenants the results. It is the only law of its kind in the country, and twenty years of it produced a paper in Water Policy that reads like a control group for the other 49 states.

About 134,000 wells have been tested under the law, roughly a third of the state's private wells. Of those, 15.7 percent exceeded at least one primary drinking water standard. Historically the culprits were gross alpha radioactivity and arsenic, both natural. Then New Jersey added three PFAS compounds to the required panel in 2022, and since then more than 12 percent of tested wells have exceeded the standard for at least one PFAS. Read that again slowly: one law, one state, one required panel, and one in eight wells came back hot for forever chemicals that nobody was looking for until the panel forced the question. Multiply that dynamic across the states with no panel at all and the national blind spot starts to look less like a gap and more like a policy.

Here is the part that should bother you: the law is a consumer-information law. It requires testing, not treatment. A seller can hand you results showing 30 micrograms of arsenic and still close the deal, because the statute's theory is that informed buyers negotiate, remediate, or walk away. Economists studying the program found that even with the mandate, only about a quarter to a third of the state's wells have been tested after two decades, since the law fires only at property transfer and landlords' five-year cycle is lightly enforced. A mandate closes part of the distance between guidance and behavior. It does not close all of it.

The AI Map That Screens a Parcel for Free

This is where machine learning enters, and it enters with an honest limitation attached, because the USGS has now published public, parcel-scale arsenic exceedance probability maps built with boosted regression trees and random forests, trained on real well chemistry and geospatial predictors like precipitation and soil geochemistry, which makes the maps the cheapest screening tool in the well-water business and also one that almost no homebuyer knows exists.

Now the honest part, because the headline accuracy number is a comfort blanket. The model predicting exceedance of the 10 microgram limit scores 91.2 percent overall accuracy on test data. Its sensitivity is 33.9 percent. In plain language: it misses roughly two-thirds of the wells that actually exceed the standard, while its 98.2 percent specificity means it is very good at identifying low-risk areas. That asymmetry is the correct way to use it. A low modeled probability is genuinely reassuring. A high modeled probability is a signal to order the full metals panel instead of the basic bacteria-and-nitrate screen. What the map cannot do is clear a specific well, because geology varies over tens of meters and the models resolve at roughly a kilometer. Maps are screening, not clearance. Confuse the two and the model becomes another way to feel safe without being tested.

Run the Arithmetic Before the Offer

A certified-lab well panel runs $150 to $400 depending on the state and the parameter list, while arsenic treatment, typically an adsorption or reverse-osmosis system, costs $5,000 to $15,000 installed, nitrate systems land in a similar range, and radon-in-water aeration adds thousands more. Against the USGS finding that roughly 1 in 5 private wells exceeds a health benchmark, the expected-value math is not close: a buyer who skips the test accepts a one-in-five chance of inheriting a five-figure problem on closing day, against a $300 test that would have priced the problem into the negotiation. Even discounting the USGS number hard, calling it 1 in 10 and the fix $7,500, the expected exposure is $750 against a $300 test. The test is the cheapest contingency in the entire transaction, and most buyers spend more on the home inspection's crawl-space photos than on the water their children will drink.

Sellers should run the same math from the other side of the table. A clean pre-listing test costs $300 and removes the buyer's highest-anxiety unknown, the one contaminant class that is invisible, odorless, and unregulated. In New Jersey, where the test is mandatory, it is simply paperwork. Everywhere else it is a competitive advantage priced like a dinner for two.

The Case Against Testing, Stated Fairly

Testing finds things you then own, and that is a real cost, not a talking point. A positive result can delay closing, trigger renegotiation, or become a disclosure obligation that follows the property. Some buyers, quietly and rationally, prefer the option value of not knowing, and New Jersey's information-only design concedes the point: the law's theory is informed choice, including the choice to buy the arsenic and remediate it. Most exceedances also have off-the-shelf fixes, and the 1-in-5 figure bundles mild exceedances with severe ones. A well at 11 micrograms of arsenic against a 10-microgram standard is a $3,000 treatment decision, not a health emergency, and testing programs that present every exceedance as a crisis will train people to ignore the program.

Fairness cuts the other way too. Treatment money is not evenly distributed, and a testing mandate without remediation support can become a tax on rural sellers, the exact population least able to absorb a $10,000 system. New Jersey's two decades show mandates work partially and slowly. The honest position is that testing is necessary and insufficient, a flashlight rather than a fix, and anyone selling you certainty about well water, from either direction, is selling something.

Where This Analysis Stops Short

Several disclosures belong here. The USGS exceedance percentages come from compiled sampling programs, not a randomized national well census, and local geology dominates so national averages mislead locally. New Jersey's 15.7 percent reflects New Jersey geology, including a radium belt and arsenic-bearing bedrock, and it is not a national number. The Iowa figures, 9 percent tested in the past year and 40 percent drinking untested and unfiltered, describe one state's survey sample, not a national testing rate. Treatment cost ranges are typical-market figures, not quotes, and PFAS treatment sits at the expensive tail. The Lombard model statistics describe test-set performance on compiled well data. The composite opening anecdote is illustrative rather than documented. Nothing here is legal advice about your state's disclosure rules; a real estate attorney in your state will have sharper opinions.

What to Do About Your Well

Buying a home with a well means writing a New Jersey-style test contingency into your offer even if your state does not require one: a certified lab panel covering coliform bacteria, nitrate, arsenic, lead, PFAS, radon in water, and volatile organic compounds, ordered by buyer or seller before closing, with both parties reviewing the results in writing. Before you spend the $300, check the public USGS arsenic probability maps for the parcel as a free screen. A high modeled probability means ordering the full metals panel, not the basic screen, and budgeting treatment into the offer price rather than discovering it after the move.

Owning a well means testing annually for bacteria and nitrate, the two contaminants that change fastest, and running the full panel every three to five years. Keep a log the way you keep oil-change records, because treatment systems need maintenance, filters need replacement, and a system installed in 2019 and ignored since is a decoration. Selling a home with a well means testing before listing, because $300 of clean results removes the negotiation leverage that a buyer's anxious inspector would otherwise hand them. Forty-three million people drink this water. In one state, someone checks. Everywhere else, the check is yours to order, and the water keeps no records of its own.
