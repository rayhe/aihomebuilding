Your Lot Is in Radon Zone 3. The 6-Million-Test Map Says the Zone Is Wrong.

By Catherine Chen · October 7, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Somewhere in a university lab, a computer spent months chewing through six million radon test results, two hundred layers of geology, weather records, census data, and house characteristics, and when it finished, it produced a map that embarrasses the one your county still uses. Drawn from a 1993 EPA survey, the old map paints entire counties in three flat colors: high, moderate, low. Published in the Proceedings of the National Academy of Sciences by Longxiang Li and colleagues, the new one works at the zip-code level and month by month, and it found something the old map cannot say. More than 83 million Americans live in homes whose radon exceeds the federal action level, and most of them live in the zones marked low.

Pause on that. Those homeowners were told by the zone system that they had nothing to worry about. The model built from their neighbors' test results says otherwise. Zones are averages. Radon is a crack.

Radon is a colorless, odorless gas that rises from uranium breaking down in soil and rock, and it enters homes through the foundation: cracks, joints, sump pits, the porous block of a basement wall. EPA estimates put it above the 4 picocuries-per-liter action level in one out of every fifteen homes, and attributes more than 21,000 lung cancer deaths a year to it, making radon the leading cause of lung cancer among people who have never smoked. It is the strangest hazard in residential construction. No stain, no draft, no smell. Nothing reveals it except looking, and the looking starts with a ten-dollar kit.

What the model actually does

The PNAS team trained a random forest on millions of measurements collected by independent labs between 2001 and 2021, layered against roughly 200 predictors: uranium concentration in bedrock, soil moisture, barometric pressure, home age and size, basement presence, even median household income. Against zip codes it could check, the model landed within a mean absolute error of 22.6 becquerels per cubic meter, and it revised the national average upward to 53.3 Bq/m3 against the EPA's older 48.1 estimate. Then it did the thing zone maps cannot: it filled in the blank zip codes, the ones with no tests on record, by matching their geology and housing stock to places that do have tests. That is the whole trick of modern prediction. It does not need your address. It needs your address's cousins. And in February 2026, Ecosense turned this class of public data into a consumer-facing interactive radon map, which means buyers can now look up their own risk layer instead of trusting the three-color county map.

One of its sharpest findings is aimed directly at new construction. A separate deep-learning study of Canadian and Swedish housing found radon levels rising in newer housing stocks, not falling: tighter envelopes, deeper roof insulation, and higher R-values seal houses so well that they also seal the gas in. What drives indoor radon, per the researchers' shortlist, reads like a building code checklist: basement porosity, insulation depth, and the air dynamics of how occupants open windows. Energy tightness and thermal performance are what the code rewards, and every unit of progress on those fronts makes the radon problem marginally worse, unless the ventilation design accounts for it. Almost nowhere does it.

The cheapest intervention sits in an appendix

Radon-resistant new construction, the industry calls it RRNC, is a short list of slab-time details: a gas-permeable layer under the slab, plastic sheeting, sealed penetrations, and a vent pipe stubbed from the sub-slab up through the roof, ready to be activated if a post-construction test comes back hot. Florida's health department puts the installed cost under $1,000 per home, with no special skills or materials. And the EPA's own cost-benefit analysis, published in December 2025, gives the new-build strategy a return that reads like a typo. Build two million homes over twenty years in high-radon areas with radon-reduction features, test them after construction, and install active soil depressurization where tests fail: an estimated 19,000 to 48,600 lung cancer deaths avoided, at a return of $25.99 to $53.80 for every dollar spent. Retrofit testing and fixing of existing homes returns $7.38 to $15.27 per dollar by comparison. Building it in the first time is roughly four times more efficient than fixing it later.

And yet the prescriptive path for all of this, IRC Appendix F, is an appendix. Appendices are optional by default. A jurisdiction adopts them or it does not, and adoption is a patchwork, which means the intervention with the best return in the EPA's own math is optional in most of the country, while the AFCI breaker and the seismic hold-down sailed through as mandatory. The code doesn't care about your timeline, and apparently it does not care about a 53-to-1 return either.

The honest version of the map

Before anyone frames a house by algorithm, the limits. Community-level prediction is what the PNAS model offers, with an error band of 22.6 Bq/m3 on its checked zip codes. It cannot tell you whether your specific slab leaks. It is a screening tool, not a diagnosis, and the EPA's position survives it intact: test every home. Name the selection bias, too, in the measurement databases behind these models. Homes suspected of high radon get tested more, and retested, which tilts volunteer datasets toward the hot end; the EPA's own random-sample residential surveys, roughly 55,000 homes tested under standard protocol, remain the cleaner baseline. One more caution from the literature: a 2026 Zagreb study got a respectable test R-squared of 0.57 from a gradient-boosted model, but its training fit was near perfect, which is the signature of overfitting, and the authors flag what every one of these papers flags, the absence of real building-physics and ventilation-behavior predictors. Your soil, the models know. How you use your windows, they do not.

One correction is owed to the action level, because the EPA is explicit about it. Four picocuries per liter is not a line between safe and dangerous. There is no known safe level of radon exposure, and the agency suggests considering mitigation between 2 and 4 as well. The EPA's own materials put it bluntly: action level is not a safe level. Buyers who treat a 3.8 test as a clean bill of health are misreading the instrument.

What to do Monday morning

If you build homes, the honest play is the split strategy. Rough in the passive system on every slab: the vent pipe, the sealed penetrations, the gas-permeable layer. That is a few hundred dollars of pipe and plastic at the moment when it costs almost nothing to install. Then test every finished home, and activate a fan only where the test says so. The objection writes itself, so state it at full strength: at $1,000 a home, a 200-unit subdivision carries $200,000 of radon cost, and in genuinely low-geology areas most of those systems will never activate. Cheap is the rough-in; smart is the testing. Whether the fan ever turns on is a question the pipe does not ask. Skipping the pipe means tearing up a finished slab later, which is exactly the retrofit cost the EPA's math says to avoid.

If you buy homes, make the test a contract contingency, which the EPA itself recommends for every purchase, basement or not. Thirty-five states now mandate radon measurement or disclosure during property transactions, which means the data trail the PNAS model trained on is partly your paperwork. A ten-dollar charcoal canister works, with two honest caveats. Test the lowest lived-in level, and test under closed-house conditions, because an open-window test in October measures October, not January, and January is when the stack effect is pulling hardest. If the number comes back above 4, get a licensed mitigator. If it lands between 2 and 4, do the math on a sub-slab system anyway, because the action level is a policy line, not a medical one.

And if your jurisdiction still uses the county zone map to decide whether radon-resistant construction is worth requiring: the model trained on six million tests says the zone you trust most is the one failing the most people. Zones average. Radon concentrates. Your codebook can keep pretending otherwise; your slab will not.
