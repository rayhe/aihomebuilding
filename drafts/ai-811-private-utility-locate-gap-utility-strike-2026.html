You Called 811 Before You Dug. The Line You Hit Was Never on Their Map.

By Frank DeLuca · October 4, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Saturday, 9:40 a.m. A two-man fence crew is setting posts along the back property line in San Jose. She did everything right. Called 811 on Wednesday, waited the two working days, and by Friday the lawn was decorated with red and yellow flags like a tiny municipal parade. First post hole, eighteen inches down, the auger finds a two-inch gas service line that no flag marked, because the line runs from the meter to the pool heater, and everything past the meter belongs to her. Work stops cold. Somebody calls the gas company. Saturday is gone, the fence is late, and the repair bill is hers.

Pacific Gas and Electric counted 1,247 of these moments across Northern and Central California in 2024 alone. In 55 percent of them, nobody called 811 at all, and for homeowners that figure climbs to 89 percent. Average repair bill: $3,500. Those are the people who skipped the free phone call. This article is about the other group, the ones who made the call and still ended up holding the shovel when something went bang.

What 811 Actually Does

The 811 system is simple and it works as advertised. You call, or file online, at least two working days before you dig. Behind that call, a regional center notifies every member utility with facilities near your dig area. Each one sends a locator who marks what that utility owns, in the standard colors, with paint and flags. It costs you nothing; the utilities fund it through the rates you already pay. In California, Government Code 4216 makes the call mandatory for anyone disturbing soil with power equipment, homeowner included, and digging without a ticket leaves you liable for the repair, civil penalties, and any resulting injury. "I didn't know" is not a defense the statute recognizes.

Here is the part the public-service ads skip. Each locator marks only what their utility owns, up to the meter or the demarcation point. Your property is full of lines nobody owns but you: the electric run from the house to the detached garage or the ADU, the gas line from the meter to the barbecue, the fire pit, the pool heater, the water lines past the meter feeding the irrigation grid and the hose bibs, the private sewer lateral on your side of the property line, the septic field, the landscape lighting. None of it gets a flag. A private-locating trade group puts the private share at roughly 60 percent of buried infrastructure. Sixty percent of what sits under your lawn is invisible to the system everyone tells you to call.

Even the Marks Can Lie

Suppose you absorb that, hire a private locator, and get the full map. You are still not done, because the Common Ground Alliance's 2025 damage data shows that calling 811 is necessary but not sufficient in a second way. Of the 221,717 damage reports in last year's DIRT database, the single biggest root cause was no notification at all, 22.3 percent, the skip-the-call crowd. But add up the next tier: the facility was not marked because the locator made an error, 14.2 percent; it was marked inaccurately because the locator made an error, 9.2 percent. Nearly a quarter of all strikes happen after somebody did the paperwork and the marks were simply wrong.

Then there is the human factor after the marks go down. Excavators who failed to maintain clearance after verifying the marks: 14.8 percent. Crews that dug before verifying marks by potholing: 7.2 percent. So the system fails at the notification step, at the locating step, and at the excavation step, in roughly equal measure, and a process with three failure modes needs three countermeasures.

California writes one of those countermeasures into law: a 24-inch tolerance zone on each side of every mark, inside which the excavator must determine the exact location of the line with hand tools before any power equipment or boring goes near it. Vacuum excavation is allowed if the operator agrees. Every line is presumed active. Hit a gas or hazardous-liquid line and the statute says to call 911, not the office.

A Record High, Priced in Billions

National numbers explain why this stopped being a safety-poster topic and became a boardroom one. CGA's 2025 DIRT summary put its damage index at 102, a record high against the 2022 baseline of 100. Telecom and cable take 51 percent of strikes, natural gas 36 percent, electric 8 percent, water and sewer the rest. And the companion cost study, "Between the Lines," modeled the true annual cost at $83.2 billion, nearly triple the 2019 benchmark, with one detail that belongs on every project manager's wall: direct repair is only 5.9 percent of the total. For every dollar spent patching the pipe, about sixteen dollars evaporate into business interruption, emergency response, property damage, and schedule carnage.

New Machines in the Dirt

The private-locate business used to be a technician with an electromagnetic wand and a can of paint, and for a simple residential lot that is still mostly what it is. A wand finds conductive lines; ground-penetrating radar finds the rest, plastic included, at a higher price. Residential flat rates run $250 to $500 for a basic scan, $150 to $300 an hour for complex work, and the Alliance's own math says every dollar spent on prevention saves $4.62.

Venture money sees a different market. Exodigo, an Israeli-American startup, runs a sensor cart carrying GPR, magnetometers, and electromagnetic sensors together, then fuses the streams with AI models trained on what the company says is 350 terabytes of geophysical data, producing a 3D BIM-compliant map with centimeter accuracy. It claims 99 percent detection against a traditional miss rate of up to half of all utilities, raised a $96 million Series B in 2025, and counts Amtrak, California High-Speed Rail, and LA Metro as clients.

Treat the 99 percent the way you would treat any number on a pitch deck. It is the company's figure, independently unverified as far as I can find, and the service is priced for infrastructure agencies, not for the pool contractor. Texas put Exodigo on a statewide purchasing contract, which tells you where this technology lives for now. For a residential GC, the realistic tool remains a competent private locator with EM and GPR, hired the way you would hire any sub: on reputation, equipment, and insurance, not on price alone.

What I Did Not Prove

The $4.62 return is CGA's modeled figure, not a controlled experiment, and the private-locate price ranges are regional averages; your market will differ, so get two quotes. CGA's damage index is a modeled trend measure, not a raw count of strikes, and the Alliance itself warns against converting it into a national total. I did not independently verify Exodigo's accuracy claims, and nobody outside the company has published a test I could find. PG&E's numbers cover Northern and Central California only. And no scan, however sophisticated, replaces the shovel test: potholing to daylight the line is still the only confirmation that counts, which is why the law requires it.

Three Layers Between You and a Strike

After twenty years of watching schedules die in holes in the ground, here is the process I would make standard on any residential project that breaks soil. Layer one is free: call 811 at least two working days out, white-line your dig area first so the locators mark the right patch, and photograph every mark the day they go down, because paint fades and flags walk away. Layer two costs $250 to $500: hire a private locator for everything past the meter, the ADU feed, the pool gas, the irrigation, the septic, and get the marks in writing with a sketch you keep in the job file. At PG&E's $3,500 average repair bill, one avoided strike pays for seven scans, before the sixteen-to-one downstream multiplier. Layer three is discipline: hand-expose inside the 24-inch tolerance zone, pothole to verify depth and alignment before the excavator gets within reach, and re-verify if the marks are more than a few weeks old or the schedule slipped. Nobody on my crew runs power equipment inside a tolerance zone on paint alone, and neither should yours.

Industry data says damage is at a record high while construction accelerates into grid modernization, broadband, and housing. That hole in the ground is the last unmapped part of the project, and mapping it is the cheapest insurance on the job. Call the free number. Pay for the private scan. Dig like the map might be wrong, because a quarter of the time, it is.
