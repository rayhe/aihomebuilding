Your Insurer's AI Watched Your Yard From Space. It Raised Your Risk 71%.

By Catherine Chen · October 6, 2026 · 9 min read

California's first-in-the-nation law requires insurers to discount wildfire mitigation. The state's second-largest insurer pays $42 to $75 a year for it. The five-foot ember zone alone costs $2,780. The arithmetic, done honestly.

Sometime last year, a satellite took a fresh photograph of your roof, and by the time fire season arrived, that photograph had already been converted into a number that follows your premium the way a credit score follows a loan application, except nobody mailed you the free annual copy. An algorithm measured the distance between your eaves and the nearest manzanita, checked whether your vents were meshed, and estimated how fast a fire could move across your slope. Then it updated your number. For 52 percent of California homeowners, the number got worse, not because they did anything wrong but because the hillside did.

That is the quiet part of the wildfire insurance story nobody put in the brochure. Your premium is now set, in part, by a machine that re-reads your house from orbit every year, and the photograph it took last spring already contains the vegetation measurements, roof-material classification, and defensible-space geometry that will surface as a line item the next time your carrier files rates. And the law that was supposed to reward you for fighting back pays out in sums that would embarrass a coupon clipper. That was the pitch.

Start with the machine: ZestyAI's Z-FIRE model, the one California's Department of Insurance approved for both underwriting and rating, is trained on more than 1,400 wildfire events and two decades of loss data. It ingests annual high-resolution satellite imagery and scores each property from 1 to 10 on two scales: wildfire hazard, and the likelihood that your specific house becomes a total loss, using the same computer-vision techniques that tag faces in photos, except here the tag is a number that decides whether you can buy insurance. Farmers, California's second-largest home insurer, adopted it and committed to writing at least 30,000 new policies for higher-risk homeowners on the strength of it, and Amica uses it too. Overhanging vegetation is visible to the model. So is your roof material, and it prices accordingly. Every year.

In January, ZestyAI published what its model found across 11,808,251 California properties. Just over half saw their regional wildfire hazard worsen between 2022 and 2025, with scores climbing an average of 1.3 points. Here is the sentence that should have made every headline: a single-point increase represents a 51 percent jump in annual wildfire probability. Compound the average increase and you get 1.51 raised to the 1.3 power, which lands at roughly 1.71. Translation: the typical worsened home's modeled annual wildfire probability rose about 71 percent in three years. Read that again: not 71 percent of homes, but a 71 percent increase in the modeled chance of fire, per home, for the homes that worsened. Meanwhile the federal government's competing yardstick, FEMA's National Risk Index, barely moved over the same period. FEMA scores census tracts from historical perimeters while Z-FIRE reads your actual roof. You have never seen yours. Nearly $1 trillion in California homes sit in the gap: labeled low risk by FEMA, flagged as elevated by the AI. At the state's $800,000 median home price, that is on the order of 1.25 million houses carrying a risk label their owners have never seen.

"It's all about the embers and making sure they have nothing combustible to land on." — Daniel Gorham, P.E., IBHS wildfire researcher

Now the law. In October 2022, Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara finalized the Safer from Wildfires regulation, the first of its kind in the country, making California the test case for a question no other state had been willing to put to its insurers: what is a mandated discount worth when the science behind each discounted measure is still being written? Every admitted carrier in California had 180 days to rewrite its rating plans around mitigation credits, and to hand each policyholder their wildfire risk determination in writing. Eleven or twelve protective measures, depending on whose telling you trust, from cleared gutters to fire-resistant roofs to community-level Firewise recognition, each one now legally required to earn you a discount, whether the actuarial evidence for each specific measure exists yet or not. Simple pitch, politically irresistible: do the work, get rewarded.

The arithmetic declined to cooperate, because when Lara proposed the rule, his department estimated the average consumer would save about $100 a year, roughly 8 percent of a typical premium. A leaner story emerged from the filings. The Sacramento Bee obtained Farmers' numbers, and what they actually produced was thinner than the pitch: the average policyholder in a high-fire-risk area would save $42 to $75 a year for taking the specified mitigation steps, a figure the Bee pulled from filings the company would presumably have preferred to keep in the actuarial appendix. One Nevada City homeowner told the Bee he pays $7,200 a year for his Farmers policy. His reward for fireproofing lands somewhere between a nice dinner and a tank of gas. Consumer Watchdog's Harvey Rosenfield called it a heads-the-insurer-wins, tails-the-insurer-loses deal, and the numbers make his case uncomfortably easy.

Set those two facts side by side and do the division nobody in Sacramento did on camera, dividing the retrofit cost by the annual discount. The Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety puts the cost of the single highest-leverage retrofit, the zero-to-five-foot noncombustible zone around the house plus enclosing the area under the deck so embers cannot drift beneath it, at roughly $2,780. At $75 a year, the payback is 37 years, and at $42, it is 66. Read it twice. The discount does not finance the mitigation; it does not come close. A homeowner who spends the money because the state promised a reward will be retired before the reward covers the invoice. Thirty-seven to sixty-six years.

This is the part where the regulation's defenders will object, and one of their objections deserves a fair hearing before we go further. The small discounts may be actuarially honest. Independent research confirms that a full suite of mitigation steps meaningfully reduces risk, but nobody has published data on how much one or two steps, alone, reduce actual claim costs. Washington state's insurance office is waiting for exactly that data before it copies California's mandate. If the marginal loss reduction from a cleared five-foot zone is genuinely modest in the actuarial tables, then $42 to $75 is fair pricing, not theft. Pricing the discount above the evidence would just move the cost onto other policyholders. The code does not care about your feelings, and neither do loss ratios. Feelings are not a rating factor. It is not personal.

Fair enough. Now the part the defenders cannot wave away: the same AI granularity that justifies small discounts also lets carriers sort risk with a precision the old maps never allowed. That granularity cuts both ways, because Farmers' 30,000 new policies are real coverage in places the market had abandoned, and that matters in a state where tens of thousands of homeowners have been shoved onto the FAIR Plan at double or triple the premium. But a model that reprices your home every year from satellite photos can also punish you for things you never did: vegetation regrowing on the slope behind you, a neighbor's unmaintained lot, a regional hazard trend that moved 52 percent of the state the wrong way while owners stood still, and none of those inputs comes with a comment box where you can explain that the manzanita is on the parcel you do not own. The department's own deputy commissioner told the Wall Street Journal the state is watching for algorithmic bias. There is, as of this writing, no clear public standard for how a homeowner disputes a score derived from a photograph taken 400 miles up.

None of this means the mitigation is pointless. It means the reason to do it was never the discount. IBHS research puts the figure every homeowner in the wildland-urban interface should memorize at up to 90 percent: that is the share of homes damaged or destroyed in wildfires that were first ignited by embers or ember-set fires, not by the flame front. Embers ride the wind for miles, slip through an unmeshed vent, land in a gutter full of pine needles, and start the house burning from the inside while the fire line is still on the next ridge. In IBHS's 2019 duplex demonstration, engineers built one house ember-resistant and one house typical, then attacked both with a simulated ember storm, and only the typical side ignited. And if a home does ignite, IBHS puts the chance of total loss above 90 percent. Multiply the two nineties together and the ember pathway accounts for something like four-fifths of all wildfire total losses, which is why the cheapest line item on the mitigation menu keeps appearing at the top of the effectiveness list. The five-foot zone is not landscaping advice; it is the cheapest available intervention against the dominant ignition mechanism in American wildfire.

Which brings us to the calculation the industry will not print on a mailer. The same IBHS and Headwaters Economics research that priced the retrofit found something remarkable about new construction: building a home to wildfire standards from the first sketch costs roughly the same as building it vulnerable. Class A roof, ember-resistant vents, noncombustible five-foot zone, six inches of noncombustible material at the base of the walls: at plan stage, these are specification choices rather than change orders, which is why IBHS found the new-construction premium negligible. The National Association of Home Builders priced full wildfire-standard construction between $1,827 and $44,888 depending mostly on siding choice, and the low end of that range is a rounding error on a build budget.

So here is the honest version of the incentive, stripped of the regulatory theater. If you are buying or building in fire country, the money belongs in the purchase decision, not in a retrofit chased for a $42-a-year coupon, so specify the hardened house. No exceptions. Ask the builder for the wildfire specification sheet the way you would ask for the insulation R-values, and treat a blank stare as a pricing signal rather than a personality quirk. Under Lara's regulation you have the legal right to see your wildfire risk determination in writing; request it, find out which model produced it, and ask what specifically the model penalized, because a score you cannot see is a bill you cannot dispute, and the transparency provision is the one part of the regulation that costs you nothing to use. If you already own the house, do the five-foot zone anyway, and do it for the reason IBHS demonstrated in a South Carolina test chamber: embers cause up to 90 percent of ignitions, and nothing combustible within five feet of your walls means the embers have nowhere to land.

Colorado just made the same bet California did, wagering that a statutory discount mandate can succeed where actuarial caution says the per-measure evidence is not there yet. Its HB 25-1182, effective this July, requires insurers to credit documented mitigation, and IBHS opened its Wildfire Prepared Home designation to Colorado homeowners in April: $125 application fee, a Base level achievable by retrofit that targets exactly the ember pathway, a Plus level for radiant heat and flame contact during new construction or major renovation. The laboratory is expanding, so watch whether Colorado's discounts land any closer to the cost of the work than California's did.

What to do Monday morning

If you are buying in the wildland-urban interface, ask for the home's wildfire risk score and the model behind it before you close, the same way you would demand the natural-hazard disclosure. California law entitles you to the determination; a seller who cannot produce it is telling you something. Price the hardened house against the vulnerable one knowing the insurance discount will not close the gap for you, which means the gap has to be closed by the build specification itself.

If you are building, put the ember package in the plans: Class A roof, 1/8-inch corrosion-resistant mesh on every vent, noncombustible gutters, the zero-to-five-foot zone in gravel or stone, six inches of noncombustible base at the walls. Your architect's shrug is the most expensive line item on the sheet.

If you already own, clear the five feet. Clean the gutters. Mesh the vents. Do it this month, before the Santa Anas, and do not wait for the discount letter to motivate you. The letter is worth $42 to $75; the house is worth everything.

Why this article could be wrong

The discounts are small because the evidence is thin, and California may have regulated ahead of the science. No published study isolates what one or two mitigation steps do to claim costs; the industry opposed the 2022 mandate on exactly those grounds, and other states have declined to follow. The AI scoring that looks punitive is also what let Farmers write 30,000 policies in places carriers had fled, which is the difference between having insurance and having the FAIR Plan. And none of the score-worsening documented in the Z-FIRE report is any individual homeowner's fault: regional hazard moved faster than any household could mitigate. A regulation that rewards individual action cannot fix a regional trend, and grading homeowners on satellite photos of slopes they do not own raises fairness questions the current rules do not answer, which is why the deputy commissioner's bias warning matters more than the discount schedule: the model is mandatory, and the appeal process is not. Regional trend. Individual bill. Again next year.

What this analysis did not prove

The Z-FIRE figures come from a vendor-published January 2026 report; the 11.8-million-property analysis and the FEMA comparison are ZestyAI's methodology, not an independent audit. Farmers' $42 to $75 discount range comes from filings as reported by the Sacramento Bee in 2024; other carriers' filings differ and have evolved since. IBHS's $2,780 ember-zone figure and the $18,180 to $27,080 optimum-materials range come from a 2018 study; inflation and regional labor spreads apply. The 71 percent compounding calculation assumes the reported 51-percent-per-point risk relationship is multiplicative, which the report's phrasing supports but the proprietary model does not confirm in public documentation. No published research apportions loss reduction to individual mitigation measures, so the payback arithmetic treats the discount as the only financial return, which understates the value of the loss avoided. Treat the table as a reasoning tool, not an actuarial filing.

Start now. The satellite passes overhead again next year, so make sure it photographs a different house.
