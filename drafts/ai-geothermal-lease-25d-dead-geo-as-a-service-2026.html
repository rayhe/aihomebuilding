By Priya Greenwood · September 11, 2026

The 30% Geothermal Tax Credit Died on New Year's Eve. The Lease That Replaced It Costs Builders Nothing Upfront.

Picture two houses on the same street, finished by the same driller, heated by identical four-ton geothermal systems, and separated by the single most expensive thirteen days in residential tax-credit history. Her system was placed in service on December 30, 2025. She claimed a 30% federal tax credit worth roughly $8,400 and told everyone at the block party, because in December 2025 that credit was the single best deal in residential energy. His went live on January 12, 2026. He got nothing: same heat pump, same driller, same dirt, no credit.

Same dirt, same pipes, same heat pump, thirteen days apart, and one of them left $8,400 on the table that no longer exists.

That is what the One Big Beautiful Bill Act did to residential geothermal when it terminated the Section 25D residential clean energy credit for any expenditure made after December 31, 2025, with the IRS reading "expenditure made" as the day installation is completed rather than the day the contract was signed or the deposit was paid. A system finished in 2026, even one contracted in 2025, does not qualify. Contracts don't count. Congressional researchers spelled it out plainly: start in 2025, finish in 2026, and the credit is gone.

Most builders read that headline, crossed geothermal off their 2026 spec sheets, and went back to gas furnaces, a decision that looked prudent in January and looks expensive now that the replacement mechanism is public. That was the rational move with the information they had, and it was also wrong, because the credit did not exactly die so much as relocate from the homeowner's tax return to a financier's balance sheet.

What survived

While 25D was being terminated, the commercial Investment Tax Credit under Section 48 survived for geothermal, and federal guidance now describes a 6% base credit with bonus adders that can stack to 30% for projects meeting prevailing-wage, domestic-content, or energy-community requirements, which homeowners cannot claim but companies can.

That single asymmetry is the entire business model behind the most interesting announcement in residential geothermal this year. On March 10, 2026, Dandelion Energy, the Google X spinout backed by Breakthrough Energy Ventures, launched Geo-as-a-Service: a national program that installs geothermal heating and cooling in new homes at zero upfront cost to the builder, in partnership with Diverso Energy, a geothermal services firm majority-owned by CVC DIF, and the structure is third-party ownership, which is the detail that makes the tax math work. Dandelion or its financing partner owns the ground loop and the heat pump, monetizes the 48 ITC and its bonus adders, and the builder gets what the company's fall 2025 leasing pilot already promised: geothermal installed for less than the cost of a standard HVAC system.

This was not possible a year ago for a boring legal reason: under the old tax code, leased geothermal equipment counted as "limited-use property," which meant the commercial owner could not repossess it if the leaseholder stopped paying, and that single clause broke the leasing math entirely. A trade group, the Geothermal Exchange Organization, lobbied a fix into the same bill that killed 25D. Canary Media reported that tax attorneys at firms like Norton Rose Fulbright are now packaging residential heat pumps for tax-equity investors the way the solar industry packaged rooftop panels a decade ago, which tells you who thinks this market is real: not bloggers, but the tax-equity desks that only move when the paperwork pencils. Roughly half of residential rooftop solar in the U.S. was sold through third-party leases and power purchase agreements, and geothermal is now walking the same path about ten years late, with the same financiers circling.

Three scenarios

Run the numbers for a 2,400-square-foot new build in a mixed climate, because this is where the story stops being about tax law and starts being about your utility bill.

A four-ton geothermal system, vertical loop on a tight lot, runs about $28,000 installed. Industry guides put the per-ton installed cost at $4,000 to $8,000, and the heat pump unit itself is only 20 to 40 percent of the total, which means the box in your utility room is the cheapest part of the most expensive HVAC option on the market. Everything else is the hole in the ground.

The conventional alternative? A high-efficiency air-source system with a furnace, roughly $13,500 to the builder. In 2025, the homeowner claimed 30% of $28,000 under 25D, or $8,400, bringing the net cost to $19,600, a premium of about $6,100 over conventional, and at that premium the only question was how fast the ground would pay you back. Geothermal equipment runs at 300 to 600 percent efficiency, per the Department of Energy's consumer guide, and Dandelion's field data shows heating and cooling bills dropping more than 60%. On a home spending roughly $2,500 a year on heating and cooling, that is around $1,500 a year back in your pocket, which puts payback on the premium at about four years, after which the ground is effectively paying you.

In 2026, buying the system outright, the premium is the full $14,500, payback stretches past nine years, and the sales conversation ends before it starts. Almost nobody signs that.

In 2026, through a third-party-ownership lease, the builder's installed cost drops to at or below conventional HVAC, because the financier rather than the builder is the one harvesting the tax credit. That tax value, 30% plus a domestic-content adder on Dandelion's U.S.-built equipment, flows to the system owner, not the homeowner. In exchange, the homeowner gets the most efficient heating and cooling equipment commercially available and a lease payment engineered to sit below the old utility bill, which is the entire pitch: premium equipment, no premium outlay, monthly math that works from day one.

Read that again. And the homeowner who lost the $8,400 credit may still end up paying less per month than the neighbor who got it, because the credit moved from the buyer to the balance sheet and the monthly math barely noticed.

Where the money is actually buried

Here is the part of the geothermal invoice nobody talks about at the design meeting: drilling and excavation eat 35 to 50 percent of the total system cost, which on a $28,000 system means $10,000 to $14,000 of hole before a single watt of heating is delivered. Loop fields get sized by rules of thumb padded with safety factors, because no driller ever got fired for drilling too much hole, and that padding hides inside a line item the homeowner never sees. A 20 percent oversize from conservative sizing wastes $2,000 to $2,800 per home, money that buys nothing except the driller's peace of mind. Across a 50-home development, that is $100,000 to $140,000 of unnecessary drilling, which is why the loop field, not the heat pump, is where geothermal projects are won or lost.

This is where the AI story actually lives, far from the robot fantasies, in the unglamorous arithmetic of borefield design. It is math eating margin. An open-source package called Geoloop, published this year, models borehole heat exchanger performance with depth-dependent thermal properties and runs stochastic sensitivity analysis so designers can optimize a field instead of padding it. Researchers applying machine-learning-driven planning to a low-enthalpy geothermal case in Austria reported a 27% increase in expected reward and a 35% cut in data-acquisition costs versus conventional planning. With DOE funding, the Energy Center of Wisconsin built a free modeling tool for hybrid geothermal systems that deliberately shrink the ground loop by pairing it with a smaller supplemental system, cutting the most expensive component on the invoice.

None of these tools replace the cheapest accuracy in the business: a thermal conductivity test of the actual soil, which runs $600 to $2,000, and one test can eliminate a borehole, paying for itself the first time it does.

For a production builder, the playbook writes itself: model the loop field instead of guessing it, test the soil instead of assuming it, and consider a hybrid design that trims peak loop capacity. Then finance the whole thing through a third-party structure that captures the credit your buyer no longer can, because the cheapest kilowatt-hour is the one someone else's tax equity paid for. Each step is unglamorous, and together they are the difference between geothermal as a luxury upgrade and geothermal as the default spec.

Why you might still say no

Now the part the leasing brochures leave out, starting with the bluntest one: a lease is not ownership. Under a lease, the homeowner does not get the tax credit, does not own the equipment, and lives with whatever escalator clause the financier wrote into year seven. Anyone signing a geothermal lease should read the escalator the way they would read an adjustable-rate mortgage disclosure, because it rhymes.

Geology does not negotiate: hit solid rock and drilling costs explode, while a high water table or contaminated soils can trigger environmental permits that add months and thousands of dollars before the rig even arrives. Have a small infill lot and a horizontal loop is fantasy; vertical drilling on a tight lot in a dense suburb can cost double the brochure number. No model, however elegant, fixes bad ground, and every savings figure in this article assumes the driller's test bore came back friendly.

Then there are the transaction costs, which are real: stacking 48 ITC bonuses means documenting prevailing wages, domestic content, and energy-community eligibility. That paperwork is a rounding error for a production builder doing 500 homes. For a custom builder doing four? A dealbreaker. This is a production-housing game, and the custom market will keep buying furnaces.

And the modeling tools are only as honest as their inputs, because Geoloop is open source and auditable, which is genuinely good, but a conductivity model fed with assumed soil data produces a precisely wrong borefield. Garbage in, gospel out, hole in the ground.

What this analysis does not prove

The cost figures here are midpoints from industry pricing guides, not bid data, and regional drilling rates vary by a factor of two or three. Dandelion's per-home lease economics come from company announcements; I found no independent audit of what builders actually pay under Geo-as-a-Service. Those energy savings assume a mixed-climate home spending roughly $2,500 a year on heating and cooling; mild climates save less, cold climates save more. Treat the tax discussion here as informational rather than advisory: whether a specific project qualifies for the 48 ITC and its bonus adders turns on facts only a tax professional should bless. Note also that the 45L new-energy-efficient-home credit died for homes acquired after June 30, 2026, so production builders lost on two fronts this year and the lease structure only repairs one of them.

What to ask before anyone drills

If you are building or buying new construction in 2026, four questions will save you more money than any brochure, and the first one determines whether your builder is working from this year's playbook or last year's obituary.

First, ask your builder whether they have priced a third-party-owned geothermal option, not just a purchased system. If they tell you the credit is dead and geothermal is over? They are working from 2025 information. Forget the residential credit; the commercial credit is financing your neighbor's system.

Second, ask who modeled the loop field and with what, because "our driller has twenty years of experience" is not a model, and a conductivity test plus a modeled field is the minimum acceptable answer.

Third, if there is a lease, ask for the escalator schedule in writing and compare the year-ten lease payment against your projected utility bill, not your current one.

Fourth, remember that new construction is the one moment this is cheap, because trenching a loop field while the excavator is already on site for the foundation costs a fraction of drilling it under a finished lawn later. Retrofitting geothermal is a renovation project, while specifying it at framing is a line item.

Two houses, same street, thirteen days apart: one got the last of the old credit, and the other might get the first of the new math, and the only question that matters is which house your builder is building. Ask which one your builder is offering before the foundation is poured.
