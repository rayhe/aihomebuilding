Recycling Your Solar Array Costs 23 Times More Than Landfilling It. That's the Whole Problem.

By Priya Greenwood · October 4, 2026

Last spring a homeowner outside Phoenix got a quote to take down the solar array her contractor had installed in 2014. Twenty-two panels, half of them storm-cracked, the rest limping at degraded output. Removal and hauling: $1,900. Then came the second number, the one that stopped the conversation cold: recycling would add roughly $28 a panel on top, close to $600 total, while the landfill across town would take the whole stack for what amounted to the price of lunch. She asked the contractor which one most people choose. He laughed. Almost nobody asks.

That ratio, repeated at every kitchen table and every utility-scale repower in the country, is the entire story of solar panel recycling in America. Landfilling a crystalline-silicon module costs somewhere between $1 and $5. Recycling it costs $15 to $45. Faced with that spread, American panel owners do the obvious thing, which is why fewer than one in ten decommissioned panels in the United States gets recycled, according to the National Renewable Energy Laboratory.

I ran the math for the most ordinary object in the whole system: a 20-panel residential array, the kind installed by the hundreds of thousands during the 2010s boom. Twenty panels at about 20 kilograms apiece is 400 kilograms, just under half a ton. NREL's modeled average recycling cost of $28 per module puts the decommissioning bill at $560. Landfill tipping fees run roughly $55 a ton, so the same array costs about $24 to bury. Twenty-three to one. Every time someone declares solar recycling a technology problem, that number is standing right behind them, quietly explaining that it is not.

The Wave Is Not Coming. It Is Unloading.

Panels sold in the boom years carried 25- to 30-year performance warranties, and those warranties now function as the industry's calendar: the big 2010s residential cohort should be retiring in waves starting around 2035. Except the calendar is wrong. Field data from recyclers shows panels reaching end of life much faster than the warranty sheets suggest, closer to 11 years on average, and roughly 65% of what arrives is construction or operations breakage, not graceful old age (Solar Power World, December 2025). Hailstorms flatten entire arrays in an afternoon. Repowering projects yank working 200-watt panels to make room for 600- and 700-watt replacements that harvest more power from the same racking. NREL's Garvin Heath puts it plainly: installations from two decades ago are nearing end of life, and roughly a million tons of US panel waste is expected by 2030.

Globally the curve is steeper than the American one. IRENA's 2026 update projects the cumulative weight of retired panels passing 12 million tonnes by 2035 and exceeding 200 million tonnes by 2050 under a 1.5-degree pathway, with annual volumes topping 3 million tonnes by 2035 and 25 million by 2050, a sixteen-fold increase in cumulative weight between those two dates that the industry's recycling capacity is nowhere near ready to absorb. Today solar panel waste is less than 1% of global electronic waste by weight. By 2050 IRENA expects it to exceed 21%. Meanwhile the United States keeps adding capacity: utility-scale solar is set to add 70 gigawatts across 2026 and 2027 alone. Every panel installed is a panel that will one day retire, and the industry has been treating retirement like a problem for the future tense. Its trucks are already loading.

What the Robots Are Actually Good At

A retired panel is not a mysterious object. Roughly three quarters of a crystalline-silicon panel by weight is glass, with an aluminum frame, a junction box, silicon cells, and small amounts of copper and silver threaded through it. Strip the numbers: the parts worth money are the hardest to get at, while the part that is easy to handle, the glass, is worth almost nothing on its own. Pulling a frame off by hand, locating the junction box, separating the laminate without shredding the valuable layers: that is skilled, slow, physical work, and at loaded labor costs of $15 to $25 an hour, manual disassembly is simply unsustainable at the scale the waste numbers demand.

This is where the automation story is genuinely interesting, and where it deserves credit rather than the usual press-release treatment. At OnePlanet's River City facility, vision-guided robots handle deframing, conveyance, and processing, with AI-optimized parameters that adapt in real time to each panel's characteristics. Initial capacity is about two million panels a year, scaling toward six million. At SolarPanelRecycling.com's Texas plant, incoming panels are read by AI for depth and width in a 3D-mapping step against the company's growing module database, which locates the junction box so a robot can remove it with precision (Solar Power World). A startup's PV Circulator line goes further: AI vision identifies each panel's make and model from a database, recognizes component form factors, and adjusts the process automatically, with the machines sharing learned optimizations over a neural network so no two lines have to learn the same panel twice.

The machines exist and separation works, but money doesn't move. Some operators claim 94 to 96% material recovery, including the glass, the aluminum, the copper, and crucially the silicon and silver. Nobody in the industry seriously argues the technology is the bottleneck. Almost every serious analysis reaches the same conclusion instead: the economics are. A robot that disassembles faster does not close a 23-to-1 cost gap by itself. It narrows the labor line item, which is one line item, on a spreadsheet where the real problem is the price of throwing the thing away.

NREL's Honest Model

In 2021 NREL ran the numbers without romance, and they still stand as the clearest picture of what the market does when nobody intervenes (pv magazine, September 2021). Projecting 500 gigawatts of US installed capacity by 2050, the model generated 9.1 million metric tons of PV waste. Under baseline conditions, through 2050: 80% landfilled, 1% reused, 10% recycled. Used modules could be resold at 36% of new prices, which sounds encouraging until you notice it barely moves the needle, because the resale market for working used panels is real but thin.

The model also found the levers that actually move the needle, and neither of them is a smarter robot: cutting the recycling cost from $28 to $18 per module could raise the recycling rate by more than one-third by 2050, while recovering silver, copper, and silicon at genuine semiconductor-grade purity could generate a cumulative recycler net income of $1.3 billion over the same period, which is the difference between an industry and a charity case. EDF Renewables' 2024 white paper frames the prize: the market value of recycled end-of-life PV materials is expected to grow to $15 billion by 2050, from under $1 billion in 2024. SolarCycle's CEO, who launched the company charging $18 a panel including shipping back in 2022, has named the target publicly: landfill parity, the point where recycling a panel costs no more than burying it, by the end of this decade (pv magazine USA, October 2025). Watch that number. If it holds, my $560 homeowner bill collapses toward $24, and the whole argument of this article becomes history.

The Silver Thread

Buried inside the waste numbers is a materials story that reframes the whole problem. The solar industry consumed roughly 6,000 tonnes of silver in 2023, against global mine production of about 30,000 tonnes that year (Intelligent Living, September 2026). Engineers have thrifting silver out of cells for two decades: usage fell from around 200 tonnes per gigawatt-peak in 2006 to under 30 tonnes today, but deployment grew faster than the thrifting, which is how you end up burning a fifth of the world's mined silver to make things that harvest sunlight. Yansong Shen, who directs the ARC Research Hub for Photovoltaic Reliability and Sustainability at the University of New South Wales, has warned that without continued thrifting, copper substitution, and large-scale recycling, most currently known silver reserves could be consumed within 25 years.

Several of the metals inside a panel sit on the US critical-minerals list and are largely imported, which is why Garvin Heath keeps making the supply-chain argument: recovered silver, copper, and silicon are domestic material that did not have to be dug up or shipped in. China produces about 80% of the world's polysilicon. Decommissioned American panels contain substantial quantities of it, sitting in piles, waiting for someone to decide the material is worth more than the burial fee. This is the one place in the recycling spreadsheet with a genuine tailwind. Commodity prices reward the recycler. The tipping fee doesn't care. It never does.

Nobody Owns the Afterlife

If the economics are upside down, the policy landscape is a shrug. EPA's own page reads like an inventory of good intentions: California and Hawaii have formally reclassified panels as universal waste, giving them a clear collection path; New Jersey set up a recycling commission; North Carolina published a decommissioning report. Washington State passed the only solar-specific extended producer responsibility law in 2017, requiring manufacturers to fund takeback, and the industry's backlash was so fierce that enforcement was delayed to 2030. New Jersey passed mandatory recycling legislation in January 2026 and deliberately stopped short of producer responsibility, which means the cost stays with the consumer. There is no federal framework, and the EPA's effort to reclassify end-of-life panels as universal waste has slipped past its original target date.

Europe shows what the alternative looks like, and it is not a technology story at all. Panels have fallen under the EU's Waste Electrical and Electronic Equipment directive since 2012, with member states required to recover 85% and recycle 80% of panels since 2018; untreated panels are banned from landfills. Industry groups like PV Cycle manage the takeback, funded by an upfront manufacturer fee, 70 cents a panel, which is the entire trick: when the recycling cost is built into the purchase price, the same object that looks like expensive waste in one system looks like feedstock in another. France's nonprofit SOREN charges manufacturers about 1 euro per module through an ecoparticipation scheme. Eighteen European countries collected 48,395 tonnes of module waste in 2022. The infrastructure exists because the law made it exist, not because the robots got clever.

Jigar Shah, the Generate Capital co-founder, made the political point with unusual bluntness: it is far more cost effective for manufacturers to be forced to work together and own recycling facilities jointly, and that happens through policy, not through people opting in. In the US the policy has not happened. Under the Trump administration, federal solar-waste rules have had little appetite behind them, with the environmental agenda running toward deregulation instead. So recyclers are left lobbying states for the bluntest instrument available: landfill bans, which would forbid throwing expired panels away and create the stable demand their plants need. When disposal is cheap and legal, recovered material struggles to compete with it. That sentence is the whole American story in one line.

Objections, at Full Strength

All of which risks making a dead panel sound like a dead battery, and the honest accounting says it mostly is not. Crystalline-silicon panels generally pass TCLP toxicity tests, which means a panel in a sanitary landfill is not leaching the poisons the hazard framing implies. The First Solar counterexample matters too: its cadmium-telluride modules already run a closed-loop takeback program, proving that extended producer responsibility works when a manufacturer decides to build it. And a meaningful share of the supposed waste wave is not waste at all in the moral sense: repowering projects discard working panels for efficiency gains, swapping 200-watt modules for 600-watt ones, which is a consumption choice wearing the costume of an end-of-life event.

The deeper objection is about blame. Nobody who bought a 2014 rooftop array was warned that retirement would cost them $560, and the installers who sold those systems made their margin up front and walked away. The homeowner holds the bag because the bag was designed that way: the cost of the afterlife was never priced into the purchase, never disclosed at closing, never carried by the manufacturer who profited. Extended producer responsibility is not a radical idea here; it is the recognition that the current system is a subsidy from the future, paid in landfills, by people who were never at the table when the installer signed the quote. There is also a quieter critique worth naming: even the EU system, the world's best, runs on 1-euro fees and collection logistics, not on the AI vision lines that get the headlines. The robots are the most photogenic part of the solution and arguably the least load-bearing.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The $560 homeowner figure is a model built on NREL's $28-per-module average, which reflects utility-scale economics; residential pickup and transport are excluded, and the real homeowner bill likely lands higher, in the $300 to $900 range reported anecdotally, which means my 23-to-1 ratio is, if anything, conservative. The 11-year observed working life comes from recycler field data, not a peer-reviewed longitudinal study, and it is skewed toward panels that failed early enough to enter the waste stream. IRENA's 200-million-tonne 2050 figure assumes a 1.5-degree deployment pathway; the regular-loss and early-loss scenarios differ by about a quarter. No independent audit exists for recycler recovery claims of 94 to 96%; those are vendor statements, and vendors in an infant industry have every incentive to round up. SolarCycle's landfill-parity target is a corporate goal, not a forecast. Washington's 2030 enforcement date, New Jersey's 2026 law, and the EPA's slipped rulemaking are all moving policy targets that could shift before this article's queue date arrives.

What to Do Before the Panels Come Off

If your array is a decade or more old, start with the company that installed it. Many installers and manufacturers run take-back programs, especially for panels still under warranty, and that is the cheapest off-ramp available. Failing that, SEIA and state environmental agencies maintain directories of specialist PV processors; a growing number of general e-waste facilities now accept modules alongside other electronics, though transport is usually on you and regional prices vary sharply with distance to the nearest specialist line.

If you are budgeting: $15 to $45 a panel is the honest range. For a 20-panel array, park $300 to $900 against the day they come down, or better, negotiate end-of-life terms into your next install or repower quote while the installer still wants your business.

If you are buying panels now, ask the manufacturer what happens to them at end of life, and get the answer in the contract rather than the brochure. Manufacturers with funded takeback loops exist. They are the ones behaving as though the warranty calendar is real.

If you build homes, this is your problem too. Spec mechanical mounting that lets panels come off intact rather than being shredded with the racking. Put a decommissioning line item in new-build solar packages. It is coming out of someone's pocket eventually, and the only open question is whether that someone finds out at closing or at the curb.

The greenest energy technology of the last twenty years built its economics on a quiet fiction: that the object could be immortal. Panels die. They die earlier than the brochures say, they pile up faster than the models said, and right now the cheapest legal thing to do with a dead one is to bury it. Robots keep getting better. Silver keeps getting scarcer. Policy, in America at least, is getting nothing. Which of those three curves bends first will decide whether your grandchildren inherit a circular solar economy or just a very well-documented landfill.
