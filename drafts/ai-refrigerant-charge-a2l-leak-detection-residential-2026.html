Your New Heat Pump Ships With a Leak Sensor the Law Required. It Can Also Read Your Charge.

By Frank DeLuca · September 19, 2026

Forty minutes. That is what a proper commissioning sequence takes on a new residential heat pump: gauges on, airflow verified, superheat and subcooling logged against the manufacturer's charging chart, numbers written down. I've watched that sequence run exactly once on a home I managed; on every other job the crew fired the unit up, confirmed cold air at the register, and drove to the next one. Nobody wrote a number down. Twenty years of projects going sideways has taught me that the skipped forty minutes are always the forty minutes that matter.

It was the refrigerant charge they skipped, and the field data says it is skipped almost everywhere, which ought to bother anyone about to sign a check for a new system. More than 4,000 residential cooling systems measured in California showed 34 percent undercharged, 28 percent overcharged, and only 38 percent carrying the charge the nameplate calls for, with an undercharge of 15 percent described as common in the field data. Proctor Engineering's CheckMe! database of 13,258 systems found 57 percent needed charge repair, and the average correction was 16 ounces added to the undercharged ones. Ninety percent of those repairs brought the system within specification, so the fix is easy and the diagnosis is what nobody bothers to do, which should embarrass the trade more than it does.

Worst of all, this is not a legacy problem, and California's Refrigerant Charge and Airflow Verification Program documented 12,453 installations and found that 65 percent of existing units needed a charge adjustment, which you could blame on age, and that 45 percent of new units needed one shortly after installation, which you cannot blame on anything except the install. Under the ACCA quality-installation standard that feeds the EPA's Energy Star program, the estimate is that 62 percent of residential systems are not properly charged and that improper installation across the board cuts performance by as much as 30 percent. Your system earned its rating in a lab with a perfect charge, and it is running in your house with a 62 percent chance it never had one, which means the rating on the yellow sticker describes a machine you do not own.

Refrigerant got expensive while nobody was watching

Here is why the charge problem costs more this year than last. Under the AIM Act's HFC phasedown, bulk R-410A prices are up 40 to 70 percent since 2022, with the schedule stepping down to 40 percent of baseline production this year, 30 percent in 2028, and 20 percent by 2030. Your existing R-410A system is grandfathered and perfectly legal to service, which is the good news; the bad news is that the refrigerant costs more every year because less of it gets made, which is the whole ballgame, and it plays out on a schedule Congress already wrote into the phasedown years.

Do the arithmetic on a slow leak, because this is the original math that makes the article worth your time. Proctor's field average correction of 16 ounces per undercharged system is a fair stand-in for what a slow leak costs you per service call, and every phasedown step ratchets that number again through 2030. Put an illustrative number on the 2022 refrigerant line item, say $200, and apply the documented 40 to 70 percent increase: the same 16 ounces runs $280 to $340 today, and that is before the service call itself. Every phasedown step ratchets that number again through 2030. A system with a slow leak is now a subscription to a shrinking commodity, and most homeowners discover the subscription at the worst possible moment, when the compressor fails early because low charge raised the operating temperature and shortened its life. That failure gets priced at compressor-replacement money, not leak-repair money, which is exactly how a $340 top-off becomes a $4,000 decision.

Ask me how I know. A compressor does not die of old age at year nine; it dies of heat, and low charge is one of the quietest ways to cook one, degree by degree, summer by summer, until the protection circuit cannot save it.

Your new system already carries the sensor, courtesy of the government

Now the turn that surprised me when the research crossed my desk. Every new residential system sold since the January 2025 manufacturing cutoff runs on an A2L refrigerant, R-454B or R-32, which is mildly flammable. Because of that, equipment standards UL 60335-2-40 and UL 2683 require a refrigerant detection system that initiates shutdown within 30 seconds of a leak on systems with charge above the room limit, per ASHRAE 15 safety requirements. That detector exists to keep a flammable cloud from forming, and it is already on the truck, already wired into your condenser, already watching the one signal an AI charge diagnostic needs, which nobody priced in, least of all the manufacturers who lobbied hardest against the standard.

And the signal that sensor watches is nearly the same signal an AI charge diagnostic needs. For years, the academic literature has been building this exact tool, and Kim and Braun published a method that estimates refrigerant charge state in variable-speed heat pumps from evaporator inlet quality and compressor discharge superheat, two values derivable from temperature and pressure signals alone, with no gauge manifold in sight. A team working from ASHRAE's 1043-RP chiller fault dataset ran random-forest and gradient-boosted classifiers against six fault classes and found both models classified refrigerant leakage and overcharge faults at high accuracy, even with sparse training data. One water-to-water heat pump study got charge-fault detection within 4.5 percent in cooling mode and 1.1 percent in heating. Ding and colleagues applied back-propagation neural networks to improper charging in VRF systems back in 2021, which means the research community has been sitting on this answer for half a decade while the field kept guessing with gauges. In the laboratory, the case is closed: a handful of sensors plus the right model reads charge state without a gauge manifold.

Connect the two halves, because this is where the process guy in me gets interested: the compliance sensor is the install base, and nobody priced that in. Built into the 8 to 18 percent equipment premium for A2L systems is the detection hardware, so the diagnostic layer that rides on it costs the manufacturer a software update, not a sensor. Forty years of gauge manifolds, meet the compliance cloud, which is the only part of this story that costs the industry nothing extra. Free is rare. Take it. A contractor who shows up with a phone app reading live charge-state estimates from the mandated detection stream is not buying new tools. He is reading the tool the law made him install, which is the kind of free lunch this industry never gets. That is the convergence nobody in the trade press has written up, and it is the cheapest path from a 62 percent failure rate to something a homeowner can actually verify without learning to read a charging chart.

Skepticism, stated at full strength

None of those models ran on a real attic system. Laboratory datasets have clean sensor placement, calibrated instruments, and faults injected one at a time, which describes roughly zero residential installations, where the reality is mismatched replacement sensors, duct leaks confounding the load, and a decade of grime on the coil. Nobody has published a peer-reviewed field deployment of ML charge detection on residential systems, and I am not going to let a model score from a chiller lab carry the article. Treat the lab numbers as proof the physics works, not proof the product works. Useful as physics; vapor as a product pitch.

Two more honest caveats before the checklist, and I am not burying them, because a detector is not a preventer: the mandated sensor shuts the system down fast, but it does nothing about the loose fitting, the rubbed-through lineset, or the installer who never weighed in the charge in the first place. And charge is one fault of three, so a perfectly charged system on leaky ducts and wrong airflow still fails, which is why the ACCA bundles all three into the quality-installation spec, and no sensor on earth changes that ordering. There is also timeline noise in the refrigerant transition: the EPA's May 2026 rule eliminated the January 2026 installation deadline for pre-2025 R-410A inventory, so the stranded-equipment scarcity story moves slower than the price story.

What to actually do, by who you are

If you are buying a new system or a new house, write charge verification into the startup checklist and ask for the commissioning sheet. Keep the words simple: superheat and subcooling measured against the manufacturer's charging chart, outdoor ambient noted, airflow verified first, and a contractor who can produce that sheet on demand is in the 38 percent. If your builder's contract does not have a startup checklist line item, add one. Forty minutes of gauges costs less than one 16-ounce top-off at today's refrigerant prices, and the sheet lives in the project file next to the Manual J, where the next technician can actually find it in five years instead of starting from zero.

If you own a legacy R-410A system, run the leak math before your next service call. One top-off per year at the new pricing is the break-even signal against a repair quote for the actual leak, and two consecutive top-offs with no leak repair is the repair-vs-replace conversation, because the refrigerant keeps getting more expensive through 2030 while the compressor keeps running hotter. Ask the tech whether the leak was found and fixed, not just whether the pressures look good, because "topped off" without "leak located" is the sentence that costs you $340 a year.

If you run a residential crew, this is a process fix, not a technology purchase. Per the RCAVP data, 45 percent of new installs leave wrong, which means the fix is a weigh-in scale, a charging chart laminated to the truck door, and a policy that no system starts up without numbers on paper. An AI diagnostic layer is coming, riding on sensors the standards already require, and the contractors who will use it well are the ones who already measure; the ones who do not measure will not be saved by software, because no model can fix a crew that refuses to look.

What this analysis did not prove

The refrigerant price increase figures come from an industry compliance guide, not a commodity index, and distributor pricing varies by region and cylinder size, and there is no futures market for R-410A, so the price trajectory to 2030 is inference from the phasedown schedule, not data. Take the 16-ounce figure: it is Proctor's average correction across systems that needed repair, not a measured leak rate; a true leak-rate distribution from the field does not exist in the published literature I could find. Those ML accuracy numbers come from controlled studies, and no peer-reviewed residential field deployment exists as of this writing. And the sensor-convergence argument assumes manufacturers will expose the detection stream to diagnostic software, which none have committed to doing yet. Physics: proven. Product: not yet. Write that on the checklist too.
