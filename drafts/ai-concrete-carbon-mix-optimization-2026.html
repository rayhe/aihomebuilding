Your Foundation Poured 14 Tons of CO2 Before the Walls Existed. An Algorithm Can Erase a Third of It.

By Priya Greenwood, September 4, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

Four and a half truckloads of concrete went into a friend's foundation in San Jose this spring, roughly 45 cubic yards of a 2,000-square-foot slab-on-grade with perimeter footings, the most ordinary pour in residential construction.

Nobody at the site said the number out loud. Nobody had run it.

That pour put about 14 tons of carbon dioxide into the atmosphere before a single wall was framed.

Fourteen tons is more than the average American car emits in two and a half years of driving, and it was gone in an afternoon, irreversible the moment the water hit the cement, which makes the foundation the single most carbon-intensive moment in most home builds and the one the industry talks about least.

That silence is starting to break. Not because builders suddenly grew a conscience, but because software finally made low-carbon concrete legible at the level of a mix design.

Why concrete is a chemistry problem, not an energy problem

Cement production accounts for up to 8 percent of global man-made CO2. More than all of aviation, roughly three times over. Half of that carbon comes from the kiln's fuel, and the rest comes from the chemistry itself: heating limestone releases its bound CO2 whether the kiln runs on coal or sunshine.

That distinction matters because it means you cannot buy your way out with renewable electricity, which is the standard decarbonization playbook for nearly every other building material. The only ways to cut the carbon are to use less cement per yard, to replace some of it with industrial byproducts like fly ash or slag, or to inject captured CO2 into the mix so it mineralizes into stone.

Every one of those moves is a mix-design decision, and mix design has been, until very recently, an exercise in expensive conservatism. When the compressive strength of your foundation is the thing standing between a family and a lawsuit, nobody experiments, because engineers over-specify cement the way a nervous cook over-salts: cement is predictable, cement is cheap, the cost of being wrong is catastrophic, and the result is concrete that routinely contains 10 to 15 percent more cement than it needs.

What the algorithms actually do

In March, a team led by researchers at the University of Illinois released BOxCrete, an open-source Bayesian optimization framework trained on more than 500 strength measurements across 123 mixes, that predicts compressive strength development and then optimizes mixes for two goals at once: hit the strength target, minimize the embodied carbon. Gaussian process regression reached an R-squared of 0.94 with quantified uncertainty, which is the number that matters most to a structural engineer, because an optimizer that cannot tell you how unsure it is cannot be trusted with a foundation.

It is not alone. A study published this week in Scientific Reports used an ensemble of six machine-learning models on easily available mix parameters, water, cement, sand, coarse aggregate, curing age, and reached an R-squared of 0.9245 on strength prediction, with explicit uncertainty assessment for deployment conditions. Ten years ago this work lived in proprietary industrial datasets. Now the code is open. The data too.

On the commercial side, the less glamorous technology is CarbonCure, which injects a precise dose of captured CO2 into fresh concrete at the batch plant, where it reacts with calcium oxide to form nano-calcium-carbonate mineral, permanently embedded, slightly improving compressive strength.

That strength bump lets producers cut cement content 3 to 6 percent across their mix designs, and on average the system removes about 25 pounds of CO2 per cubic yard.

Read that number twice. The injected pound of CO2 is nearly irrelevant. The avoided 24 pounds, the cement that was never manufactured, is the entire story. CarbonCure charges producers about a dollar per yard and saves them roughly $1.50 a yard in cement, which is why over 200 plants license it, a quiet adoption curve driven not by regulation or idealism but by the oldest force in construction economics, the green option being the cheap option, and nobody had to pass a law to make it happen.

The math for a real house

Here is the part nobody had calculated for a typical build, so I did it myself, with inputs shown and assumptions stated.

A 2,000-square-foot slab-on-grade at four inches takes about 25 cubic yards. Perimeter footings add roughly 20 more. Call it 45 yards, 34.4 cubic meters, the number every batch plant dispatcher in America can picture without looking anything up. Published life-cycle figures put structural concrete at roughly 410 kilograms of CO2 per cubic meter, dominated by the cement fraction, which accounts for about 88 percent of a mix's emissions.

Multiply it out. 34.4 times 410 gives 14,104 kilograms, or 14.1 metric tons of CO2 for the foundation, and that is the baseline: the most ordinary house, the most ordinary pour.

Now layer the reductions. Replace 30 percent of the cement with fly ash and the published intensity drops to about 290 kilograms per cubic meter, which brings the foundation to 10.0 tons, a 4.1-ton cut. Apply machine-learning mix optimization of the BOxCrete kind, trimming another 12 percent of cement while holding strength, and subtract roughly 1.2 more tons. Add CarbonCure at 25 pounds a yard across 45 yards, another half ton. Total: about 8.3 tons against a 14.1-ton baseline, a 41 percent reduction, on the same slab, with the same forms, on the same schedule.

The cost delta on all of this is the detail that should end the conversation. CarbonCure's dollar-a-yard is already offset by its cement savings at the plant level. Fly ash and slag mixes have been cheaper than pure Portland for decades wherever they are available. Type IL Portland-limestone cement, the one-for-one swap that cuts clinker content, is typically cost-neutral. The low-carbon foundation is not the expensive foundation. It is the foundation nobody asked for.

The part where I pump the brakes

None of this is as clean as the arithmetic suggests, and the counterargument deserves its full weight.

Fly ash is a byproduct of coal power, and coal is dying, which means the cheapest supplementary material in the playbook is getting scarcer every year; slag and natural pozzolans are the actual future, and their regional availability is uneven. Thirty-percent replacement can slow set times, which your concrete sub will notice at 6 a.m. on a cold pour day. BOxCrete is research software, brilliant and open, but no residential batch plant is running Bayesian optimizers on a 45-yard order today, and the gap between a paper and a purchase order is where most construction AI goes to die.

And the strongest objection of all: 14 tons is a lot for a foundation, but it is small against the lifetime operational carbon of a gas-heated, poorly insulated house. If the same builder skips the heat pump and the air sealing to chase a low-carbon slab, the math inverts. Embodied carbon is the opening act, not the show.

Still, the opening act is the one you can change with a phone call to the batch plant, and the policy weather is shifting. New York now sets global-warming-potential limits on concrete mixes for all state projects, mandatory since 2025 and tightening from 2027. California's Buy Clean law has required environmental product declarations and industry-average GWP limits on state work since 2022, and the spec language is moving from prescriptive minimum-cement rules to performance requirements, which is exactly the door optimized mixes need. What starts on public jobs has a way of becoming the default bid everywhere.

What to actually do on your next build

Ask your ready-mix supplier three questions, in this order. First, do you batch with Type IL Portland-limestone cement as standard, because it is the zero-effort swap that cuts clinker by roughly a tenth. Second, what is the highest supplementary-cementitious-material replacement your 3,000-psi residential mix can carry without blowing the schedule, and can you show me the break-test history. Third, is your plant licensed for CO2 injection, and if not, why.

Then ask for the environmental product declaration for the mix you are buying, because an EPD is an independently verified life-cycle document, and the moment you ask for one, you join the demand signal that is dragging this industry forward faster than any mandate.

If the supplier cannot produce one, that tells you everything the brochure will not.

The foundation under your house will outlast you. The carbon it banked does not have to outlast the climate it was poured into.

Limitations

This analysis used 410 kilograms of CO2 per cubic meter, a figure published for structural concrete; a typical 3,000-psi residential slab may run slightly lower cement content, so 14.1 tons is an upper-range estimate, not a measured one. The 12 percent optimization saving is modeled from BOxCrete's published multi-objective capability, not from a residential pour, and no third-party audit of CarbonCure's 25-pound-per-yard average exists for small residential orders. Fly ash availability and set-time behavior vary regionally and seasonally. West Coast 2026 pricing informed the cost claims; your market's cement chemistry and the distance to the nearest licensed plant will move the numbers.
