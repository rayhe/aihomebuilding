TyBOT Ties 1,100 Rebar Joints an Hour. Your Foundation Is Sixteen Slabs Too Small.

By Jake Kowalski · September 28, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Five in the morning on a bridge deck outside Orlando, and a yellow gantry is crawling across sixty feet of rebar mat, snapping wire ties with a sound like a stapler the size of a refrigerator. By lunch it will have tied more intersections than a full ironworker crew manages in a week. Nobody on that deck is bent over anymore, and that is the whole pitch.

TyBOT is the machine's name, and Advanced Construction Robotics of Allison Park, Pennsylvania builds it. It rides a rail, or as of this year the timber edge forms themselves, and its dual cameras see every bar intersection and tie it. No BIM file, no pre-programming, no machine learning training run. Just eyes and a wire gun.

On paper it guarantees a minimum of 900 ties an hour and field crews report 1,100, with observed bursts north of 1,200. A skilled ironworker ties 150 to 250 an hour. Divide it out and the robot is roughly six times faster, running in the dark under a light ring off a generator that goes twelve hours between refuels, and setup takes about two hours. One fifteen-pound wire spool holds roughly three thousand ties.

1,100 vs. 200 Ties per hour: TyBOT field rate vs. a typical human ironworker, per Advanced Construction Robotics and contractor reports.

2026: the change that almost opened the residential door

Until this year, TyBOT needed a screed rail to travel on, which meant the contractor had to re-sequence the job to get the rail installed earlier than the schedule wanted it, which is annoying. This year ACR rolled out bogies that ride directly on the timber edge forms a crew already erects for the pour. That sounds like a footnote, and on a bridge job it basically is. But houses do not have screed rails; houses have edge forms. This is the first time the machine's travel system has spoken residential.

It still does not speak residential: every one of the 65-plus documented deployments is horizontal infrastructure: bridge decks, parking structures, big flat mats. A Florida contractor who runs the thing put the threshold bluntly: you need a minimum of two or three spans, forty feet of width or wider, or there is not enough production to justify the mobilization. Snow on the mat stops the cameras, and so does debris. And the purchase price for a TyBOT 3.0 starts at $425,500, which is why almost everyone uses the robot-as-a-service model instead, where ACR charges per completed tie.

Sixteen-slab math

So let us do the thing nobody in the press releases does: run the numbers on an actual house. Take a typical slab-on-grade, thirty by forty feet, twelve hundred square feet, number-four bars at twelve inches on center each way.

Read that last row twice. Thirty-five minutes of tying, then two hours of setup, a mobilization, and a per-tie price ACR does not publish: on a single home the setup alone exceeds the work. At the vendor's own twenty-thousand-square-foot threshold, you need about sixteen foundations in one place before the robot earns its truck ride. A wire spool holds three thousand ties, so one spool covers roughly four and a half houses at the fifty-percent pattern, which is a fun stat that changes nothing about the threshold.

Stated honestly, the pitch is not "the robot replaces your rodbuster." It is "the robot replaces your rodbuster if you pour sixteen slabs in a row," which describes exactly one kind of residential customer: the production builder running a subdivision, or a multifamily podium deck. Everybody else is watching a bridge technology through a fence.

What speed misses

Speed is the headline, but the back is the story. A NIOSH evaluation found that tying rebar at ground level with pliers increases the risk of hand, wrist, and low-back injuries. A Canadian study found rodworkers suffer a higher proportion of lost-time musculoskeletal injuries to the back and upper limbs than all other construction trades combined. A Florida crew chief who runs TyBOT put it the way only a guy who has done the work can: you are bent over, tying at your feet, it just hurts, you cannot stay bent over that long.

This is where the robot stops being a gadget and starts being a staffing strategy. Nobody goes home; they move to the placing and the carrying, the work that needs judgment, while the machine takes the part that destroys spines. Pair it with ACR's IronBOT, which lifts and places the bar, and the company claims installation time drops by half. It takes the worst job on the deck, not the workers.

Why it might not matter

Now the other side, because it is strong. Tying the rebar on a house foundation takes a crew three or four hours inside a six-month build. Automating that step is optimizing the fifteenth bottleneck on the list. Residential schedules die on inspections, weather, subcontractor no-shows, and change orders, and a wire-tying robot fixes exactly none of those. If your foundation crew is the constraint on your project, your project has bigger problems than wire ties.

There is also a hole in the paperwork nobody has filled. TyBOT ties single-snap ties, and every deployment is a bridge deck under an engineer's supervision. Whether a residential building inspector or a structural engineer signs off on a robot-tied mat for a house foundation is not established in any public source I could find. Until an AHJ somewhere stamps one, the residential conversation is theoretical.

What we did not prove

A few honest gaps. ACR's per-tie service price is not public, so the threshold in this article is stated in square feet, using the vendor's own rule of thumb, because a true dollar break-even cannot be computed from open data. All of the installation-savings and tie-rate figures are vendor or contractor-reported; no independent third-party audit of TyBOT's speed claims exists. Labor cost swings hard by market: BLS state medians for reinforcing ironworkers run from about $37,000 a year in Mississippi to over $106,000 in Washington, so the human-cost math in the table moves nearly threefold depending on your zip code. And the injury statistics describe ironwork generally, not crews working alongside this specific robot.

Who should care

If you are a production builder pouring sixteen or more slabs a quarter, or sitting on a multifamily podium deck, call ACR and make them give you the per-tie price in writing, then run it against your rodbuster's burdened rate at your local wages. That is the entire decision, and it fits on an index card.

If you are a custom builder or a remodeler, skip it. Your foundation does not have the square footage, and your schedule does not have the bottleneck.

If you are a homeowner, the robot is not coming to your job site, and that is fine, because your foundation's rebar was never the expensive part. Watch the per-tie price. Once ACR publishes it, this math stops being a thought experiment and starts being a line item, and the timber-form travel mode they shipped this year is the first sign they are thinking about houses at all.
