Your House Sits on Thirty Bolts. Nobody Counted Them.

By Jake Kowalski · September 23, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

On pour day, while the crew is finishing the slab edge and the pump truck is packing up, one guy walks the foundation with a bucket of bent steel J-hooks and shoves them into the wet concrete every few feet. Takes him maybe forty minutes. Nobody photographs it. Nobody counts. Later the inspector walks the wall, squints at a few of them, signs the card, and the next morning the sill plate goes down and those bolts disappear from human sight for the rest of the building's life.

That connection, roughly thirty half-inch bolts on a typical house, is the only thing holding the entire structure to the ground. Wind uplift, seismic shear, the whole lateral system starts there. And the record of whether it was installed correctly is, in most cases, a tired memory and a signed inspection card.

What the Code Demands

The rule is Section R403.1.6 of the residential code, and it is beautifully specific: bolts at least a half inch in diameter, embedded at least seven inches into the concrete, spaced no more than six feet apart, at least two bolts per plate section, one bolt no more than twelve inches from each end of the plate, and every bolt sitting in the middle third of the plate's width. Five numbers, no ambiguity. It is the kind of rule that reads like it was written after somebody investigated a failure, which it was.

Two of those five numbers are the ones that fail in the field: spacing and end distance. A bolt pushed during the pour ends up seven or eight feet from its neighbor. A bolt set while the finisher was thinking about lunch lands eighteen inches from the plate end instead of twelve. Both look fine from the truck, and both pass a glance. Neither gets fixed, because fixing wet-set bolts after the concrete sets means drilling and epoxying a retrofit anchor, which is twenty minutes of work nobody schedules because the schedule says the plates go down tomorrow.

The Vision Models Already Exist

Here is the part that should bother you. Computer vision can already find bolts in construction imagery with serious accuracy. Researchers running YOLO object detection on bridge-bolt inspection imagery reported a mean average precision of 0.940 with YOLO v5x, trained over 50,000 steps on a dataset built for exactly this kind of detection task (MDPI Sensors, 2023). A separate 2025 paper used LoRA fine-tuned image generation to build synthetic bolt datasets for training damage-recognition models, because real-world defect photos are rare and annotation is expensive (MDPI Buildings). Finding steel hardware in a cluttered scene and classifying it, the detection problem itself, is effectively solved in industrial settings.

And the capture pipeline is already walking every large job site. Buildots raised $130 million on September 14, bringing total funding to $297 million, on the strength of turning job site imagery into progress data for firms like Intel, Mortenson, and STO Building Group. OpenSpace just announced its AI agent ecosystem at Waypoint 2026, built on imagery from 110,000 projects and 77 billion square feet with 500 million expert-verified labels. Both platforms can find missing drywall and off-schedule concrete. Neither one is pointed at the muddy foundation at 7 a.m. on pour day, which is the only moment a camera can see the bolts before the plate covers them.

The Missed-Bolt Math

So how much does a missed bolt actually cost in structural terms? Nobody had published the back-of-the-envelope, so I ran it. Take an 1,800 square foot single-story wood frame house with roughly 180 linear feet of perimeter wall, in a high seismic design category with a seismic response coefficient of 0.2 and a seismic weight of about 35,000 pounds. Design base shear is 0.2 times 35,000, which gives 7,000 pounds of lateral force at the foundation line. Spread over 180 feet of wall, that is about 39 pounds per foot.

At the code's six-foot spacing, each bolt carries roughly 230 pounds of shear. A half-inch cast-in-place J-bolt in 2,500-psi concrete has an allowable shear in the neighborhood of 700 to 1,100 pounds. So the code builds in a safety margin of roughly three to four. Comfortable. Now miss every second bolt, gaps stretching to twelve feet: demand per remaining bolt climbs to about 470 pounds, and the margin halves. Miss two in a row, an eighteen-foot gap where bolts got knocked flat during the pour and nobody reset them, and each surviving bolt is asked for roughly 700 pounds, right at the allowable limit, with zero help if the concrete around that bolt has a honeycomb void or the bolt drifted too close to the foundation edge.

~3 bolts That is all it takes: three consecutive missed or displaced anchor bolts erase the code's full safety margin on a typical 1,800 sq ft home in a seismic zone, assuming standard 2,500-psi concrete and the illustrative inputs above.

End-distance rules exist for the same reason. The code wants a bolt within twelve inches of each plate end because wall ends concentrate shear, the same way a rope concentrates force at the knot. A bolt eighteen inches from the corner leaves the corner plate's last foot and a half effectively unanchored at exactly the spot where wind uplift and seismic racking hit hardest.

These are illustrative numbers, not a structural analysis of your house. Weights, the seismic coefficient, and bolt capacities are all ranges. But the shape of the result is robust: the code's six-foot spacing has a real margin, and a small number of field misses consume it fast.

What a Miss Costs in Dollars

Here is the asymmetry that makes this worth a phone photo. Fixing a missed bolt before the sill plate goes down means drilling and setting an epoxy anchor, roughly $25 to $40 per bolt installed, twenty minutes with a rotary hammer. Once the house is built, a missing bolt in a retrofit is a line item inside a brace-and-bolt seismic retrofit that typically runs $3,000 to $7,000 in California, where the state Earthquake Brace + Bolt program offsets up to $3,000 of it for pre-1980 homes. That ratio is absurd: a bolt verified for pennies at pour, or a retrofit measured in thousands of dollars and a grant application decades later.

The Honest Objections

Let me make the case against this idea, because it deserves a strong one. Foundations are already inspected in most jurisdictions, and an AHJ inspector with a tape measure is better at this than a neural net trained on bridges. Nobody has published defect rates for residential anchor bolts in new construction, and the real bolt crisis in California is pre-1980 houses with no bolts at all, which is a retrofit story, not a new-construction story. A model trained on bridge bolts will stumble on a muddy residential foundation full of form ties, rebar chairs, and somebody's boot.

Wet-set bolts also move. A photo taken at 10 a.m. proves where the bolt was before the vibrator passed, not where it stayed. And the detection workflow has no home yet: no residential product exists that checks bolt spacing from photos, so whoever wants this is cobbling together an open-source YOLO model, their own phone photos, and a spreadsheet with the code's spacing rules. That is fine for a curious GC, and useless as an industry practice until somebody ships it.

What We Did Not Prove

A few things this article claims without the data to fully back it up. Nobody has published what share of residential foundations have mis-spaced anchor bolts, so the prevalence of the problem is inferred from the physics, not measured. YOLO accuracy figures come from bridge and industrial bolt inspection, and accuracy on residential foundations will be lower; I have not tested it. The bolt capacity numbers are textbook ranges for cast-in-place J-bolts, and real capacity depends on the concrete strength on your pour day, which your inspector may or may not have verified. Retrofit cost figures are California seismic figures; in a wind zone the numbers and the products differ.

The Ask

If you are a GC or a super, this is the cheapest QA step on the schedule: after the bolts are set and before the sill plate goes on, walk the wall with your phone and shoot forty photos, one every few feet, with a tape measure in the frame. A free object-detection model can count the bolts and flag gaps wider than six feet. Twenty minutes of your time, zero dollars of software, and a permanent record that the most important connection in the house was built right.

If you are buying new construction, ask your builder for photos of the foundation bolts before the plate goes down. They probably do not have them. Their reaction will tell you something about how the rest of the house was documented.

And if you are in an older house in earthquake country with no bolts at all, the fix is the one that already has a grant attached. Your house predates the rule this article is about, and that is why the state's brace-and-bolt program exists. Apply at EarthquakeBraceBolt.com during enrollment.
