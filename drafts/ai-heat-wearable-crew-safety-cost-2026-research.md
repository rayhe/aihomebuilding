## Original contribution: the crew-cost model
Nobody has combined the wearable pricing with construction-specific claim costs into a GC decision model. Inputs (all sourced above):
- 15-worker residential crew × $500 SlateSafety device = $7,500 one-time (software/cellular/support included; bulk discounts available).
- One heat-stress incident averages $53,589 (OH&S) — a lost-time construction claim averages ~$40,000 at 12 months (WCRI), up to $49,947 in CA at 24 months.
- Break-even math: $53,589 ÷ $500 = 107 device-years per prevented incident. For a 15-person crew, ONE prevented incident pays for the whole crew's devices for ~7 years. At $40,000/claim: 80 device-years, ~5.3 crew-years.
- The tenure multiplier: 25.5% of heat claims hit workers in their first 60 days (WCRI) — the acclimatization window the proposed federal rule would have formalized. A crew with normal turnover is buying protection for the exact cohort at highest risk.
- OSHA penalty context: $2M+ in heat penalties in 2024 across a small citation count (60 citations 2022-2024) means per-citation exposure is large — one General Duty Clause citation on a heat fatality can dwarf the device cost by orders of magnitude.
- The counterweight baked into the model: devices are a one-time $500, but the basics (water, rest, shade, acclimatization schedules) are free or near-free and legally expected under the NEP. The band is a supplement, not a substitute.

## Limitations (for the article's dedicated section)
- SlateSafety's $500 pricing and the Air Force base outcome (30+/yr → zero) come from a CEO interview in Inc., not an independent audit. No peer-reviewed study shows these devices reduce heat incidents on residential construction sites specifically.
- The Kenzen accuracy paper's authors include paid Kenzen employees; devices were provided free — vendor-adjacent science, flagged as such.
- The $53,589/incident figure is from a 2022 industry magazine article; methodology not published. The WCRI $40,000 figure is construction lost-time claims at 12 months — different claim universe, not strictly comparable, presented as a range.
- Heat fatality counts are systematically undercounted (OSHA ~50/yr vs OSHA-estimated 550+/yr); any death-rate framing must state which number is used.
- I could not verify current Kenzen subscription pricing (per-worker per-month, unpublished) — the model uses SlateSafety's published $500.
- Six states have their own heat standards; the "no federal rule" framing applies to the other 44 states plus federal-plan states.

## Strongest counterargument
The strongest case against the article's thesis: the armband is a surveillance device wearing a safety costume. It collects biometric data on workers — mostly young, disproportionately Latino, often the newest and least powerful people on the crew — and streams it to the boss's dashboard. A device meant to say "take a break" can become a device that says "the algorithm says you're fine, keep working," and a GC who has invested $500/worker in monitoring has a financial incentive to believe the green light. The evidence base is almost entirely vendor-reported; the one dramatic case (Air Force base, 30+ injuries to zero) is a CEO's anecdote in a business magazine, and the accuracy science was co-authored by the company selling the device. Meanwhile the interventions with the strongest evidence — water, rest, shade, acclimatization, and a foreman empowered to stop work — cost almost nothing and require no one's biometric data. If a GC buys bands instead of fixing a shade-and-water program, the technology is cover for negligence. The article must state this at full strength, in Marcus's voice: the worker's body is not the employer's telemetry.
