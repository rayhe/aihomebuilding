Your Foundation Was 4,000 PSI at the Plant. It Was 3,200 PSI at Your Curb. The Water Did That.

By Jake Kowalski · September 24, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Pour day. Six forty in the morning, the pump is primed, the crew is standing around the forms with nothing to do until the first truck shows up. Then it does, backs up to the hopper, and the driver does the thing every driver does: he reaches for the water hose.

He is not stealing. He is not breaking the law. Out of the plant the concrete runs a little stiff, the chute needs flow, and the easiest way to get flow is water. A few gallons here, a few there. Nobody writes it down. Nobody can, really. Water goes in, the drum turns, and your foundation gets a little weaker with every revolution.

How much weaker? NRMCA, the National Ready Mixed Concrete Association, has a rule of thumb for this, and it is brutal in its simplicity: one gallon of water per cubic yard buys about one inch of slump. And every one of those gallons costs you roughly 150 to 200 PSI of compressive strength, wastes about a quarter bag of cement, and bumps drying shrinkage up by around ten percent. Add two gallons per yard over the design amount and you can wipe out the entire strength overdesign the engineer built into the mix.

Run the math on a normal truck. Ten yards. Driver adds what feels like a modest amount, say 30 gallons across the load, to get a stiff mix to flow on a hot afternoon. That is three gallons per yard. Call it 500 PSI gone, give or take. Your 4,000 PSI spec just became a 3,500 PSI reality, and the delivery ticket says absolutely nothing about it.

The oldest open secret in concrete

ASTM C94, the spec that governs ready-mixed concrete, actually allows jobsite water addition. Conditions are specific: the slump has to be below what was ordered, the design water content cannot be exceeded, every addition must be measured and recorded on the delivery ticket, and the purchaser is supposed to sign off. After each addition the truck is supposed to mix 30 revolutions. No water after significant discharge has started.

That is the theory. In practice, the person recording the water is the person adding the water, the ticket is a carbon copy that gets rained on, and the person who suffers the strength loss is not in the truck. This is not a story about bad drivers. It is a story about a measurement gap: the most important quality variable in your foundation is adjusted by feel, in a steel drum, by someone with every incentive to keep the pour moving.

And it is invisible after the fact. Cylinder breaks at 28 days will catch a disaster, but nothing catches the quiet version: the load that came in at 3,400 instead of 4,000, still passes, still gets a stamp, and spends the next fifty years a little more crack-prone than the drawings assumed. You cannot core every foundation. You cannot un-pour concrete.

There is now a sensor inside the drum

This is where it gets good. Command Alkon, the company whose software already runs dispatch and batching for a huge share of the ready-mix industry, ships a system called Load Assurance. Their pitch is simple: sensors mounted inside the mixer drum measure slump, temperature, volume, water additions, and water-cement ratio continuously during transit, and stream it all to the cloud in real time. A 2024 update added real-time air content measurement to the same feed.

No more driver's perception of the load. Instead the drum reports itself: how much water went in, when, and what it did to the slump. Every load shows up on the producer's dashboard. A QC manager can watch a truck add water at the jobsite from an office forty miles away. Tighter water control, in their data, means less cement per yard to hit the same strength, and less scatter in break results.

What matters for you: this is not a lab prototype. It grew out of VERIFI, which Missouri DOT researchers at Iowa State tested on real highway work and found accurate: it measured slump and temperature in the truck correctly, adjusted slump in transit with water and admixture without hurting performance, and produced documentation complete enough that the researchers recommended accepting the data for specification compliance. DOTs do not say things like that about gadgets.

The pour does not end at the truck

There is a second half to this story, and it happens after the concrete leaves the chute. A London company called Converge built a platform, ConcreteDNA, that pairs wireless sensors embedded in the pour with AI models predicting when the concrete actually reaches strength. They raised about $22 million in May 2025 to scale it, and they are partnered with DEWALT on the sensor hardware. Their claim: predict critical strength with up to 95 percent confidence, strip formwork up to 30 percent sooner, and dial back cement content by around 15 percent because you are measuring reality instead of guessing.

Treat the 30 percent and the 95 percent as company numbers, because they are. But the direction is right, and the two halves fit together: sensors in the drum watch what goes into the mix, sensors in the pour watch what the mix becomes. Between them, the black box era of concrete is ending. Your foundation's entire early life, from batch ticket to formwork strip, becomes a data record instead of a driver's memory.

What I would actually do

Here is the honest limitation: you cannot bolt sensors onto somebody else's fleet. This is producer-side technology, and your leverage as a builder or an owner-builder is in the asking, not the buying. But the asking costs nothing, and most people never do it.

Before your next pour, call the ready-mix supplier and ask one question: do your trucks carry in-drum sensors, and will you share the water-addition log for my loads? If the answer is yes, you just bought the cheapest quality insurance in construction. If the answer is a confused silence, you learned something about your supplier.

Second, enforce the ticket. ASTM C94 already requires every water addition to be recorded on the delivery ticket with the purchaser's sign-off. Put a max site-added water number on your purchase order, hand the ticket to one person on your crew, and make the driver initial every addition. It will feel awkward the first time. Do it anyway.

Third, and this is the one that actually solves the driver's problem: specify superplasticizer instead of water. A mid-range water reducer buys the same slump increase as those gallons, costs a few dollars a yard, and does not touch the strength. NRMCA says it outright in their guidance: increasing slump with admixtures will not alter concrete properties the way water does. He gets his flow. You keep your PSI. Everybody wins except the shrinkage cracks.

Your foundation is the one part of the house you can never redo. For fifty years it holds up everything you own, and its quality was decided in a spinning drum on a Tuesday morning by a guy with a hose. Now there is a sensor watching. Ask for the data.
