Your Foundation Is Sinking. A Satellite Can Measure It to the Millimeter, and the Data Is Free.

By Jake Kowalski · September 25, 2026
← Back to articles

Drive out to the Baytown Nature Center on a Saturday and you will see wetlands, fishing piers, and miles of trails spread across a 500-acre peninsula east of Houston. Fifty years ago the same ground held Brownwood, a waterfront neighborhood of more than 400 families. Aquifers underneath were pumped dry for decades, and the land sank more than nine feet. Hurricane Carla battered the low houses in 1961, and Hurricane Alicia finished the argument in 1983. Residents took buyouts and left, and today the neighborhood is a park.

Nine feet, not nine inches.

Brownwood is the extreme case, the one that shows up in the local paper every few years when somebody writes the history piece. Under millions of houses right now, the ordinary version of the same physics is running slower and quieter, and the bill lands the same way: cracked slabs, stair-step brick, doors that will not latch, and a foundation contractor quoting five figures. Expansive soils damage one home in four across the continental United States and cost more than $15 billion a year. That is more than twice the damage from every earthquake, hurricane, tornado, and flood combined, and the number comes from a HUD-published review of the civil engineering literature, not a contractor's brochure.

The ground does not sit still

Houston is the American capital of sinking land. For decades the U.S. Geological Survey has monitored the Houston-Galveston region, and its reports read like a slow-motion disaster film. At the agency's Addicks station in Harris County, the ground dropped 3.654 feet over 38 years. Regional modeling puts total subsidence at up to 10 feet in southeastern Harris County, with a broad swath of central and southeastern Harris County down at least 6 feet. Land that has dropped a foot or more grew from about 350 square miles in 1954 to roughly 2,500 square miles by 1973, and the pumping never fully stopped. A 2025 review in the journal Groundwater put the permanent loss of underground water storage at 12 cubic kilometers since 1906, roughly sixty times the capacity of Lake Houston.

Groundwater pumping is only half the story; clay makes up the other half. Across Texas, Colorado, Oklahoma, and much of the Midwest, clay soils swell when wet and shrink when dry, and that seasonal heave works a foundation like a slow jackhammer. One corner lifts while the opposite corner drops. Give it a decade and the drywall tells on you.

Radar that measures in millimeters

Satellites watch all of this now. InSAR, short for interferometric synthetic aperture radar, bounces radar pulses off the ground and compares the echoes across months and years. Shifts in the phase of the returning signal translate directly into distance moved, and the system works at night and straight through cloud cover. On a good urban target it resolves motion down to one or two millimeters per year, a figure researchers confirmed in Tianjin by checking satellite measurements against precision ground leveling point by point and finding agreement within about two millimeters on most points, all of it measured from orbit.

Engineers have been buying this data for years. SkyGeo sells InSAR monitoring to civil firms with a satellite archive stretching back to 1992, thousands of measurement points per square kilometer, and automated alerts when movement crosses a threshold. SatSense does the same for construction projects, resolving pixels down to about three meters square. No equipment goes on site. Decades of historical motion arrive as a dataset, at a fraction of what a traditional survey campaign costs. Until recently, though, every bit of it was priced for infrastructure owners and engineering firms. Homeowners were never the customer.

Then NASA launched the big one

On July 30, 2025, a pickup-truck-sized radar satellite rode an Indian rocket into orbit. NISAR, the NASA-ISRO Synthetic Aperture Radar mission, carries the most sophisticated civilian radar ever flown, a dual-frequency system that sees through vegetation and cloud cover and revisits the same ground every 12 days. It picks up vertical shifts as small as one centimeter.

First science results landed this spring. NASA pointed NISAR at Mexico City, one of the fastest-sinking cities on Earth, and mapped blocks dropping up to two centimeters per month between October 2025 and January 2026. Parts of that city sank 14 inches a year in the 1990s and 2000s. Its Angel of Independence monument, built in 1910, has needed 14 steps added at its base as the street dropped away around it. NASA's deputy project manager called the early images confirmation that the measurements match expectations, and the science team expects a flood of discoveries as global coverage fills in.

Mexico City is the showpiece, and behind it sits the quiet revolution: the data pipeline.

Do the Addicks math

Let me run the numbers that matter for a homeowner, because this is where the satellite stops being a science story and starts being a money story.

USGS measured 3.654 feet of subsidence at Addicks over 38 years, which converts to 43.8 inches divided by 38 years: 1.15 inches a year, or 29.3 millimeters a year. InSAR resolves one to two millimeters a year. A single year of Houston-hotspot movement therefore stands roughly 15 to 30 times above the measurement noise. The satellite does not strain to see it. It sees it the way you see a pickup truck in a parking lot.

Now the damage threshold. Foundation engineers work to a deflection limit near 1 inch of vertical difference over 20 feet of structure, a ratio drawn from ACI concrete standards and confirmed the hard way by Terzaghi's 1938 survey of European buildings, which found cracking wherever differential settlement passed 25 millimeters. Divide threshold by rate: 25.4 millimeters divided by 29.3 millimeters per year is 0.87 years. On Addicks-rate ground, a house can cross from fine to structurally damaged in under a year of differential movement, and the satellite registers every year of it at roughly fifteen-to-one signal to noise.

That ratio is the asymmetry the industry never mentions. A piering job runs $7,000 to $30,000, with the national average near $16,000 for seven to ten piers at $1,500 to $3,500 apiece. The all-repairs national average sits around $5,400. Satellite data that could have flagged the trend years before the first crack is now free. Skipping the check was never the cheap option; it was only the invisible one.

The free part

NASA's OPERA project publishes surface displacement maps for North America from Sentinel-1 radar, free and open access through the Alaska Satellite Facility, with data running from 2016 to the present. Its Displacement Portal lets you click a 200-foot pixel and pull up a graph of cumulative ground movement over time. David Bekaert, the JPL project manager, describes it plainly: zoom to your state, your city block, and watch how the land moves. Franz Meyer, the facility's chief scientist, says the portal was built partly for people asking what is going on around their house.

Your house, specifically, which is what makes this new.

Coverage started with the Southwest, northern Mexico, and the New York metro, and has been expanding toward the full continent. Pixels run about 60 meters, so you are reading your block, not your slab. A steady downward slope of several millimeters a year on your block is exactly the signal that used to cost a drilling program to obtain, and now it costs a mouse click.

Five moves before you call a contractor
  1. Open the OPERA Displacement Portal and check your block. A steady multi-millimeter-per-year downward trend is the flag.
  2. Learn the 25-millimeter rule: one inch of differential settlement across 20 feet of foundation is the engineering action threshold, and a marble that will not sit still on the kitchen floor is the analog version.
  3. Buying in Houston-Galveston, the Central Valley, New Orleans, or Florida sinkhole country? Ask for an InSAR desk study as part of due diligence. Commercial archives reach back to 1992, and they cost a fraction of borings.
  4. In clay country, manage water first, because consistent perimeter moisture in drought and working drainage in rain prevents most heave damage, and gutters and grading beat piers on price every time.
  5. If the satellite shows movement and the house shows stair-step brick cracks or binding doors, hire a structural engineer before you hire a pier salesman, because diagnosis comes first and hardware comes second.

Honest objections

Here is the strongest case against satellite foundation watching, given a full hearing. InSAR tracks radar scatterers: rooftops, pavement, bare earth. Your foundation is not one of them. A three-meter pixel cannot separate your house from your neighbor's, and the instrument measures ground motion, not structural distress. Uniform settlement, where the whole lot rides down together, is mostly harmless. Expansive clay complicates everything, because the ground heaves up in wet months and drops in dry ones, and that seasonal bounce can mask or mimic a long-term trend unless the time series runs for years. Free OPERA pixels are 60 meters wide, which is a block, not a lot. No consumer product exists here, no app that texts you when your slab moves, and reading a displacement graph is not a structural diagnosis. Every word of that is fair.

None of it is the point. The satellite is a screening tool, not a verdict. It answers one question better than anything else available: has the ground under this neighborhood been moving, how fast, and for how long. An engineer still decides what the answer means for your house. But the engineer used to start from zero. Now they start from thirty years of data.

What this article did not prove

Addicks arithmetic uses a regional hotspot rate; your block may move an order of magnitude less, or more. My calculation assumes the measured motion is differential across the structure, which is the damaging kind; uniform settlement rarely cracks anything. Two-millimeter validation comes from a Tianjin building study, not a Houston ranch house, and C-band Sentinel data decorrelates faster over vegetation than the L-band NISAR data now coming online. Repair costs are national averages, and Houston clay-market pricing runs higher. OPERA's portal is built for agencies and researchers, not homeowners, and nobody has published an independent study of how often its block-level signal correctly predicts lot-level foundation trouble. Treat the satellite as the reason to investigate, never as the investigation.

Still, thirty years ago the ground under Brownwood was moving and nobody was watching. Now the watching is free.
