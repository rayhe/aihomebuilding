Your Heat Pump Burns $127 Every Winter Melting Ice Off Itself. Smarter Software Wants It Back.

By Priya Greenwood • October 5, 2026

Picture January in Fairbanks. Minus 30 outside. Your heat pump doing something that looks like sabotage. It stops heating the house and reverses itself, pulling warmth out of your living room to pump it into the outdoor unit and melt frost off its own coils. Steam rises off the box in the dark. Somewhere inside, an electric resistance heater, the most expensive way to make heat that exists, kicks on to cover the gap. Then the whole thing flips back and resumes heating like nothing happened. It will do this hundreds of times before spring.

This is the defrost cycle, and it is the quiet tax buried inside every cold-climate heat pump installation. Nobody puts it on the quote. Contractors talk about HSPF ratings and capacity at 5 degrees, and both of those numbers are measured in labs where defrost gets averaged away into the fine print. But in real houses, in real winters, melting ice off your own equipment consumes a measurable slice of your heating bill. How big a slice depends on your climate, your humidity, and, as a remarkable field study in Alaska just showed, on a few lines of control software your installer never asked about.

Ice is the enemy, and the cure costs heat

Air-source heat pumps make heat by boiling refrigerant at temperatures far below freezing, which means the outdoor coil runs colder than the surrounding air. When that air holds moisture, and winter air in places like Minneapolis or Boston holds plenty, frost forms on the coil the way it forms on a freezer that never defrosts. Frost insulates. Airflow chokes. Efficiency slides. So the machine periodically reverses the refrigeration cycle, turning the outdoor coil into a condenser that melts its own ice, while the indoor coil goes cold and the house gets nothing.

How often this happens is wildly climate-dependent. Researchers modeling defrost behavior across ten cities found as few as 56 defrost cycles per heating season in damp-but-mild Vancouver and as many as 1,070 in frigid Harbin, China, with a near-perfect inverse correlation between cycle count and outdoor temperature. A separate study of three Italian locations found that accounting for defrost raised annual electricity consumption by a mean of 10.7 percent in Milan, 9.5 percent in San Benedetto del Tronto, and 4.6 percent in Livigno. Milan, not the coldest of the three, paid the highest penalty, because frost needs moisture as well as cold, and humid cold is the worst combination. A Northeast Energy Efficiency Partnerships review of forty field and lab studies put the parasitic cost of defrost at typically less than 10 percent of heating energy during low-temperature operation, and noted that drain pan heaters, the little resistance elements that keep meltwater from refreezing in the base pan, add their own unmeasured parasitic loss on top.

Ten percent. Sit with that number for a second, because it is doing more work than it looks like. Nobody buying a heat pump is told to add ten percent to the winter operating cost on the quote. Nobody comparing a heat pump bid against a gas furnace bid is shown that line item. It is just there. Humming away in the dark. Melting ice with electricity you paid for.

The software was the problem all along

Here is where the story gets interesting, because the biggest defrost waste researchers have measured did not come from physics but from factory default settings.

During the winter of 2023-2024, researchers from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory and Oak Ridge National Laboratory instrumented a ductless heat pump in Fairbanks, Alaska, measuring electrical input, thermal output, and internal variables through the coldest months. The unit shipped with what the researchers diplomatically called "defrost-aggressive" software: a control algorithm that combined demand-based and timed defrost criteria and ran the base pan heater with enthusiasm. Mid-winter, with help from the manufacturer, they changed the controls to an efficiency-focused profile, and the changes were almost embarrassingly simple, just two of them: require at least four hours of heating runtime between defrost cycles, and run the base pan heater only during defrost and for five minutes after.

Those two changes cut the base pan heater's share of total power from 5 to 13 percent down to under 2 percent, an average savings of about 80 watts running continuously, while defrost runtime as a fraction of heating runtime fell by 72 to 86 percent at temperatures below minus 15 Celsius. At the very coldest test bin, the aggressive software's constant defrosting was visibly degrading the heat delivered to the room. The machine was spending so much energy melting its own ice that the house got colder.

Read that again and notice what it implies. A huge fraction of the defrost tax was never thermodynamically necessary; it was a conservative factory setting, chosen by engineers who would rather defrost too often than field a warranty claim for a frozen coil, and your heat pump is probably running a version of that same conservative logic right now.

Teaching the machine to feel frost

Fixing this properly turns out to be a sensing problem, which is why it has become a machine learning problem. Conventional defrost controls are either dumb or expensive. Timed defrost just runs on a clock, melting ice that may not be there, while demand-based defrost watches coil temperatures against heuristic thresholds, and one 2025 study of vision-based frost detection reported that conventional methods top out around 85 percent accuracy with false-defrost rates as high as 68 percent. Every false defrost is a cycle spent heating the outdoors for nothing.

Researchers are now training neural networks to do better with hardware that is already in the box. At RWTH Aachen University, a team built a deep reinforcement learning controller that decides when to start defrosting using only standard temperature measurements, no specialized frost sensors, no heuristic thresholds. In dynamic 24-hour hardware-in-the-loop tests it improved efficiency by 9.1 percent over demand-based control and 7.1 percent over time-based control, and when the researchers let it keep learning online, it adapted to a blocked airflow condition in real time and beat its own static training by 16.6 percent. A separate paper presented at the IEA Heat Pump Conference in Chicago showed that neural networks can separate frosting behavior from normal control responses using nothing but ambient and evaporation temperatures, sensors every heat pump already has, and that reinforcement learning improved efficiency up to 9.4 percent over conventional time-controlled defrosting. Even a camera works: the 2025 vision study reached 97.79 percent frost recognition accuracy, processing each image in about 1.4 milliseconds.

Manufacturers are moving the same direction without the academic vocabulary. Carrier's cold-climate line now ships with what it calls advanced defrost: the unit monitors the outdoor coil to detect frost before engaging, adjusts compressor speed through the cycle, and senses when the melt is actually complete instead of running a fixed timer. Those units were lab-tested to minus 23 Fahrenheit. The industry knows the software matters; it just has not told you.

The math nobody puts on the quote

Let me do the arithmetic the sales brochure skips. Take a 2,000 square foot house in climate zone 6, Minneapolis or Chicago or Denver winters, where annual heating delivered runs around 60 million BTU, which is 17,580 kilowatt-hours of heat, and where a good variable-speed cold-climate unit delivers that at a seasonal COP of about 2.5, so the meter sees roughly 7,030 kilowatt-hours of electricity for heating.

Applying the field-measured penalty, ten percent of that heating electricity goes to defrost and pan heaters, about 700 kilowatt-hours a year. The Energy Information Administration projects average residential electricity at 18.2 cents per kilowatt-hour in 2026, which puts the defrost tax at roughly $127 a year for the average American ratepayer. But averages lie about geography the way they lie about everything: in Massachusetts, at 31.4 cents, the same waste costs about $220, while in Louisiana, at 12.4 cents, it costs $87. Humid cold plus expensive electricity is the worst seat in the house. That seat is called New England.

Here is the hopeful part: the Fairbanks study suggests roughly two-thirds to three-quarters of that waste is addressable with smarter controls, the 72 to 86 percent defrost runtime reduction plus the pan heater fix. That is $85 to $95 a year back at national average rates, $150 to $165 in Massachusetts, every year, for the life of the equipment, from software: no new hardware, no service call, just a control algorithm that defrosts when there is actually frost.

There is a smaller number inside the big number worth isolating, because it shows how silly the current defaults are. The Fairbanks pan heater alone, under the aggressive factory profile, was burning about 80 watts continuously. Over a five-month heating season that is roughly 288 kilowatt-hours, about $52 a year at average rates, spent keeping a drip tray warm, which efficiency-focused control nearly erased.

Arguing against the hype, at full strength

All of that is real, and it still might not be the most important line on your heating bill, because defrost is roughly ten percent of winter heating electricity, which means ninety percent is not defrost. A leaky envelope, an oversized unit short-cycling, or backup resistance strips firing at 35 degrees will each dwarf the defrost line item without breaking a sweat. If your ducts leak 20 percent into a cold attic, the fanciest defrost algorithm on earth is rearranging deck chairs. Any honest version of this story says the same thing. Envelope and sizing first. Controls second.

The AI controllers deserve their own cold water. The reinforcement learning results are from dynamic 24-hour lab tests, not full heating seasons in occupied homes, and nobody has published season-long field data on an RL defrost controller in a real house with real occupants, real setbacks, and a teenager who leaves the back door open. Carrier's advanced defrost is proprietary firmware; you cannot download it onto the unit you already own. And the Fairbanks researchers were explicit that their efficiency-focused profile was exploratory, a probe of what is possible, not a shipping recommendation. Lab demos are not products, and the graveyard of building technology is full of control strategies that worked beautifully on the test bench.

There is also a quieter risk in smarter defrost that the papers underplay. Every defrost decision is a bet, and under-defrosting has a failure mode that over-defrosting does not: a coil that ices solid can damage the unit. Manufacturers run conservative defaults partly because warranty claims are expensive. Angry customers are worse. An algorithm that shaves defrost cycles to the bone is optimizing for efficiency on a test rig where nobody has to pay for a cracked coil; in your house, you do.

What to actually do

First, if you are buying: ask whether the unit uses demand-based defrost or timed defrost, and treat a vague answer as an answer, then prefer models on the NEEP Cold Climate Air Source Heat Pump list, which is the closest thing the industry has to an independent cold-weather report card. Ask the installer two questions most of them never hear: how is the base pan heater controlled, and at what outdoor temperature does backup resistance heat lock out? The second question matters more than the first. Strips firing unnecessarily will cost you more in a month than defrost will in a season.

Second, if you already own one: keep the outdoor unit clear. Snow berms, leaf drifts, and that decorative lattice you built around it all restrict airflow, and the reinforcement learning research shows degraded airflow is exactly what confuses defrost timing. If your pan heater seems to run constantly through winter, ask your technician whether the control board supports a smarter defrost profile. Some do, via firmware, and nobody will offer unless asked.

Third, budget the tax honestly: when comparing a heat pump quote against gas in a cold climate, add roughly ten percent to the winter electricity estimate, knowing the penalty runs higher in humid-cold regions and lower in dry cold. Anyone whose payback spreadsheet assumes lab-rated efficiency in January in Minneapolis is selling you a number, not a forecast.

Fourth, shrink the load before you optimize the machine: the defrost tax scales with heating demand, so every BTU of air sealing and insulation reduces it. A tighter house needs fewer defrost cycles because it needs less heating, full stop. Controls are the multiplier, but the envelope is the base.

Limitations of This Analysis

The $127 figure is modeled, not measured: 60 MMBtu of delivered heat, seasonal COP 2.5, a 10 percent defrost and pan heater penalty, and the EIA's projected 2026 national average of 18.2 cents per kilowatt-hour, while real penalties swing with humidity as much as temperature, as the Italian data shows clearly, from 4.6 to 10.7 percent across three locations in one country. No full-season field data exists for reinforcement learning defrost controllers in occupied homes; the 9.1 percent efficiency gain comes from dynamic 24-hour laboratory tests. The 68 percent false-defrosting figure is one 2025 paper's characterization of conventional methods, not an industry consensus. Electricity prices cited are EIA projections and state snapshots, not any household's actual tariff, and time-of-use rates can shift the dollar math substantially. Nothing here replaces a Manual J load calculation or a contractor who has actually watched your unit defrost in January.

Here is the sentence I want you to carry into the showroom. Your heat pump will spend part of every winter heating the outdoors, and about a tenth of your winter electricity is the price. Most of that price is not physics but caution, baked into factory software by engineers who will never see your utility bill. The machines are learning to feel frost, so make sure yours is one of them.
