By Marcus Washington · September 11, 2026

The consent judgment landed in January 2025, and it read like a confession. Two drywall and painting companies in Phoenix, owned by the same people, had spent years systematically denying overtime to more than 1,400 of their own workers. Federal investigators ordered $7.45 million in back wages and damages. Every dollar of it was money the workers had already earned, hanging board and taping seams, and never been paid. (DOL Wage and Hour Division)

Fourteen hundred people, one city, one trade. If that number does not stop you cold, you have been in this industry too long.

I write about the people who build houses for a living, and wage theft is the crime this industry commits against its own more than any other. It does not make the evening news. Nobody perp-walks a payroll clerk. But the federal government's own enforcement numbers say the robbery is getting bigger, and a new generation of phone apps claims it can stop the theft cold. But the cure looks exactly like surveillance, because it is surveillance.

The Biggest Robbery Nobody Prosecutes

Wage theft sounds petty, and it is not. In fiscal year 2025, the Wage and Hour Division recovered more than $259 million in back wages for nearly 177,000 workers across the economy, the largest haul since 2019, and construction keeps showing up in the case files: $594,000 for 419 workers denied overtime by a Florida contractor, $207,000 for 157 workers at a Michigan electrical contractor whose overtime rates were shorted on job sites in two states. (DOL, January 2026)

Overtime shaving is only the visible part. Economists at the Economic Policy Institute calculate that illegally misclassifying a construction worker as an independent contractor costs that worker between $10,177 and $16,729 a year, in lost overtime, benefits, and employer contributions to Social Security, unemployment insurance, and workers' comp. Not a rounding error: a second car, a year of community college, gone by paperwork. (EPI)

Researchers at UMass Amherst who audited the residential construction business model found the fraud is structural, not occasional. More than one in six Massachusetts construction employers were misclassifying workers between 2017 and 2019, and their indirect estimate puts 9.5 to 15.8 percent of the industry's workforce in a fraudulent employment relationship. Cash pay, labor brokers, workers paid off the books: the study calls wage theft "a central feature" of how residential construction now operates. (UMass Amherst Labor Center)

Meanwhile the hours being stolen keep getting more valuable. Construction added 22,000 jobs in August, employment sits at 8.36 million, and average hourly earnings hit $41.66, per the September 4 BLS release. (BLS Employment Situation, August 2026) Production and nonsupervisory workers, the people actually on the tools, averaged $39.24 an hour in July, up 5.2 percent over the year. (AGC) Every hour shaved off a timesheet in 2026 is worth more than the same hour was in 2021, and stolen hours scale with the wage.

The Geofence Clock

Enter the apps. Rhumbix, founded by Navy veterans in 2014, has processed 3.29 million timecards and 105.1 million crew hours through its field app, capturing $4.4 billion in crew wages. Its marquee customer, Sprig Electric, runs 600 field workers and says it cut timekeeping and payroll processing by 360 hours, saving $36,000 a week. ClockShark sells GPS-verified time cards to smaller shops: $40 a month plus $8 per user on the standard plan, a virtual fence around each job site so workers can only clock in once they have arrived, location recorded only while they are clocked in. One customer, Caspar Building Systems, reports saving $12,000 a year on payroll alone. (Rhumbix; ClockShark)

What changes is the paper trail. Paper timesheets die, and with them the foreman's pencil shaving twenty minutes off Friday. Buddy punching dies, because the geofence knows you were not on site. Overtime becomes automatic instead of negotiated. A worker who used to reconstruct her week from memory on Sunday night now has a location-stamped record of every minute, generated by a phone in her own pocket.

Here is the honest tell, and credit to Rhumbix for saying it out loud early: workers hated being tracked. When the company pushed its location-based timekeeping a decade ago, adoption stalled on privacy, so it started anonymizing individual data and presenting it at the crew level. (ConstructionDive) The product only survived by admitting what it was. Every vendor in this space is selling the same bargain. We will protect your paycheck, and we will know where you stood.

The Math Nobody Ran

Methodology first, because the vendors will not show you this. ClockShark's standard plan costs $8 a month per user, which is $96 a year per worker before the $40 base fee. AGC's July figure for production-worker pay is $39.24 an hour. Divide $96 by $39.24 and the tool pays for itself if it recovers 2.45 hours of stolen wages per worker per year: two and a half hours, out of roughly two thousand.

Flip the calculation: a worker losing fifteen unrecorded minutes a day, the classic shave, over 250 workdays at $39.24 an hour, loses $2,452.50 a year. That is twenty-five times the per-worker cost of the app. Last year's average Labor Department recovery was $1,465 per worker, about 37 stolen hours at craft wages. At crew scale the arithmetic gets silly: a 20-person crew costs $200 a month, or $2,400 a year, on ClockShark's standard plan. One wage dispute at the DOL average, protecting just two workers, returns $2,930, which covers the subscription with money left over.

Assumptions, stated plainly: 250 workdays, straight time only, no overtime premium in the calculation, standard-tier pricing. Add time-and-a-half and the break-even drops under two hours. None of this is about the decimal places. The economics of stealing wages only work in the dark, and a $96-a-year flashlight ruins them.

The Case Against, at Full Strength

Now the part the vendor demos skip, starting with the bluntest objection. Every timekeeping database belongs to the employer, and in most wage-theft cases the employer is the defendant. A timekeeping app controlled by the thief is a lock the thief holds the key to, and no geofence changes who can edit, export, or quietly delete the records. Paper timesheets could be forged by a foreman with a pencil. Digital ones can be edited by an admin with a login, at scale, with an audit trail nobody outside the company ever sees.

Then the deeper limit: a geofence proves presence, and presence is not the crime. Misclassification is the dominant fraud in residential construction, costing workers $10,000 to $17,000 a year per the EPI math, and no time clock ever built can see it. A misclassified framer can clock in perfectly inside the geofence, work a flawless 40 hours, and still lose a sixth of his compensation to a 1099. An app solves the theft it can photograph and misses the theft that matters most.

Surveillance creep is real, not theoretical. That same device that proves you were on site also proves you took a 22-minute lunch, stood idle for nine minutes waiting on material, or left the site at 3:40 on a Friday. Productivity scoring and discipline ride on the same data feed as payroll protection, and nothing in the standard vendor contract I have seen draws a line between the two. Warehouse workers already know what happens when the productivity tool and the surveillance tool are the same device.

Finally, the coverage problem. Workers most vulnerable to wage theft are the least likely to opt into a location-tracking app. Undocumented workers paid in cash, the exact population the UMass researchers describe, will not hand a contractor a GPS record of their movements. Neither will anyone who has watched immigration enforcement use workplace data. A tool that only protects documented workers on legitimate payrolls leaves the worst of the theft exactly where it was.

My grandfather carried a union card for forty years, and he had a rule about any deal where one side brings the skill and the other brings the camera. The camera always ends up belonging to whoever signs the checks.

What This Analysis Could Not Prove

Limits, stated plainly. Vendor savings figures, Sprig Electric's $36,000 a week and Caspar's $12,000 a year, are self-reported company marketing with no independent audit. That $259 million covers all industries; the release does not break out construction's share of FY2025 recoveries. EPI's misclassification costs use 2021 earnings, and the UMass audit data covers 2017 to 2019 in Massachusetts, not the national industry in 2026. Rhumbix's anonymization claim dates to a 2016 interview, and I could not independently verify its current product behavior. ClockShark's pricing comes from the company's published FAQ and plan terms change. No independent audit exists of geofence clock-in accuracy: GPS drift, indoor work, and parking-lot clock-ins are all real failure modes nobody publishes error rates for.

What to Do Before the App Goes on Every Phone

Four moves, depending on where you stand.

If you run a residential crew and you are still on paper timesheets, run the break-even above with your own wage rates, then stop negotiating with yourself. At $96 a year per worker, the app is cheaper than one payroll dispute, and the DOL's enforcement numbers say disputes are not getting rarer. But put the data rules in writing before the pilot: who can view location history, whether it can be used for discipline or productivity scoring, and what happens to the data if you cancel. A vendor that will not put those answers in the contract has told you how it sees your people.

If you are the worker being handed the app, understand the trade you are making. Your hours are your money, and a location-stamped record is the strongest evidence you will ever have in a wage claim. Keep your own copy: screenshot your timesheets weekly, because the database belongs to the boss. If your pay is short, the Labor Department's helpline is 866-4US-WAGE, and the agency publishes a free timesheet app for iOS and Android so you can keep an independent record. (DOL) If your shop has a steward, this technology belongs in bargaining before deployment, not in a grievance after it.

If you are buying a home, none of this is abstract. Wage disputes on your project become liens, stop-work orders, and delays that land on your closing timeline. Ask your builder how its subcontractors track and pay hours. A builder who cannot answer that question clearly is telling you something about how the rest of the job is run.

And if you are one of the growing share of rookies on site, Bridgit's 2026 benchmark puts the average crew at 36.4 percent workers with less than a year of tenure, 56 percent on the biggest jobs, and the industry is on track to lose 41 percent of its workforce to retirement by 2031. (Bridgit via Bricks & Bytes) New hands are the ones least likely to keep their own time records and most likely to get shaved. The geofence is not your friend, but until the industry fixes the theft, it might be the only witness you have.
