Your Attic AC Makes 20 Gallons of Water a Day. Its Safety Pan Holds Six.

By Catherine Chen · September 24, 2026
← Back to articles

At 6:40 on a Friday evening in late August, the Hendersons locked the front door and drove to the lake for a three-day weekend. Nothing was wrong with the house; the thermostat sat at 74, the fridge hummed, and in the attic, their three-ton air handler did what three-ton air handlers do all summer: it wrung moisture out of the air and sent it down a 3/4-inch PVC line, about a gallon an hour, all weekend long. Somewhere around midnight, that line finished clogging. Slimy biofilm, the kind that grows in every dark wet pipe in America, sealed the last millimeter, and water started backing up into the primary pan until it found the edge. Nobody noticed, because nobody could have.

Monday afternoon the Hendersons came home to a collapsed section of master-bedroom ceiling, insulation hanging like wet laundry, and drywall mud running down the closet wall, with a restoration estimate of eleven thousand dollars and change. What the adjuster asks first is the question that decides everything in water claims: did this happen suddenly, or gradually? A clogged condensate line produces the wrong answer on purpose. It drips for hours, then pours, and by the time anyone sees it, the whole event reads as "gradual." Gradual is where claims go to be denied.

Code knew first

Here is the part that should bother you. This failure mode is not a surprise, an edge case, or a gap in the standards. Section M1411.3.1 of the International Residential Code, in substantially its current form for years, requires a secondary drain or auxiliary drain pan for every cooling coil "where damage to any building components will occur as a result of overflow from the equipment drain pan or stoppage in the condensate drain piping." An attic air handler over a finished bedroom is the textbook case. Three compliance paths follow in the same section: an auxiliary pan with its own drain line terminating at a conspicuous point, so the occupants see the drip; a separate overflow drain connected to the equipment pan at a higher level than the primary; or an auxiliary pan with a UL 508 water-level detection device that shuts the equipment off before the pan overflows.

Notice what method one and two have in common. That secondary drain has to discharge somewhere the homeowner will notice, over a window, by a door, anywhere conspicuous. This is not a backup pipe for its own sake. It is an alarm. A drip from the secondary line is the building saying "the primary is clogged, call someone, today." An elegant little piece of regulatory design, really: no batteries, no Wi-Fi, just water falling somewhere you will see it. Which is why it is so depressing how often the field defeats it.

How the field defeats it

Spend an evening on the inspector forums and a pattern emerges. Certified inspectors keep finding the coil's secondary drain port capped at the unit, plugged with the little plastic cap it shipped with, while the auxiliary pan sits beneath it doing the whole job alone. One veteran inspector on the InterNACHI board reports that many installers leave the secondary plugged and consider the job code-compliant, and the counterargument from the floor is worth hearing at full strength: with the secondary sealed, overflow water does not politely reroute into the pan. It rises inside the coil cabinet, seeps from the metal joints, and gets picked up by the blower and sprayed into the ductwork as humidity, which means you get mold in the ducts and condensation at the windows weeks before you get a single drop in the pan. A pan-only installation does not fail loudly. It fails by relocating the water to places you will find last.

Missing from most of these attics: the switch. Method three, the pan plus a UL 508 water-level detection device, is cheap to install and devastating to skip. A pan with a switch shuts the air conditioner off when the water rises; the Hendersons would have come home to a warm house and a full pan instead of a collapsed ceiling. Distributor pricing I checked this week puts the common options at $13.52 for a clip-on pan switch, $21.08 for a pipe-mounted elbow switch, and $25.53 for the RectorSeal pan switch. Thirteen dollars and fifty-two cents buys the device the code imagined, the one that was not there.

Doing the weekend-flood math

Headlines earn their numbers, so here are the receipts. A residential air conditioner produces roughly 0.8 to 1.2 gallons of condensate per hour per ton under moderate humidity. Take the low end, 0.8, times three tons, times eight hours of runtime on a hot day: 19.2 gallons per day. That is a conservative number in a humid climate and an absurd one in a dry one, but for the Gulf Coast, the Southeast, and the mid-Atlantic it is unremarkable.

Now the pan. Code minimum for an auxiliary pan is 1.5 inches deep and 3 inches larger than the unit in each dimension. Assume a 24-by-24-inch air handler, a plausible footprint for a three-ton horizontal unit, which makes the pan 30 by 30 inches. Volume: 900 square inches times 1.5 inches is 1,350 cubic inches, and at 231 cubic inches per gallon that is 5.85 gallons. A pan built exactly to code holds just under six gallons.

Divide. At 0.8 gallons per hour, six gallons fills in seven and a half hours of runtime, less than one summer day. Once the line clogs on a Friday night, the pan starts overflowing sometime Saturday, and by Monday the system has produced roughly three days of condensate, on the order of 45 to 60 gallons, straight through the ceiling. Installed correctly but missing only the switch, the code-minimum safety device buys you one afternoon. That is the math the Hendersons never did, and honestly, why would they? A pan like that looks like protection. Seven hours of runtime is what it actually is.

What this means for your buildDo not mistake the pan for the protection. The switch, or the conspicuous secondary drain, is the protection. If you are building, spec all three layers: secondary drain line to a visible termination, auxiliary pan, and a UL 508 float switch in the pan wired to the control circuit. If you are buying, the attic photo that matters is not the pretty one of the unit. Get the one of the drain.

Why the claim is worse than the water

Water damage and freezing account for 22.6 percent of all homeowners insurance claims, second only to wind and hail, with an average payout of $15,400 over the 2019-2023 window, according to Insurance Information Institute data. It is also, by industry estimates, the claim category with the highest denial rate of any type, roughly 10 percent, and the denials cluster around one distinction: sudden versus gradual. Standard policies cover the burst pipe but not the slow seep you should have maintained.

A condensate flood lands exactly on that fault line, which is the part of this story I find genuinely unfair. No homeowner watches slime grow inside a sealed PVC line in a dark attic, and cutting pipe is the only way anyone could, yet the event unfolds over hours or days, the damage accrues incrementally, and the adjuster's framework has no category for "sudden to the water, gradual to the ceiling." Prevention that costs $13.52 is not just cheaper than a claim. It is cheaper than the argument about whether you deserved one.

What the camera catches, and what it cannot

The hopeful part of this story, and the reason AI belongs in it, is that the installation defects are all visible. A phone photo in the attic before drywall, or a home inspector's camera at purchase, can check seven things in under two minutes: is there a trap, with a seal at least two inches deep and a cleanout that opens; is there a secondary pan under every horizontal attic coil; is the pan at least 1.5 inches deep, three inches larger than the unit, and corrosion-resistant; is there a float switch in the pan or on the secondary port, wired to the control circuit; does the drain line run continuously downhill at 1/8 inch per foot with no bellies where standing water breeds slime; does the secondary line terminate somewhere conspicuous, over a window or door, and not inside a wall; and is the trap actually wet, not evaporated dry over the winter. Every one of those is a yes-or-no question answerable from a photograph. So consistent is the defect pattern that a vision model trained on a few hundred attic installs could probably flag the missing switch the way it flags a missing smoke detector.

Intellectual honesty requires the other half of that story, though: no camera sees inside the drain line. Biofilm and algae growing in standing water inside the pipe are the number one clog cause, and they are invisible to every inspection method short of cutting the line open, which means the photo audit catches installation defects and nothing about maintenance state. The line still needs its annual flush regardless, and vinegar tablets or pan strips still need quarterly replacement. A homeowner who photographs a perfect install and then ignores the drain for five years has bought a false sense of security with excellent documentation. Treat the camera as a supplement to maintenance, not a substitute, and anyone selling it as more is selling something else.

A fair hearing for the defense

Fairness demands the installers' side, and it is stronger than the code absolutists admit. In dry climates, condensate volumes are trivial, sometimes under a gallon a day, and secondary protection looks like code theater written for Florida. Traps sized to the book, with seal depth at twice the plenum pressure per the engineering guidance, are fiddly to build in cramped attics, and a trap that dries out over the heating season admits unconditioned air through the drain line all winter, which is its own efficiency penalty. Some old hands will tell you the secondary port plug stays in because an open secondary piped to the pan just gives the water a second path to nowhere when the pan itself has no drain.

There is also the honest version of the cost argument. On a production build with four hundred units, a $25 switch plus the labor to wire it is real money, and the builder who installs it eats the cost while the benefit accrues to a homeowner who will never know it was there. That is a genuine collective-action problem, the kind the code exists to solve by making it mandatory, and the fact that it is mandatory and still routinely skipped tells you how thin inspection coverage is at rough-in. Nothing in the code is wrong. Enforcement is the gap.

What we did not prove

We did not measure how often secondary ports are left plugged or switches omitted; the inspector-forum evidence is vivid but anecdotal, and no national study counts these defects. My 5.85-gallon pan calculation assumes a 24-by-24-inch unit and code-minimum margins; real units and pans vary, and larger pans buy more time. Nineteen-point-two gallons per day uses the low end of the per-ton range at eight hours of runtime; actual production swings with humidity, setpoint, and duty cycle, and in arid markets the whole scenario is nearly moot. That $15,400 average claim covers all water damage, not condensate floods specifically, and the 10 percent denial figure is an industry estimate, not a published actuarial table. Prices for float switches are current distributor listings and move with the market.

Seven photos

Here is the actionable part, and it fits on an index card. If you are building: add seven photos to the pre-drywall checklist, one for each item in the audit above, dated and tied to the unit. Cost is zero. The superintendent's phone is already up there.

If you are buying: before closing, get into the attic, or hire the person who will, and find the air handler. Photograph the drain line from the pan to wherever it goes. Look for the trap, the pan, and above all the little device that shuts the machine off when the water rises. If the secondary port is plugged and there is no switch, you have found a $25 part standing between you and an eleven-thousand-dollar ceiling, and you have found it at the only moment when someone else still has to install it.

Names in this story are a composite; the math is not. Somewhere tonight, in an attic in Houston or Atlanta or Charlotte, a PVC line is halfway clogged, and the pan below it holds six gallons against a machine that makes twenty a day. Code saw this coming years ago and wrote three answers. Two of them are probably in your attic already. The third costs thirteen dollars.
