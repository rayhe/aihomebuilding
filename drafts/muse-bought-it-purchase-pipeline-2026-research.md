# Research: Muse Bought It — What Happens Between "Buy It" and the Charge

**Slug:** muse-bought-it-purchase-pipeline-2026
**Journalist:** Marcus Washington (real people, real workflows, human impact)
**Date:** November 7, 2026
**Angle:** Muse can now spend your money. Not recommend, not link you to a checkout page — actually complete the purchase while you are doing something else. The machinery that makes that safe is specific: a one-time card number, a separate approval process called Sentinel, and a tap on your phone. The machinery that decides who pays when it goes wrong is also specific, and it is not on your side. This article walks a single purchase through the whole pipeline — the three rails Muse can pay on, the approval tap where liability changes hands, and a worked dollar ledger for a purchase that goes wrong — so a reader can decide, before the first order, which rail to use and what to never let the agent buy.

## Kill test
Passes. A reader deciding whether to let an agent spend money needs to know, before the first charge, exactly what happens between their words and their bank statement: which payment rail is used, what the approval tap actually authorizes, and who eats the loss when the agent buys the wrong thing. This is the decision that turns a demo into a financial relationship.

## Differentiation from prior coverage
Site article "muse-who-pays-when-your-agent-fails-2026" covered liability in general. This one is different: it is the purchase pipeline itself, step by step, with the three payment rails compared side by side and a worked dollar ledger for one wrong purchase. The liability findings are the ending, not the subject.

## Primary sources

1. **Stripe, "Stripe helps Muse, Meta's new personal AI agent, shop across the internet with Link"** (stripe.com/newsroom/news/stripe-helps-meta-muse-shop-with-link, September 8, 2026) — the primary payment source. Key facts: at more than 1 million businesses that accept Link, Muse checks out instantly using the US consumer's preferred payment method saved in Link; for other businesses, "Link issues Muse a single-use virtual card scoped to the approved purchase"; "For every purchase, consumers are asked to approve the transaction total directly in the chat interface, and Muse never sees their underlying payment details." Link has 300 million users globally per Stripe. Link's wallet for agents was introduced earlier in 2026 and also powers Grok Bot and Instinct.
2. **Meta, Muse Supplemental Terms of Service** (muse.ai/terms, last updated September 8, 2026) — as reported by vr.org and eesel.ai from the terms text. Key facts (Section 7.7, paraphrased in reporting): the user "bear[s] all risk of financial loss arising from such actions" and is "solely responsible for setting appropriate limits and safeguards (including transaction limits and category restrictions)"; "certain transactions may be irreversible by their nature"; Meta's aggregate liability, including for claims arising from Muse "executing unintended transactions," is capped at the greater of $250 or what the user paid Meta in the preceding twelve months. Meta's help page states the same rule in plain language: "You're responsible for all transactions your Muse makes on your behalf."
3. **Stripe, Link Agentic Terms** — as reported by top5apps.ai from Stripe's terms. Key facts: "you are responsible for all Agent-initiated transactions as if you had taken the action yourself," including transactions "caused by bugs, hallucinations and/or misinterpretations" or that "differ from your provided instructions." Agent purchases are explicitly excluded from Link's unauthorized-transaction protection.
4. **Meta, Muse safety design post by Tarek Sheasha, Meta Superintelligence Labs** (September 8, 2026) — as reported by vr.org. Key facts: the payment credential "is tied to that particular merchant, a particular dollar amount, and only valid for a limited period of time." A second route, for sites where the user's card is already on file, has Muse detect the checkout page and ask for approval "with the exact details of the purchase every time."
5. **Meta, Muse Help Center, "How Muse works with payments"** (meta.com/help) — as reported by trucommerce.ai. Key facts: two methods listed — Link by Stripe, which "keeps your payments safe by issuing a one-time card number for each purchase, so your real card number stays private," with Link coverage for "damages, theft, loss, price drops and returns" on eligible purchases; or Muse logging in on the user's behalf using credentials saved in the Secure Credential Store and paying with a saved card, with approval requested before completing a purchase. "All orders are fulfilled by the merchant that you purchase from."
6. **Link purchase protections (XCover, via Stripe/Link)** — as reported by vr.org and top5apps.ai. Limits: up to $500 per claim for damage, loss and theft; $250 for no-fee returns; $500 for price protection; $1,000 under the refund guarantee. Excluded from all four: services, tickets, travel, food orders, software — which is most of what agent demos actually buy.
7. **Shopify / Shop Pay** — announced September 21, 2026 by Shopify CEO Tobi Lütke; live as a wallet in Muse September 24, 2026. Shopify added Meta as an AI channel in Agentic Storefronts; Muse searches Shopify's product catalogs and completes checkout with Shop Pay using saved billing, shipping, and payment information. "There are no fees associated with selling in Meta's direct checkout. You pay only your standard payment processing fees."
8. **PayPal** — joined September 22, 2026 (PYMNTS): Muse users can check out through PayPal at PayPal merchants. Meta's own cut: Meta said it expects, over time, to take "a small fee from transactions" — no rate, no start date, no scope, no statement of who pays.
9. **Amazon block** — September 20, 2026, twelve days after launch: Amazon blocked Muse purchases, saying access by an unauthorized AI agent violates Amazon's Conditions of Use; Amazon's stated reasons were that Meta did not notify it, the agent does not identify itself, and it appeared to store customer credentials.
10. **Meta Connector Platform docs** — as reported by eesel.ai: a purchase is classed as a "sensitive write," and "Sensitive writes cannot be set to Always allow." Meta is "not currently approving new financial money movement tools, such as transfers between financial accounts." There is no per-purchase cap, no monthly budget, and no category block anywhere in Muse's settings or help pages; the per-purchase approval tap is the only spending control.
11. **The wider protocol race (context):** Visa Intelligent Commerce and Trusted Agent Protocol (announced April 30, 2025; TAP launched October 2025 with Cloudflare); Mastercard Agent Pay (launched April 29, 2025, Agentic Tokens, agents must be registered and verified); Google Agent Payments Protocol AP2 (announced September 16, 2025, 60+ organizations); OpenAI/Stripe Agentic Commerce Protocol with Shared Payment Tokens. ## Original contribution: the three-rail comparison and the wrong-purchase ledger

Novel analysis for this article: I compared the three rails Muse can pay on — Link one-time card, merchant-saved card via the user's own login, and Shop Pay — on five questions that decide what a buyer actually risks, and I worked a single wrong purchase ($1,200, the wrong night, non-refundable — the shape of a real hotel-booking failure reported for another agent) through each layer of protection to show, in dollars, who pays what.

| Question | Link one-time card | Merchant-saved card (own login) | Shop Pay |
|---|---|---|---|
| Does Muse see your real card number? | No — one-time number, merchant- and amount-scoped, time-limited | No — Muse uses the saved credential, never sees the number | No — Shop Pay holds the payment details |
| Does the merchant know an agent bought it? | No — looks like an ordinary card authorization | No — looks like an ordinary logged-in order | Yes — order attributed to Meta in Shopify admin |
| What protects you if the goods arrive damaged or never arrive? | Link purchase protections: up to $500 damage/loss/theft, $1,000 refund guarantee | Merchant's own return policy and your card's chargeback rights | Shopify/merchant policy and your card's chargeback rights |
| What protects you if Muse bought the wrong thing? | Nothing in Link's terms — agent purchases are yours "whether intended or not" | Nothing — same rule, and no Link protections attach | Nothing — approval was given, liability is the buyer's |
| Spending limit other than your approval tap? | None | None | None |

Headline finding: **the approval tap is the entire budget.** There is no per-purchase cap, no monthly budget, and no category block in Muse. Meta's terms hand the job of setting limits to the user, and Stripe says expanded controls are "planned," not shipped. Every purchase needs a fresh tap, and a purchase is a "sensitive write" that cannot be set to Always allow — that is the one hard guardrail in the whole system — but nothing stops you from tapping Allow forty times in a week, and nothing totals those taps for you.

Second finding: **the wrong-purchase ledger.** Take a $1,200 hotel night, wrong date, non-refundable — the exact failure shape reported for a rival agent's restaurant booking with a $200 cancellation charge. Meta's liability cap is the greater of $250 or what you paid Meta in the last twelve months, so on the $20/month plan that cap is $250, and it covers only claims against Meta, not the merchant's charge. Link's purchase protections do not apply: services and travel are excluded from all four protections, and agent-initiated transactions are excluded from unauthorized-transaction protection. The merchant keeps the $1,200 unless its own policy allows a change. Your card's chargeback rights are the last resort, and a chargeback for "I approved it but the agent picked the wrong night" is a weak dispute — you did authorize the transaction, and the authorization record will show it. The ledger: merchant $1,200 kept, Meta at most $250 in theory and $0 in practice for a merchant's non-refundable policy, Link $0, you $1,200 minus whatever the merchant concedes. The approval tap is not a safety net. It is the moment liability transfers to you.

Third finding: **the rails are not equal, and the best one is the default.** The Link one-time card is the only rail that adds anything beyond your card's normal protections — the $500/$250/$500/$1,000 goods protections — and it is the only rail where the credential itself is scoped to one merchant, one amount, and a limited time, so a leaked or misused number cannot be spent twice. The merchant-saved-card rail adds nothing: same liability, no Link protections, and the purchase is indistinguishable from one you made yourself. Shop Pay adds merchant consent — Shopify opted in where Amazon opted out — but no extra buyer protection beyond the card. Practical rule: pay through Link, keep the wallet thin (one card with a modest limit, not your primary account), and never let the agent buy services, tickets, or travel on a rail where the protections do not reach.

## The strongest counterargument
The approval tap is a genuine control, and it is stronger than what most shopping already gives you: every single purchase requires a fresh human decision with the exact total shown, which is more friction than one-click ordering, saved cards, and subscriptions that renew silently for years. The liability language is also standard — every card network's terms make the cardholder responsible for authorized transactions, and "you approved it" has always been the line. The counterargument has force, but it fails on two points. First, an approval tap is only as good as the attention behind it, and an agent that prepares forty checkouts a week trains its user to tap Allow the way people click through cookie banners — the control degrades with use, and nothing in the system measures that degradation. Second, the old rule assumed the person tapping had done the choosing. When the agent chooses the merchant, the size, the date, and the total, and the human only confirms, the human is authorizing a decision they did not make. The law has not caught up to that distinction yet, and the terms are written as if it never will.

## Limitations
- Meta's Supplemental Terms and Stripe's Link Agentic Terms were read through secondary reporting (vr.org, eesel.ai, top5apps.ai, trucommerce.ai) that quotes them directly; I did not independently fetch the full terms text in this research pass. Direct quotes used in the article are the ones these sources quote verbatim and that agree across at least two of them.
- The $1,200 wrong-night ledger is a worked example built from the reported terms and protection limits, not a real Muse transaction. No Muse user has publicly documented a wrong purchase of this size with a dollar outcome; the restaurant-booking failure cited is from a rival agent (Instinct), reported in the Substack piece.
- Link purchase-protection limits are as listed by Link via XCover and reported by vr.org/top5apps.ai; eligibility conditions and claim caps per year were not fully verified.
- Juniper Research's $8 billion / $1.5 trillion forecast is a vendor forecast, not measured spend; actual 2026 agentic transaction volume is not publicly reported.
- Meta's "small fee from transactions" has no published rate, date, scope, or payer; any claim about what Muse purchases cost the merchant beyond standard processing is speculation.

## Actionable takeaways for the reader
1. Pay through Link's one-time card, never through a saved card on a merchant login — it is the only rail that adds protection and the only credential that cannot be spent twice.
2. Keep the Link wallet thin: one card with a modest limit, not your primary card or a debit card tied to your checking account.
3. Read the approval total every time. That tap is the moment liability transfers to you, and it is the only spending control that exists.
4. Do not let Muse buy services, tickets, travel, or food on your behalf yet — Link's purchase protections exclude all of them, and those are the purchases where "wrong" costs the most.
5. After any agent purchase, check the merchant confirmation email and your statement the same day; a wrong purchase caught in hours is a phone call, caught in weeks is a chargeback fight.
6. If a store blocks Muse the way Amazon did, do not work around it — the block is the merchant saying it does not consent to agent purchases, and buying anyway puts you outside every protection in the stack.
