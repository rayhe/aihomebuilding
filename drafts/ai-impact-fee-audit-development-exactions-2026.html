Fremont Adds $150,000 in Fees to Every New Home. UC Berkeley Needed Months to Figure Out Why.

By Catherine Chen, September 16, 2026, Policy & Regulation

In the summer of 2017, researchers at UC Berkeley sat down to do something simple: add up the development fees on a new home in seven California cities. Months later they were still interviewing planning officials, cobbling together piecemeal schedules across departments, revising their spreadsheets, and discovering that the people who administer the fees could not always explain how the fees were set. Their conclusion: the fees were "extremely difficult to estimate."

That was a team of professional researchers. Now imagine you are a builder with a construction loan clock running, a framing crew booked for Monday, and a permit technician telling you the fee estimate needs another two weeks. The meter is running.

What they eventually produced should embarrass everyone involved. In Fremont, total development fees added roughly $150,000 to the cost of each new home, according to the Terner Center's seven-city study. A later Terner analysis of 691 affordable-housing projects built between 2020 and 2023 found impact fees averaging nearly $20,000 per unit, with 134 projects assessed more than $30,000 per unit, for a statewide total of about $300 million a year paid by affordable developments alone (full brief).

Here is the part that should interest anyone holding a fee invoice, because the invoice in your hand was probably assembled under exactly these conditions. The researchers found that fees are usually set without oversight or coordination between city departments, that the type and size of fees vary wildly from city to city, and that "projects are often subject to additional exactions not codified in any fee schedule." Translate that out of academic prose: the bill you just paid may contain line items that do not appear in any document the city has published. The code does not care about your feelings, but it does require the city to show its math, which is the entire point of the Mitigation Fee Act’s nexus requirement, a statute written precisely because cities used to charge whatever they wanted and call it impact.

Under California's Mitigation Fee Act, impact fees must have a documented nexus to the development and be roughly proportional to its impact. That covers parks, traffic, drainage, affordable housing exactions, the visible machinery of growth paying for growth, which is the theory, and the theory is sound even when the administration is a mess. It does not cover school fees or utility connection fees, which ride on separate statutory authority and separate invoices, which is exactly why a single project can accumulate fee bills from four different offices that have never spoken to one another. Terner's team documented that fragmentation in 2018, and it has not improved.

This is where the machines enter: GreenLite, an AI-powered private plan review firm, raised a $49.5 million Series B in September 2025, claims its system accelerates permit timelines by 75 percent, says it is authorized in roughly 2,000 jurisdictions, and is expanding into residential development. Nearly a quarter of states have advanced private-provider plan review legislation in the past three years, which means the legal infrastructure for third-party review is arriving faster than the municipal staffing crisis is resolving, and the vendors know it. The sales pitch promises plan review in days instead of months, which is the sentence that gets the funding rounds, and it is aimed at builders who have waited ninety days for a stamp. Quieter, and more important for fee bills, is the fact that these systems ingest the plan set, the local code, and the fee schedule into one database. A machine that has actually read the entire stack of schedules can do something no human clerk reliably does: check every line of your invoice against the schedule that supposedly produced it.

Which suggests an obvious discipline that almost nobody practices, which is strange, because the same builders who would never pay a subcontractor invoice without a line-item breakdown pay the city’s invoice without asking for one: the fee bill audit. Before you pay, you demand the line-item breakdown with a published schedule citation for every line, because a fee without a citation is a rumor with an invoice number, and rumors should not be paid. You check the schedule's effective date, because fee schedules typically update every July 1 and a bill computed on a superseded schedule is appealable on that ground alone. You flag any line item with no citation at all, because Terner proved those exist, and an uncited line is either a mistake or an exaction nobody voted on, both of which deserve a question. And you run the ADU tripwire: under Government Code section 65852.2, accessory dwelling units under 750 square feet are exempt from impact fees entirely, and larger ADUs pay only fees proportional to the primary dwelling. A fee bill charging full freight on a 640-square-foot ADU contradicts the statute on its face, which makes it the single easiest line item in California residential construction to challenge, and the one most often paid without question. Software that reads both the plan set and the schedule catches that in seconds, which a human scanning a multi-page invoice does not.

There is also a cost nobody puts on the pro forma, which is fitting, because the process of learning what you owe somehow costs money before you owe anything: the cost of figuring out the fees. Terner's researchers needed months and repeated interviews across departments to assemble totals for seven cities, which tells you everything about the experience awaiting a solo builder who just wants a number before Friday. Scale that down to one builder estimating fees on one project and you still get 40 to 80 hours of staff or consultant time chasing schedules, calling departments, and reconciling conflicting answers. At a $150-an-hour blended consultant rate, the price of estimating the fees runs $6,000 to $12,000 before a single fee is paid. That is my arithmetic, not a measured survey, and I am labeling it as such, but the direction is undeniable, and if anything the estimate is conservative, because it prices the consultant’s hours without pricing the delay, the carrying cost, or the decisions made blind while waiting for a number. Opacity is a tax, and it is levied before the official taxes begin, which makes it the only tax on the project that nobody debates at a council meeting.

Skepticism is warranted about the tools, too, because GreenLite's 75 percent acceleration figure is a vendor claim, and its residential expansion was announced as a plan, not a delivered product. Municipalities have every incentive to keep fee administration exactly as opaque as Terner found it, because fee revenue is load-bearing in city budgets and an audit machine threatens the slop in the system. Expect slow data access, PDFs that defeat text extraction, and schedules posted as scanned images, because nothing says transparency like a fee schedule that has been photographed, printed, and photographed again. The same opacity the researchers documented will fight the tools trying to pierce it. Count on it.

And the strongest case against the audit-everything posture deserves a full hearing. Impact fees exist because new homes genuinely burden parks, roads, water systems, and schools, and the Mitigation Fee Act's nexus requirement is real law, not decoration. A city that cannot fund infrastructure shifts the cost to existing residents, which is its own unfairness, and it is worth saying so plainly, because the alternative to impact fees is not free infrastructure but the same bill arriving through property taxes. Auditing a fee bill is not the same as arguing fees should be zero, and anyone who treats it that way will lose the appeal and deserve to. The honest position is narrower: pay what the published schedule says you owe, to the penny, and not a dollar more. Nothing more. Precision cuts both ways: it also protects the city's legitimate revenue from the suspicion that the whole schedule is negotiable.

What this analysis does not prove, stated plainly, is this: no public dataset measures how often residential fee bills contain errors, so I am not claiming a specific error rate; the audit checklist is built from the schedule mechanics Terner documented, not from a measured sample of bad bills. The $6,000-to-$12,000 opacity tax is my model applied to Terner's process description. GreenLite's figures are vendor claims with thin independent verification. And nothing here is legal advice: Mitigation Fee Act challenges are fact-specific, appeal windows are short, and the Government Code section cited above has subdivisions and exceptions that a two-paragraph summary cannot capture. Talk to a land-use attorney before you withhold payment on anything, because the appeal window on a fee protest is measured in days in some jurisdictions, and a missed deadline converts a winnable challenge into a donation.

What you can do this week, without any software at all:

  • Demand the line-item fee breakdown with a published schedule citation per line, in writing, before you pay.
  • Check the schedule's effective date against your bill; a superseded schedule makes an appealable bill.
  • Run the ADU tripwire. Under 750 square feet means no impact fees, full stop.
  • Challenge first the line items with no schedule citation. Terner found them; your city has them too.
  • Ask whether your jurisdiction accepts private plan review. A reviewer whose software itemizes fees as part of the package is worth more than the permit acceleration alone, because the permit gets you permission while the itemization gets you the number you can actually challenge, and the challenge is where the money is.

The code is not self-executing. It never was. Somebody has to read the schedule, and for the first time, that somebody does not have to be you.
