The Cheapest Bid Is a Scope Gap With a Signature. AI Bid Leveling Catches What Excel Misses.

By Frank DeLuca · September 7, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

It is Tuesday afternoon in the job trailer, and four electrical bids are fanned across the desk like a losing poker hand: $118,400, $104,900, $97,200, $86,500.

Circling the cheapest one is what the owner wants to hear about on a fixed-price job, which is why nobody in the trailer flinches three months later when the electrician's invoice arrives carrying a $31,000 change order for lighting controls and temporary power. He is too tired to be surprised. He has seen this exact movie more times than the concessions stand has seen him.

What happened in that trailer is not corruption or incompetence, though both keep addresses in this business. It is a failure of comparison. Bid leveling is the process of normalizing subcontractor quotes into an apples-to-apples comparison, and it is the most grueling step in preconstruction: days of transposing unstructured PDFs into spreadsheet rows, hunting for what each sub quietly excluded, trying to remember which allowance was a real number and which was a wish. Trade press calls the manual version a breeding ground for human error, and twenty years of watching projects go sideways has not convinced me the trade press is wrong, which is the most backhanded compliment I have ever paid a journalist.

Where the Money Actually Leaks

A sub whose quote excludes temporary power, panel labeling, or lighting controls wins on price. Full stop. Then he bills the difference back as change orders, at margins that would make a bookmaker blush. Dodge Data, via Rhumbix, puts the national average change-order rate at 10 percent of contract value, with some projects hitting 25 percent. Rework alone runs 5 to 10 percent of project value on a typical job, per PlanRadar's 2025 synthesis, and across the US economy Dodge estimates rework and delays cost roughly $177 billion a year, about 5 percent of all construction spending. On a residential project running $800,000, that is real money wearing a fake mustache.

None of this is news to anyone who has ever signed a subcontract. The leak is not in the field; the estimator lost $31,000 on Tuesday, at the desk, in the five minutes he spent circling a number instead of the hour he should have spent reading page nine of the low bid, where the exclusions always live, printed small and priced large.

The Machines Read Page Nine

This is the exact step where a new class of AI tools is setting up shop. On September 1, 2026, STACK Construction Technologies launched STACK IQ, a conversational AI for estimating and preconstruction that, among other things, audits an estimate for missing items before the bid goes out the door. Togal.AI reads plans and produces auto-measured reports in minutes. Beam AI pairs machine takeoff with human quality control and turns concrete and rebar quantities around in 24 to 72 hours in spreadsheet-ready format. ConWize and a growing bench of competitors are building the same idea from the other direction: ingest the ugly PDF quotes, output a side-by-side scope comparison where the exclusions have nowhere to hide.

Understand what the pitch actually is: it is not that the AI is smarter than your estimator; it is that the AI is tireless in exactly the way estimators are not, which is the only kind of superiority that matters at 6 p.m. on bid day, when the coffee is cold and the owner wants a number. It will read all four PDFs and flag that Bid 4 excludes lighting controls. It will notice that Bid 2's allowance for fixtures is $4,000 lower than the other three, which is not a bargain but a typo wearing a disguise. It does the transposition labor, the part of the job nobody ever got into construction to do.

The Math, With the Caveats Attached

Run the arithmetic on a typical residential GC contract and the stakes get concrete. An $800,000 contract at the 10 percent average change-order rate carries $80,000 in changes, and if even one-quarter of those changes trace back to scope gaps a properly leveled bid tab would have caught, which is my conservative supposition rather than anyone's measured figure, that is $20,000 per project recoverable by doing the comparison right.

I want to be transparent about that quarter, because the whole point of this article is that unexamined numbers are where the trouble starts, and it is my assumption rather than anyone's measured figure. No study I can find cleanly separates scope-gap change orders from owner-directed changes or unforeseen conditions, and I would distrust one that claimed to. Dodge measured the 10 percent; that quarter is mine. Use it anyway, with the caveat attached, which is more than most vendor pitch decks will do for you.

What the Demo Will Not Tell You

Before anyone reaches for a credit card, the caveats deserve the same attention the math got. STACK IQ was six days old when I wrote this sentence, which means no independent verification of its bid-auditing accuracy exists and the customer quotes in the launch announcement are vendor-supplied, selected by the company that makes the product. Most pricing in this category is quote-based and varies across trade press reports, so I cannot give you a reliable per-seat number, and on the question that matters most to the small GC, whether residential estimators are actually adopting these tools, the honest answer is that the data does not exist and adoption claims are anecdotal. I have been in this business long enough to treat a launch-week press release the way I treat a sub who says his crew starts Monday: with hope, and a backup plan.

The Objection, at Full Strength

Now the strongest case against all of this, stated at full strength. Bid leveling software does not fix the incentive problem underneath the spreadsheet. Owners still award to the low bidder, and the low bidder is very often low precisely because he left something out. An AI that perfectly identifies the scope gap does not stop a GC under margin pressure from accepting the cheap bid anyway and planning to manage the gap in the field, which is a polite way of saying he plans to win the fight later at markup.

Worse, perfect leveling transparency can become a weapon pointed at the user, because once subs learn exactly which line items get scrutinized, the exclusions migrate into allowances and alternates, where no comparison table reaches them. Software levels what it can see. It cannot see what the sub decided not to write down. And for the residential GC running two to four projects a year, the bid volume may never justify a subscription or provide enough historical data to train anything on. It improves the spreadsheet; it does not change the poker game.

What This Article Did Not Prove

The limitations, stated plainly. I did not test any of these tools hands-on, and I could not verify STACK's accuracy claims against an independent benchmark, because none exists yet. My $20,000-per-project figure is arithmetic built on one measured input and one assumption, clearly labeled above. I did not measure how often leveled bid tabs actually change award decisions in practice, which is the number that would really settle this. Every sub quoted in that marketing is a vendor success story. Everything in the evidence section is secondhand, and the part of the story I care about most, whether better leveling survives contact with a low-bid owner, is the part nobody has measured at all.

What to Do With This

If you are a GC or builder: before awarding any trade package over roughly $50,000, produce a leveled bid tab, line items as rows, subs as columns, exclusions flagged in a color that embarrasses someone. AI cuts the transposition labor, but the discipline of requiring standardized scope sheets from your subs costs nothing and works even in Excel. Start there. Software is the accelerant; the scope sheet is the fuel.

If you are a homeowner on a fixed-price contract: ask your GC whether the sub bids were leveled, and whether you can see the exclusions list. A bid $30,000 under the next competitor with six exclusions is not cheaper. It is a $30,000 change order wearing a low number. Asking costs nothing, and the answer tells you, in about four seconds, how your builder handles the Tuesday-afternoon scene in the trailer.

If you are a sub: standardize your own proposal format against the GC's scope sheet. Subs who make their bids easy to level win more work from good GCs, because estimators remember who wasted their Tuesday, and they remember who didn't. In a market where AI is reading your proposal line by line, the cleanest bid is also the most quotable one.

I have watched this industry buy software for twenty years, and I have watched the same Tuesday-afternoon scene play out in trailers from Fresno to Fort Lauderdale. In the end, the tools that last are not the ones with the best demos but the ones that attack the step where the money is actually leaking, which right now is the gap between four PDFs that nobody had time to compare properly. Level the bids. Read page nine. Read the exclusions. Circle the real number.
