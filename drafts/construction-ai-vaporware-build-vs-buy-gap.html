Half of Builders Say They Use AI. Fewer Than 5% Use It to Build Anything.

By Jake Kowalski · August 1, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Dustin Burns sat down one afternoon with his company's historical cost data, a regional benchmarking dataset, and a large language model. Four hours later he had a working web application that pulled real project costs, compared them against regional averages, and generated conceptual estimates a project manager could actually use. One HTML file.

Burns is Senior Vice President of Technology and Business Solutions at McCownGordon Construction, a Kansas City-based general contractor that has grown from 30 employees to over a billion dollars in annual revenue. He builds hospitals, police headquarters, and university science centers across four states, and he knows what construction software looks like from inside a company that operates at that scale and scrutiny level.

His verdict on what he built in an afternoon, shared on the Bricks and Bytes podcast in July 2026: "I can make you something that looks like it's really expensive, but it took me four hours." Then: "And it is vaporware." Not his tool specifically. His point was that from the outside, the four-hour prototype is indistinguishable from what vendors sell at conference booths for thousands per month, and his filter for separating real construction AI from dressed-up demos is whether the company behind it is building its own models or wrapping someone else's API.

49% Share of single-family builders who say they use AI "in some capacity," per the NAHB/Wells Fargo Housing Market Index survey. Fewer than 5% use it for anything beyond marketing copy and emails.

A Number Everyone Cites and Nobody Interrogates

Every quarter, NAHB and Wells Fargo run a Housing Market Index survey that has become the default barometer for builder sentiment. In 2026, that survey reported 49% of single-family builders use AI in some capacity, a figure that gets repeated at HBA chapter meetings, in vendor pitch decks, and across trade publications as evidence that residential construction is halfway through a technology transformation.

Look at what that 49% are actually doing. Twenty percent use AI for marketing materials. Eleven percent for market analysis or project planning. Fewer than 5% use it for any of ten other business functions the survey asked about, which means most of that headline figure consists of builders typing listing descriptions and Instagram captions into ChatGPT. Writing assistant, not operational AI. And the gap between those two categories is where this industry's real story lives.

Vendor pitches do not distinguish between them. A builder who asks ChatGPT to "write me a Facebook post about our new development" and a builder whose estimating software auto-quantifies drywall from plan drawings both check the same "uses AI" box in a survey response, but only one of those workflows will compress project timelines or catch a $12,000 framing error before drywall goes up, and nobody tracking adoption statistics seems interested in asking which one the respondent meant.

What Contractors Report When You Ask Better Questions

ServiceTitan's 2026 State of AI in the Trades report surveyed 1,032 contractors across seven trades and arrived at numbers that cut closer to reality. Only 12% have embedded AI into daily operations. Another 34% are experimenting, which in practice usually means someone at the company has an account and plays with it when they remember. Among the 12% who have genuinely embedded AI, 62% report measurable gains in efficiency and productivity, and many save three or more hours per week on tasks that used to require manual data entry, comparison shopping, or back-of-envelope calculations that occasionally went wrong in expensive ways.

A separate ServiceTitan residential report, this one surveying 1,000 residential contractors specifically, found that 74% view AI as key to efficiency while only 25% are actually using it. Among early adopters, 48% report increased productivity and 45% report time savings, while 73% believe starting early creates a competitive advantage they cannot afford to surrender to rivals who moved first.

44% Share of contractors who cite lack of training as the top barrier to AI adoption, tied with integration complexity at 44%. Only 18% cite employee resistance. Crews are not saying no. Nobody is showing them how.

Barriers reveal what headline adoption numbers hide. Training and integration complexity tie at 44% as the leading obstacles, followed by difficulty understanding available tools at 38% and unclear ROI at 37%. Employee resistance lands at just 18%. Builders are not fighting the technology. What they cannot figure out is how to connect it to the six disconnected software platforms already running their business, each doing one job adequately while sharing data with none of the others.

Six Platforms, Zero Interoperability

Only 20% of contractors operate on a single software platform, according to multiple industry surveys. Everyone else runs what analyst Grant Fuellenbach calls a "Franken-stack": estimating in one system, scheduling in another, project management in a third, accounting in a fourth, communications scattered across email threads and text messages that live nowhere permanent and are discoverable by no one except the person who sent them.

This fragmentation is not laziness or incompetence. Residential construction never developed around unified data infrastructure the way manufacturing or logistics did, partly because projects are inherently one-off and partly because margins have historically been too thin to fund the kind of IT integration that a hospital system or airline considers routine. A custom home builder running $2-5M in annual revenue might use Buildertrend for project management, QuickBooks for accounting, a spreadsheet for estimating, text messages for sub coordination, and a physical file folder for permits. Each tool works at its own job, and none of them talk to each other. Plugging an AI layer on top of five disconnected data sources produces five disconnected AI features, each one lacking the context it would need to deliver the integrated scheduling-estimating-procurement intelligence that vendor demos promise with such confidence during the twenty-minute conference booth presentation.

Burns understood this at McCownGordon, which is why his team's approach started with data, not software. "We deploy AI to automate the quantification process for estimating," he explained on the company website. "The AI reads the drawings and outputs the quantities, which saves our teams approximately 80% of the traditional time." That 80% is real, measured, verifiable. It is also the product of a billion-dollar company with a dedicated SVP of Technology managing the data pipeline, and a $3M custom builder without that person is not going to replicate the result by subscribing to a vendor that wraps the same general-purpose model behind a login screen and a construction-themed UI.

What NAHB Published and What Builders Heard

In March 2026, NAHB's BuilderBooks arm published AI in Residential Construction: A Blueprint for Lasting Impact and Success, a 246-page guide by Grace Tsao Mase, a Yale-trained architect with three U.S. patents and a multi-state contractor's license. It became one of the top-selling titles at the 2026 International Builders' Show, and its central claim is that builders using AI can complete projects up to 30% faster without sacrificing quality.

That 30% will get repeated at every chapter meeting through the end of the year, creating expectations most builders' current AI setup cannot deliver, because a 30% speed improvement requires AI embedded in scheduling, procurement, estimating, and field coordination all working against the same dataset, not a chatbot writing emails while the scheduling software sits in a different tab unaware that anything changed.

BuildOps surveyed 600 contractors across the U.S. and Canada. Of those using or testing AI, 45% with dedicated AI budgets are spending that money on outside consulting, paying someone else to figure out what they should be doing. Willingness and budgets both exist. What does not exist is a playbook that maps AI capabilities to a specific builder's workflow, in their specific stack, at their specific scale, without first requiring them to solve the data integration problem that nobody in the vendor market has an incentive to address because fragmentation is what keeps the per-seat subscription model profitable.

35% Share of teams across industries that have replaced at least one SaaS tool with a custom build, per Retool's 2026 survey of 817 respondents. Construction is catching the same wave, one builder at a time.

Build It Yourself, or Pay Someone to Pretend They Did

Burns proved a point with his four-hour web app, but the takeaway is not "every builder should learn to code." It is narrower and more immediately useful: building software costs almost nothing now, and SaaS pricing has not adjusted to that reality.

Retool's 2026 Build vs. Buy Shift Report surveyed 817 respondents across industries and found that 35% have already replaced at least one SaaS tool with a custom build while 78% plan to build more custom tooling this year. Construction is catching that wave: a mid-Atlantic design-build firm profiled by Pro Remodeler is running custom GPTs for estimating, production, admin, and project management, not as experiments but as operating tools their staff use daily, with results flowing back into the prompts every week.

For a residential builder doing $1-5M, the realistic version of "build it yourself" starts small. Take your most painful administrative bottleneck, usually estimating or client communication, feed your historical data into a large language model with a structured prompt, and test whether the output saves meaningful time. If you spend $20 per month on API access and recover three hours per week of estimating labor, that is roughly a $150-per-hour return on your twenty dollars, a ratio that makes the $800-per-month vendor wrapper offering the same underlying model with a nicer interface an uncomfortable proposition for anyone willing to do the arithmetic.

Building carries real risk, though, and that risk is worth taking seriously. Burns has a technology team that maintains what he creates, and that mid-Atlantic firm has someone who understands prompt engineering well enough to adjust when models change. A solo custom builder who restructures his estimating workflow around a tool that works beautifully on day one and breaks on day ninety when the underlying model updates will wish he had never started, because by then his entire estimating process depends on something that just stopped producing accurate numbers and he has no one to call except the same consultants charging $200 an hour to figure out what went wrong.

What This Means If You Are Building or Buying a Home

If you are hiring a builder, ask specifically what "we use AI" means. A builder who says AI cut their quantification time by 80% and a builder who says AI writes their newsletter are describing two fundamentally different operations, and only one of them is producing more accurate bids, catching errors earlier, and spending expensive human attention on structural decisions rather than administrative tasks that a machine handles more reliably.

If you are a builder reading this in August 2026, the honest assessment is that the vendor market is oversaturated with wrappers sold on demo-day polish rather than jobsite results. Tools delivering real ROI are trained on construction-specific datasets with live material pricing and residential-specific assemblies, not general-purpose models wearing a hard hat. Buildxact's AI estimating solves a defined problem for a defined user. A generic "AI project management" dashboard that repackages ChatGPT behind a construction-themed login screen does not, and the sooner you learn to tell those apart, the sooner your AI budget starts earning a return instead of funding someone else's pitch deck.

Residential construction lost 43,600 jobs over the past twelve months, marking the eleventh consecutive annual decline according to NAHB data, with estimated economic damage from labor shortages running to $10.8 billion per year. Builders who cannot systematize and automate will not replace what they cannot hire. But operational AI that addresses this crisis looks like quantification automation, schedule optimization, and predictive change-order analysis running against a builder's own historical data. It does not look like a chatbot with a hard hat emoji in the header, no matter how impressive the conference demo was.

Limitations

This analysis relies on self-reported survey data from ServiceTitan (1,032 and 1,000 respondents, respectively), BuildOps (600+), and NAHB/Wells Fargo. Self-reported AI adoption rates are inherently inflated because respondents define "using AI" on their own terms, and the definition varies wildly between a builder running an integrated estimating system and one who asked ChatGPT a question about lumber prices once. McCownGordon is a large commercial GC with dedicated technology leadership whose approach has limited transferability to small residential operations without equivalent technical staff. NAHB's "30% faster" claim from BuilderBooks has not been independently verified with controlled studies across builder sizes, geographies, and project types. Retool's build-vs-buy data spans all industries rather than construction specifically, and the 35% replacement rate may look different in a sector with a lower technical baseline than the software and finance firms that dominate that survey's respondent pool.

Against This Argument

Vendor wrappers serve a real function: they lower the technical barrier so that builders who lack a Dustin Burns on staff can still benefit from AI without understanding API calls, prompt engineering, or data normalization. A well-designed SaaS product that packages AI behind a simple interface and provides customer support, ongoing training, and regular updates against model changes earns its subscription the same way a framing nailer earns its price over a hammer and a bag of nails, because speed matters, reliability matters, and a builder who spends three weekends configuring a custom GPT instead of framing a house has made a bad trade regardless of the per-hour ROI calculation. What this industry has right now is a vendor market that is immature rather than fraudulent, and the meaningful difference between a wrapper worth paying for and vaporware worth avoiding is whether the product delivers consistent results once the conference demo is over and a project superintendent has to use it on a Tuesday morning with wet boots, a subcontractor who no-showed, and an owner's rep asking why the schedule slipped again.
