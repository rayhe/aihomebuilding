Your Siding Has a 30-Year Warranty. The Installation Manual Has 40 Ways to Void It.

By Catherine Chen · September 20, 2026 · 8 min read

CertainTeed paid $103.9 million to settle claims that its fiber-cement siding cracked, warped, and let water into the walls behind it. James Hardie spent six years fighting nearly identical allegations over HardiePlank and walked away when a federal judge dismissed the multidistrict case, tossed the plaintiffs' expert evidence, and threw out the individual claims the next day. Read both dockets and one pattern repeats until it becomes the whole story: the manufacturers' defense, in every case, in every brief, is improper installation.

That defense has a document behind it. James Hardie's installation instructions run to dozens of pages of tolerances measured in fractions of an inch: nails driven snug into a printed nail line three-quarters of an inch to one inch wide, a minimum 1-1/4 inch overlap between courses, six inches of clearance above finished grade, two inches above roofs and decks, a quarter-inch gap at horizontal flashing that must not be caulked, kickout flashing at every roof-to-wall junction bent to 100 to 110 degrees. Miss enough of these and the 30-year non-prorated warranty the brochure advertised becomes a 30-year record of things the installer did wrong. Here is the part the brochure does not mention: most of those details become unverifiable the moment the crew moves to the next course.

Consider the nail line. HardiePlank lap siding is manufactured with an embossed line marking where blind nails must land. Blind nailing means the fastener goes through the top of the course and the next course overlaps it, which means the nail line, the nails, and any evidence of where they landed are permanently covered within the hour. James Hardie's own product literature warns that the nail line should be used as a guide for placement and not as a lap line, a distinction that matters enormously and is visible to nobody after installation. Field joints get flashing behind them, never caulk, and the flashing disappears behind the joint. Kickout flashing at the roof-wall junction gets buried under the courses that follow. An inspector who arrives after the scaffolding comes down can admire the finished wall. She cannot verify a single fastener.

$4.75 / sq ft
What the CertainTeed settlement valued a failed siding job at for repair and replacement. On a 2,000-square-foot wall area, that is roughly $9,500, which is the price of being wrong about the installation.

The window nobody is watching

Fifteen years of siding litigation is really an argument about evidence, and the evidence has a shelf life measured in minutes. By the time water shows up inside the wall, which is typically years later, the question is always the same: was the product defective, or was it installed wrong? The CertainTeed settlement answered that question the way mass litigation usually does, by paying both sides to stop asking, with a two-tier payout that priced the ambiguity itself. Homeowners with qualifying damage chose between a repair-and-replace option (new siding plus $4.75 per square foot for labor) and a quick-cash option ($4.25 per square foot), and the gap between those numbers is roughly the market price of not knowing whose fault it was.

This is where the technology argument actually lands, and it is narrower than the marketing suggests. Nobody needs AI to admire a finished wall. What is needed is a camera on the wall during the forty minutes when each course is still exposed: a photograph of every course before the next goes on, checked against the manufacturer's own checklist. Nail in the nail line or below it. Overlap at least 1-1/4 inches. Joint flashing present behind every field joint, with no caulk bridging the gap. Kickout flashing visible at the roof junction. Clearances measurable against a reference. Each image becomes a course-by-course compliance record, timestamped, geotagged, and stored where neither the installer's memory nor the manufacturer's warranty department can revise it later.

Note what this is not competing with. It is not competing with the final inspection, because the final inspection cannot see any of this. It is competing with amnesia. Municipal inspectors check flashing and water-resistive barrier before the siding goes up, then return to a finished wall and sign off on what is visible, which is paint and straight lines. The warranty's actual requirements live in the gap between those two visits, in work that no official ever witnesses. A phone camera pointed at each course is the only inspector that was ever going to be present.

The checklist that is also a contract

For the homeowner paying $15,000 to $40,000 to reside a house, the practical version of this costs nothing and requires no software. James Hardie's requirements are public, and the critical ones fit on an index card: six inches above grade, two inches above roofs and decks, quarter-inch gaps at flashing left uncaulked, kickout flashing where the roof meets the wall, nails in the nail line driven snug but not overdriven, field joints flashed not caulked, membrane flashing around every window and door opening, one inch between gutter end caps and the siding. Photograph each course from straight on before the next course covers it. Keep the photos with the contract and the warranty paperwork. If a claim ever arises, the question of whether the installation met the manual stops being a matter of competing recollections.

Drone and phone-based vision inspection services now offer to automate the checking: models trained to flag nail placement, flashing presence, and clearance violations from imagery captured during the install. The pitch is straightforward, and for a few hundred dollars on a residing job it is cheap relative to the $9,500 cost of being wrong. But treat the automation as a second set of eyes, not a verdict. Vision models trained on one manufacturer's profile can misread another's, nail-line position and exposure vary by product and climate zone, and no model can verify what is behind the siding, which remains the most consequential part of the wall.

What the lawyers would like you to notice

Now the part of this column where I ruin the sales pitch, because the legal architecture here deserves scrutiny. There is a reason the installation manual specifies tolerances to the quarter inch, and building science is only half of it. Every specified detail is a potential finding of improper installation, and every finding of improper installation is a warranty denial that does not require arguing about the product. The manual is a contract written by one party, enforced by that party's claims department, against homeowners who have never read it. The HardiePlank multidistrict dismissal is instructive: six years of litigation, expert evidence excluded, class certification denied, individual claims dismissed. Product-defect claims against the manufacturer are brutally hard to win. Installation-error claims against the contractor face the mirror-image problem, which is proving, years later, which course was nailed wrong.

A course-by-course photo record cuts both ways, and homeowners should understand this before they start documenting. High-resolution evidence of every nail and gap is exactly as useful to the manufacturer's claims department as it is to the homeowner's lawyer. The CertainTeed settlement existed because fault had become unprovable at scale; better documentation moves the ambiguity to an earlier date with better lighting, but it does not dissolve it. And the homeowners who most need this protection, the ones who hired the cheapest bidder and will never pay for an inspection service, are the least likely to have anyone photographing the nail lines. Technology does not fix a market where the buyer cannot evaluate the work and the seller is paid before the wall is tested by weather. It just gives the dispute better exhibits.

What this piece did not prove

Boundaries, stated plainly. The CertainTeed settlement figures come from settlement-administration summaries and secondary reporting, not from court-published damages findings, and CertainTeed denied the siding was defective. Details of the HardiePlank multidistrict dismissal come from a Bloomberg Law summary; the underlying opinions were not independently reviewed here. Clearance and fastener specifications are drawn from the HZ5-zone installation instructions and installer-published checklists; other climate zones and products differ, so verify against the manual for the exact product on the wall. That residing cost range is a national market estimate, not a quote. No independent field study of AI siding-inspection accuracy was available, and the photograph-each-course method is a synthesized protocol, not a tested one.

The code does not care about your feelings, and neither does a warranty claims department. What both respect is a record. Six inches, two inches, a quarter inch uncaulked, a photograph before the next course goes on. Thirty years is a long time to trust a wall nobody watched being built.
