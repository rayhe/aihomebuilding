25% of the Crew Framing Your House Is Losing Its Hearing. An Algorithm Heard It First.

By Marcus Washington · October 6, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Ask a framer what the job sounds like and he will not talk about decibels; he will talk about the saw, the circular saw that is the metronome of residential construction, running from the first layout line at 7 a.m. to the last ripped stud before the light goes. A man can spend four hours a day inside its scream without ever thinking of it as an injury, because nothing bleeds and nothing breaks, and the damage does not arrive until years later, when he is asking his wife to repeat herself for the third time and she is looking at him the way my mother looked at my father, who came out of thirty years of steel work with two hearing aids and a grudge.

My father wore his plugs most of the time. They were the orange foam kind, and the plant gave them out by the bucket, and the hearing tests came around once a year like flu shots, and even so the top end of his hearing went first, then the middle, then one Christmas he sat at the table nodding along to a conversation he was not actually in. That is what the long version of this story looks like. In residential construction, the short version is worse, because the tests my father got are not required at all, so read that sentence again.

In manufacturing, in mining, in general industry, federal law requires the employer to monitor noise, test hearing annually, train the worker, and keep the records for decades, while in construction none of that exists. OSHA said so in writing in 1983: the hearing conservation amendment "is applicable to all employees... except those engaged in construction or agriculture." Construction got its own noise standard instead, 29 CFR 1926.52, and as the industrial hygienist Christina Trahan told EHS Today, there is no guidance in the construction regulations about what a hearing program must contain, "so, basically employers just hand out hearing plugs."

Hand out plugs: that is the entire federal hearing conservation program for the industry that builds your house.

The Numbers Are Brutal and Nobody Disputes Them

NIOSH keeps the receipts, and they are not ambiguous. Analyzing a decade of audiograms, the agency found that workers in new single-family housing construction have a 25 percent prevalence of hearing loss, putting house framers, siders, and roofers in the top five worst sub-sectors in the country, shoulder to shoulder with highway builders and oil pipeline crews. Elizabeth Masterson, the NIOSH epidemiologist who led the study, put it plainly: the damage is severe enough to affect workers' daily lives, and more needs to be done.

Step back one level and the pattern holds across the whole trade. CDC surveillance finds 13 percent of all construction workers have hearing difficulty and 7 percent have tinnitus. Among noise-exposed workers who were actually tested, 23 percent have material hearing impairment, meaning they cannot reliably understand speech, and 16 percent have it in both ears. Among workers tracked by the Building Trades medical screening program, who average twenty-plus years in the trade, 58 percent show material hearing impairment; among carpenters it is 65 percent. By age 25, NIOSH researchers found, the average carpenter's hearing matches that of a healthy 50-year-old who never worked in noise.

And here is the number that should end every debate about whose fault this is: 52 percent of noise-exposed construction workers report not wearing hearing protection. Not sometimes forgetting. Not wearing. More than half, in the second-deafest industry in America.

28% Share of construction workers who have ever had their hearing tested since entering the trade, per the CPWR worker survey. Of those tested, fewer than half were tested because an employer required it. In general industry, the annual test is federal law.

Two Rulers, One Morning

Now the math, because the math is where the scandal lives.

A framing circular saw runs about 100 decibels, give or take, which makes a four-hour cutting session the most ordinary thing in residential construction. Hold OSHA's construction ruler up to that morning: the permissible limit is 90 decibels over eight hours with a 5-decibel exchange rate, which works out to two legal hours at 100 decibels. Four hours is 200 percent of a legal dose, and that is the generous ruler. Hold NIOSH's ruler up to the same morning: the recommended limit is 85 decibels with a 3-decibel exchange rate, which allows fifteen minutes at 100 decibels. Four hours is sixteen legal doses before lunch.

Same saw, same morning, same ears, and either twice a safe day or sixteen times a safe day depending on which federal agency's ruler you pick up. And here is the detail that should make every residential builder sit up: even a full eight-hour shift in perfect compliance with OSHA's construction limit, 90 decibels all day, every day, accumulates more than three times the dose NIOSH considers safe. A framer can spend his entire career inside the law and still go deaf by the book.

That gap, the 90 versus the 85, is the whole story in two numbers. General industry at least has to measure against its ruler: audiometric testing, exposure monitoring, training, records kept for the duration of employment plus decades. Construction was promised the same protections in an advance notice of proposed rulemaking in August 2002. That was twenty-four years ago, and since then no proposal has followed and no final rule has been issued; the drawer is still closed.

What the Machines Can Hear

This is where the AI enters, and I want to be fair to it, because the technology is genuinely clever even if the industry around it is a mess.

Soundtrace, which made its name selling connected audiometric testing to factories, now runs what it calls an AI-powered platform that ties three data streams together: the audiogram, continuous noise dosimetry from cloud-connected monitors, and hearing-protection fit testing, which OSHA itself named a best practice. Soundtrace's pitch is predictive: instead of discovering a standard threshold shift at the annual test, a year after the damage happened, the system flags the workers whose exposure pattern and early audiometric drift say they are heading for one, so the employer can intervene on the exposure before the loss becomes permanent. Tests take about six minutes, no sound booth, no mobile van booked months out, results same day with audiologist review.

Fit testing matters more than it sounds, because a foam plug inserted wrong attenuates almost nothing and nobody on a residential site is checking insertion technique. Soundtrace pairs the test with verification that the protection actually works on that worker, which is the difference between handing out plugs and running a program.

On the hardware side, QHR widened release of its Headset and Headset Pro this summer, running machine learning on the device itself for workers in 80-plus-decibel environments. QHR's design attacks the reason half the workforce skips protection: standard plugs seal out the foreman's instructions along with the saw. QHR's hybrid array claims up to 34 decibels of attenuation across active, passive, and environmental layers while keeping voices intelligible. Protection you can hear through is protection a framer might actually wear.

None of this is science fiction; it is shipping product, priced for real businesses, and in construction, where nothing is required, the entire pitch is voluntary.

The Money Argument Writes Itself

Soundtrace published its own cost analysis in March, and since it is vendor math I read it the way I read all vendor math, which is to say I checked the inputs, and the inputs are not theirs. An accepted occupational hearing loss workers' comp claim averages $96,786 including indemnity and medical, per National Academy of Social Insurance data, and NIOSH puts the national annual toll at $242 million. For third-party testing, the company's own published figure averages about $300 per employee per year, with an in-house program cutting that 40 to 60 percent.

Run it for a residential GC with twenty noise-exposed workers: twenty times $300 is $6,000 a year for a documented baseline program, against one claim averaging $96,786, which means the program costs one-sixteenth of one claim. And the real exposure is worse than the arithmetic, because hearing loss is a latent injury: the claim arrives ten or twenty years after the exposure, long after the crew has turned over twice, and the quality of the records the employer kept during those years is the entire defense. No baseline audiogram, no exposure history, no fit-test documentation, and the employer is negotiating against an audiogram taken last month with nothing behind it.

Now the part that should anger you: in construction, the employer is not required to keep any of those records, which means the records that would win the claim are exactly the records nobody has to create. So most small builders create none of them, and discover the exposure the day the lawyer's letter arrives.

16x The average accepted hearing-loss workers' comp claim ($96,786, NASI data) costs sixteen times what a year of audiometric testing for a 20-person crew costs (~$6,000). An employer who tests has a documented defense; one who doesn't is guessing.

The Case Against All of This, at Full Strength

Now the part the vendors will not put on their websites.

First, and this is the one that matters: audiometry does not fix the 52 percent problem. No predictive algorithm ever built does anything for a worker who will not put the plugs in, and construction's actual failure mode is fit and compliance, not detection. Trahan's line stands: strip away the language and the industry's hearing program is a bucket of foam plugs. An algorithm that detects damage early in a workforce that refuses protection is a very expensive way to document a defeat.

Second, the workforce is mobile and the data is not. Framers change employers the way the rest of us change phone plans, and no single builder accumulates the longitudinal history the predictive model needs. Worse, nobody has settled who owns the worker's hearing record when he leaves. A baseline audiogram is a medical record with twenty years of liability attached to it, and the residential GC holding it is one server breach or one subpoena away from a problem he never signed up to have.

Third, the surveillance question is real, and it is the workers themselves who will ask it. Continuous dosimetry linked to each worker's profile, exposure analysis tied to individual audiograms, cameras that read whether the plugs are in: this is a medical surveillance program wearing a safety vest. This is the same workforce that underreports injuries, where the NASP survey found only about half of injured Hispanic workers report them, and it is being asked to strap on the monitor and trust the data will be used for protection rather than discipline. My union family would have had exactly one question about that, and it would not have been about the algorithm's accuracy.

Fourth, the vendor claims are vendor claims: I found no independent peer-reviewed evaluation of Soundtrace's predictive analytics, no longitudinal study of AI-driven hearing conservation on residential sites specifically, and no evidence that early detection changes behavior in a workforce where half the workers skip protection entirely. I find the mechanism plausible; the outcome data, though, does not exist yet.

Fifth, the technology does not touch the actual regulatory failure. A 24-year-old stalled rulemaking is not a technology problem. OSHA could have required testing, monitoring, and training for construction workers when my father still had his hearing, and every administration since has found a reason not to. No startup fixes that; only a rule does.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

That dose table is my arithmetic, not a field study: I used 100 decibels as the illustrative saw level from published tool-noise references, not measurements from a residential site, and real exposures vary with the tool, its condition, the space, and how the day is actually spent. That $96,786 average claim figure is Soundtrace's citation of NASI data; I did not pull the NASI dataset independently, and claim values swing enormously by state, severity, and wage. Similarly, the $300-per-employee testing figure is Soundtrace's published third-party average; actual quotes will vary, and the 40-to-60-percent in-house savings is the company's claim, not an independent finding. CPWR's survey numbers are self-reported, which means the 52 percent non-wear figure and the 28 percent ever-tested figure are probably optimistic, since people overreport the virtuous behavior. And nothing here proves that builders who adopt AI hearing conservation actually reduce claims. I stand behind the mechanism and the arithmetic; the outcome data is not in yet.

What a Serious Builder Does Monday Morning

You do not need artificial intelligence for the first three of these; you need a spine.

First, get baselines: every noise-exposed worker gets an audiogram at hire, on the company's dime, kept for the duration of employment. This is the single document that decides a workers' comp claim a decade from now, and in construction nobody requires it, which is precisely why you should do it voluntarily.

Second, fit-test the protection instead of just handing it out: a plug that is not inserted right is a placebo. If you are buying protection by the bucket, you are buying the appearance of a program. Verify the attenuation on the actual worker or stop pretending.

Third, measure the site rather than the worker: a week of area dosimetry during framing tells you which tasks blow the dose and which crews need rotation, and it sidesteps the surveillance fight entirely, because the monitor watches the air, not the man.

Fourth, if you want the AI layer: the predictive platforms make economic sense once you have the first three in place: $6,000 a year for a twenty-person crew against a $96,786 average claim is not a hard decision, provided you understand you are buying early warning, not compliance, since compliance in construction does not exist.

Fifth, if you are the homeowner rather than the builder: add one question to the interview, which is when did you last test your crew's hearing, and can I see the program? A builder who can answer is running a business that will still be solvent when your warranty claim arrives. A builder who looks confused is telling you everything about how he runs the rest of the job, too.

My father got the tests: his plant was required to give them, the union made sure they happened, and he still lost the top end of his hearing to thirty years of honest work, and the tests did not save his ears. What they did was prove, year after year, that the damage was the job's fault and not his, which is why the claim paid and why he could look the company in the eye. Framers building your house do not even get that. Twenty-four years after OSHA admitted the rule was needed, the drawer is still closed, the saws still scream, and half the crew still will not wear the plugs; the algorithm can hear it happening now, and the question is whether anybody with the power to stop it is listening.
