Your Water Pressure Is 110 PSI. The Code Says 80. The Fix Is Two Parts, and the Second One Gets Skipped.

By Frank "The Foreman" DeLuca · September 26, 2026
← Back to articles

Thread a fourteen-dollar gauge onto the laundry tap on a Saturday morning, with every fixture in the house shut off, and watch the needle. In a surprising number of American homes it climbs past 80 PSI without pausing, past 100 without hurrying, and settles somewhere around 110 while the house stands silent. Nothing is running: no shower, no dishwasher, no irrigation. That number is the static pressure inside every pipe, every braided supply line, and every toilet fill valve in the building at that moment. It has probably been that number for years.

Plumbing code sets a maximum of 80, and Section 604.8 of the International Plumbing Code says that where pressure inside a building exceeds 80 PSI static, an approved pressure-reducing valve must be installed to bring it down, with the residential code repeating the requirement at P2903.3.1. So that Saturday-morning reading is not a curiosity. It is a measurement of a code violation, sitting quietly in the walls, doing slow work.

Where the pressure comes from

Water arrives at your house carrying whatever pressure the municipal system needed to deliver it. Utilities pump their mains hard enough to reach the farthest service connection, the highest elevation in the zone, the top floor of the tallest building on the line, and every fire hydrant in between. Depending on where your house sits relative to the source, the pressure at your meter can land anywhere from 25 to 170 PSI. Live downhill from the reservoir or at a low point in the pressure zone and you get the high end of that range, free of charge, twenty-four hours a day, whether your plumbing wants it or not.

Cave Creek, Arizona, states the arrangement plainly in its utility guidance: the code caps residential pressure at 80 PSI, mains in low-lying areas can run as high as 100, and responsibility for reducing it is, in the town's words, specifically defined to be the responsibility of the customer. Your utility will deliver 110 PSI to your foundation forever and consider its job done. Everything downstream of the meter is your project to manage, which is a sentence I have watched surprise a great many homeowners.

What the pressure does over twenty years

High pressure does not burst pipes. That is the misconception that keeps people calm, and it is wrong in the way that matters. What high pressure does is fatigue. Every solenoid valve closure, dishwasher, washing machine, ice maker, stops flow abruptly, and at 110 PSI the resulting shockwave hits harder than the piping was designed to absorb. Toilet fill valves, which are twenty-dollar assemblies built around rubber seals, start running constantly as the seals compress past their design limit. Faucet cartridges drip no matter how firmly you close the handle. Braided stainless supply lines are typically rated to 125 PSI, so they do not fail on day one; the liner fatigues over years of sustained load and gives up early. Copper joints and soldered fittings absorb microstress with every spike until a pinhole opens inside a wall cavity. Pinholes do not announce themselves; they soak drywall, framing, and subfloor quietly, sometimes for months, and by the time you see the stain the repair involves a drywall contractor, not a plumber.

Appliances follow the same curve: dishwashers, washing machines, and water heaters are engineered around controlled inlet pressure, and sustained overpressure shortens the life of the internal valves and seals first, which is why the failures show up as the dishwasher just died at year six rather than anything anyone connects to the gauge on the hose bib. Manufacturers rate most residential fixtures and appliances around 80 PSI, and exceeding it can void the warranty language you never read. Twenty years of projects have taught me that the expensive failures are rarely dramatic; they are quiet numbers nobody measured.

The fourteen-dollar test and the four-hundred-fifty-two-dollar valve

This diagnostic costs less than a pizza. Buy a pressure gauge with a lazy hand, the second needle that stays behind at the peak reading, for twelve to fifteen dollars. Thread it onto a hose bib or the laundry tap with every fixture in the house shut off. Then read it at night, between ten in the evening and five in the morning, because municipal pressure peaks when neighborhood demand drops, and a two o'clock in the afternoon reading can understate the real number by twenty PSI or more. If the static reading is above 80, the code requires a pressure-reducing valve. That reading is also your leverage with the plumber, because now you are not describing a vague worry; you are handing over a number.

A pressure-reducing valve is a bell-shaped brass fitting that threads into the main line where it enters the house. Inside the dome, a spring and diaphragm step the street pressure down to the setpoint, usually 55 to 65 PSI, and hold it there regardless of how the municipal supply fluctuates upstream. Code is specific about what qualifies: the valve must conform to ASSE 1003, carry a strainer, be installed where it can be serviced without cutting pipe, and be designed to fail open, so a dead valve passes water instead of stopping it. Devices run $65 for a basic unit to $700 for a large or premium one, and the installed national average lands around $452, covering roughly two and a half hours of plumber time plus materials. Set it to 55 or 60 and leave it alone. Expect ten to fifteen years of service, after which creeping or swinging house pressure means the valve is done, which is normal. It is a wear item, like a water heater anode rod or an HVAC capacitor, and it belongs on the same replacement schedule in your head.

The second half of the fix

Here is the part that gets skipped, and it is the most important paragraph in this article. A pressure-reducing valve does not only lower your pressure; it closes your plumbing system. Before the valve, thermal expansion from the water heater, water expanding as it heats, could push back out through the service line into the street main, but after the valve that path is shut. With the path shut, the expanded water has nowhere to go, so system pressure spikes every time the heater runs a recovery cycle, sometimes past 100 PSI, hammering the exact components the valve was installed to protect.

The code anticipated this exact failure: Section P2903.4 of the residential code requires an approved thermal expansion control device on any system with a storage water heater whenever a device is installed that prevents pressure relief back through the supply, and it names the pressure-reducing valve explicitly. In plain language: a PRV-only install on a house with a tank water heater is itself a code violation. An expansion tank, a small steel tank with an air bladder that mounts near the heater and absorbs the extra volume, is not an accessory. It is the second half of the fix, and it adds roughly $150 to $300 installed.

Skip it and the system tells you, in its own way. Watch the temperature-and-pressure relief valve on the water heater start weeping, dribbling water down the discharge pipe to bleed off the spikes. Homeowners replace the T&P valve, which changes nothing, because the T&P valve was doing its job. I have read the full sequence in a homeowner's own account: 150 PSI street pressure, a backflow preventer at the meter, no expansion tank, years of chronic relief-valve leakage, resolved completely by a PRV and an expansion tank installed together. Order of operations matters. Test, then regulate, then give the expanded water somewhere to go. Two parts. Always two parts.

The honest objections

A pressure-reducing valve is not a universal good, and anyone who tells you otherwise is selling the valve. If your street delivers 65 PSI, a PRV buys you nothing and throttles your showers for the privilege. It is a mechanical device with a ten-to-fifteen-year life, which means the fix becomes a maintenance item you own in perpetuity, and a failed unit can choke flow to a trickle or stick open and pass full street pressure silently for years. Cheap gauges are imprecise, and a single reading confuses thermal-expansion spikes with static pressure if you do not know what you are looking at. Most water damage comes from aging supply lines, poor installation, and deferred maintenance, not from pressure alone, and a PRV on a house with forty-year-old galvanized pipe is addressing the wrong problem first. Some plumbers sell the valve as a cure-all because it is a fast ticket with a good margin. Treat it as one line item in a water system, not a talisman.

Frank's pressure playbook: buyers, builders, owners
  1. Buying? Put a gauge on the hose bib during inspection. A static reading above 80 PSI is a code violation you can hand the seller with a number attached, and a $600 to $750 credit for the two-part fix is cheaper for everyone than the argument.
  2. Building? Specify the PRV and the expansion tank on the plumbing schedule together, with the setpoint written down: 55 to 65 PSI. Rough-in is the cheapest time to install both, because access is everything and the drywall is not up yet.
  3. Owning? Test annually. Gauges are cheap and pressure creeps as valves age. When the reading climbs or starts swinging, the PRV is at end of life. Check the expansion tank bladder yearly with a tire gauge; if it will not hold air, the tank is a paperweight.
  4. Have a tank water heater and a PRV but no expansion tank? You have half a fix and a code violation. The weeping relief valve is the system filing its complaint.
  5. Selling in a high-pressure zone? A receipt for the two-part install is a $700 line item that defuses a five-figure objection before the buyer's inspector writes it up.

What this article did not prove

I metered zero houses for this article. Cost figures here are national survey averages, and in high-cost labor markets the installed price runs higher. Municipal pressure varies enormously by elevation zone and utility, so 110 PSI is representative of low-elevation zones, not a measurement of your street. No controlled study links specific pressure levels to appliance lifespan deltas; the mechanism rests on manufacturer maximum ratings, warranty language, and the accumulated field experience of plumbers. Insurance figures, water damage at 22.6 percent of claims and $15,400 average severity, cover all causes, not the pressure-attributable share, so the twenty-to-one framing compares total fix cost against average claim severity rather than a measured prevention rate. Nighttime testing guidance reflects how municipal pressure zones behave under varying demand, not a metered experiment. Treat the numbers as directional and the fourteen-dollar test as the thing that makes them specific to your house.

Twenty years of projects have taught me that the expensive failures are never the dramatic ones; they are the quiet numbers nobody measured. Water pressure is a quiet number, sitting in your walls at 112 PSI while you sleep and working on every seal and joint you own, and the code has stated the limit for decades. Fourteen dollars and ten minutes will tell you where you stand. That is the cheapest diagnostic in the entire house.
