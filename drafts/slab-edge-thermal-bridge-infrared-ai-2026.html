Your Slab Edge Is the Coldest Square Foot in Your House. The Infrared Scan Sees What the Inspector Can't.

By Priya Greenwood · September 7, 2026 · 8 min read

Cold floors in a new house feel like a personal insult, because you paid for R-19 walls and R-49 in the attic, you sat through the sales pitch about the tight envelope, and your socks still go numb crossing the kitchen in January while the thermostat holds its smug 70 and your feet file a formal dissent.

One number explains it, and the number is almost rude. Thermal modeling published by GreenBuildingAdvisor sets a house at 25 degrees outside and 70 inside, then reads the floor temperature where the bottom plate meets the slab, and the thermometer says 44 degrees.

No draft, no cracked window: forty-four degrees of solid floor, because heat is pouring straight out through the edge of your concrete.

Concrete feels permanent, but thermally it is nearly transparent: twelve inches of solid concrete rates about R-1, and heat's escape route from your warm floor to frozen soil runs roughly six inches through the slab edge, which means R-0.5 sits directly beneath your R-19 wall, the worst square foot in the whole envelope, and nobody photographs it.

What the code demands, the crew forgets

Energy codes have chased this detail for years, and the 2021 IECC now requires R-10 slab-edge insulation, two feet deep in Climate Zone 4 and four feet in Zone 5, while Oregon's residential code goes further and demands R-15 around the full perimeter. Robby Schwarz, who answers code questions on Colorado's energy code help line, wrote in late 2025 that builders constantly trip over slab-edge details because the foam is difficult to install, protect, and inspect, which is a polite way of describing a detail that fails in exactly the ways you'd predict.

Foam stops six inches short of grade, and the specified depth becomes a suggestion.

Backfill scrapes the boards, UV chews the exposed strip above grade, and in termite country the code grants an outright exemption because termites tunnel straight through foam to reach your framing, so every one of these failures shares a single convenient property: once the dirt goes back in, nobody can see it. Not the inspector. Not the buyer. Not you, standing on your 44-degree floor wondering where the money went.

South of Zone 4 the situation is simpler and worse: Texas, Florida, Arizona, where the code requires nothing at the slab edge, which means millions of slabs went into the ground this decade with zero edge insulation, perfectly legal, quietly radiating heat into the dirt.

What the camera sees

Infrared thermography has read building envelopes for decades, and on a cold night a thermal camera pointed at a house makes missing insulation glow like a confession. Interpretation was always the catch: reading a thermal image takes a trained thermographer who can separate a real bridge from a reflection, a wind artifact, or an emissivity trick of the material, and good auditors are scarce while colorful images are cheap.

That last part is changing. A 2021 field study in Applied Sciences trained a neural network to find linear thermal bridges in infrared images and validated it on real buildings at 89 percent precision and 87 percent recall; later work applied RetinaNet object detection to pick insulation defects out of facade scans automatically (Buildings, 2025), and researchers are now training models to synthesize thermal views from ordinary photographs (arXiv, 2024).

None of this replaces the camera or the cold night. It replaces the expert squinting at the image afterward.

Geometry favors the slab edge here, because the bridge shows up as a straight glowing line at the base of the wall, exactly where the foam should be, which is about as easy a detection problem as infrared gets. An auditor charging around $200 for an infrared add-on to a home energy audit, against HomeAdvisor's 2025 national data putting full audits at $212 to $698, can run the scan through a classifier and hand you a marked-up image instead of an opinion. Ask for the scan before the drywall goes up and again before backfill, because those are the two moments the evidence is visible.

Do the math before you preach

Now the honest arithmetic, because this detail does not deserve hype: ASHRAE's published F-factors for slab-on-grade floors put an uninsulated slab at 0.73 and a slab with 24 inches of vertical R-10 at 0.54 Btu per hour per foot of perimeter per degree (Table A6.3). Take a 2,000-square-foot home, 40 by 50, 180 feet of perimeter, sitting in Chicago at 6,536 heating degree days, and multiply it out: 0.19 times 180 times 6,536 times 24 hours.

Missing foam costs about 5.4 million Btu a year. Roughly 54 therms. Around $90 a year at $1.50 per therm through a 90-percent furnace.

Installed cost of that foam during construction runs roughly $1,200 to $2,000, which is two-inch XPS at a couple dollars a square foot over 360 square feet of edge plus labor and the protective covering above grade, and $90 a year against $1,500 is a payback north of 15 years. As an investment, slab-edge insulation loses to nearly every other envelope upgrade you could name.

So don't buy it as an investment. Buy it for the 44-degree floor that becomes a 54-degree floor, buy it because in Zone 4 and up it is already code and you're paying for compliance either way, buy it because NREL's slab insulation testing measured nearly 25 percent site energy savings on its test module when the stem wall finally got its code-required insulation. And if you're building where code requires nothing, understand that the $1,500 you skip today becomes a $10,000 excavation later.

Comfort now or regret later; pick one.

Arguing against all of this

A skeptic reads the $90-a-year math and asks why this deserves a thousand words when attic air sealing pays back in three, and the question is fair: the energy case is thin, and anyone selling slab-edge foam on ROI is selling. The comfort case is real but subjective, because some people never notice a cold floor and thermostats don't measure feet.

Then there's the AI, and here the hype deserves a haircut. Those 89-percent precision figures come from peer-reviewed field experiments, not products with warranties and support lines, and the arXiv authors warn, correctly, that thermography is artifact-prone, with reflections, emissivity, wind, and sky temperature all conspiring to paint confident nonsense in false color. A scan done on a mild afternoon, or read by software trained on the wrong building stock, means nothing no matter how pretty the image. And the foam itself is fragile stuff: UV degradation above grade, crushed boards behind the backfill, termite highways in the Southeast. Insulation you can't inspect is insulation you have to trust, which is exactly the problem this article started with.

What to do on Monday

Photograph the foam before backfill. If you're building in Zone 4 or colder, the code already requires R-10 at depth. Make the photo a contract milestone: no photo of continuous foam at the specified depth, no backfill. This costs nothing and catches the most common failure.

In warm climates, add it while the trench is open. Two feet of R-10 around the perimeter during construction is about $1,500 on a typical slab. Retrofitting means excavation, landscaping demolition, and a bill five to ten times higher. There is no second cheap chance.

Order the infrared scan with a 20-degree delta. Thermal imaging needs at least a 20-degree indoor-outdoor temperature difference to mean anything, so winter is your window in cold climates and summer nights work where cooling dominates. A $200 add-on to an energy audit, run before drywall and again at final, is the cheapest verification this detail will ever get.

Check the termite exemption before anyone cites it. Code exempts slab-edge foam only in very-heavy termite infestation zones. If your builder claims the exemption in Ohio, get it in writing and check the map.

Protect what's above grade. Foam exposed above the soil line needs a durable covering, flashed and rated for UV. Unprotected foam chalks, crumbles, and quietly quits within a few seasons.

Limitations of this analysis

The $90-a-year figure is a steady-state estimate for Chicago gas heat, and mild climates, cheap gas, or heat-pump economics all move it, while F-factors approximate a three-dimensional seasonal heat flow that no single number captures exactly. Installation cost is built from material pricing rather than a contractor bid survey, and it will vary by market. The AI accuracy figures come from published experiments rather than commercial products, and I found no third-party audit of any AI thermal-bridge tool sold to auditors. The 44-degree floor is modeled, not measured in your house. Treat every number here as directional, and verify the foam with your own eyes before the dirt covers it.

Your slab is the heaviest thing you own and the leakiest detail you'll never see. Foam it, photograph it, scan it, then enjoy warm socks.
