Your Lumber Supplier Just Became Your Software Vendor. The Data Bill Comes Later.

By Frank DeLuca · September 7, 2026

Your estimator shows up at 6:40 on a Tuesday. Coffee in one hand, a rolled-up plan set under the other arm, and by noon he has produced exactly one material takeoff for one house, which he will now email to himself, print twice, and lose the printout of by Thursday. Every builder reading this just nodded. That morning ritual is the entire business case behind the deal announced August 25: Builders FirstSource, the largest residential building-products supplier in the country, led a $25.3 million Series A in Digs, an AI platform that turns construction documents into what it calls a single living source of truth, and signed a five-year commercial agreement to put that platform in front of its 140,000 builder customers.

I have watched twenty years of software companies walk onto job sites promising to fix exactly this morning. Most of them left on a stretcher. This one is different in a way that should make every builder pause, because the vendor is not some SaaS company with a construction vertical. It is the company that sells you the studs.

What the Platform Actually Does

Digs, founded in 2022 in Vancouver, Washington, ingests the usual chaos of a build, meaning the plans, the change orders, the texts, the emails, the finish selections, and uses AI to structure it all into one searchable project record. Builders can ask questions of their own project data the way they would ask a superintendent, except the AI remembers everything. It generates material takeoffs automatically, builds a visual record through construction, and hands the homeowner a digital twin of the finished house at move-in, complete with warranties and maintenance guidance.

Co-founder Ryan Fink told HousingWire the AI can perform about 700 takeoffs in the time it takes one estimator to perform one, and that takeoff and design-studio diagramming time drops 80 to 90 percent. Pricing runs about $59 per user per month on annual plans, with homeowners, subs, and vendors getting access free, which means the person selling the materials is not charging for the seats that matter least and is charging a very reasonable rate for the ones that matter most.

Running the Numbers for a Real Shop

Let me do the arithmetic the vendor decks skip, using a five-person custom builder as the model. An estimator with a fully loaded cost of roughly $80,000 a year costs about $40 an hour. If takeoffs consume a third of their week, that is about $13,000 a year spent counting lumber on paper. Cut that by 80 percent and the software saves around $10,000 a year in estimator time alone, against a Digs bill of $59 times 12 months times maybe five seats, which lands at $3,540 a year.

But estimator time is the small money. Rework is the big money, and rework is what bad information causes: the wrong tile ordered because the selection sheet never made it into the plan set, the beam sized off a superseded drawing, the warranty callback because nobody documented the flashing detail. Rework routinely runs 2 to 5 percent of contract value on residential jobs, so on a $600,000 custom home one avoided rework event is worth $12,000 to $30,000. One. That is the whole ROI case: if a shared, AI-searchable project record prevents a single blown order per year, the subscription pays for itself roughly four times over, and that math holds before you count the hours your PM stops spending as a human search engine for other people's paperwork.

Here is the sentence the sales deck will not include: none of that math requires buying the software from your lumber supplier. It works exactly the same if you buy it from Digs directly, or from a competitor like Higharc, which raised $95 million in June to bring suppliers onto its own platform from the other direction. So the question is not whether the tool works. The question is what it costs when the company that supplies your materials also owns the rails your project data runs on.

The Part Nobody Announced

Read the press release again and notice what is missing. There is no statement about who owns the project data. There is no statement about what happens to your takeoffs, your specs, your selections, and your unit economics if you switch suppliers at the end of the five-year deal. There is no statement about whether Builders FirstSource gets aggregated visibility into the material costs of the 140,000 builders buying through the platform it now distributes.

I want to be fair about this, because I can already hear the counter: builders already hand their specs to their supplier to get quotes, so the supplier sees the data anyway. True, and irrelevant. Seeing one quote at a time is not the same as hosting the structured, AI-queryable, year-over-year record of every build your company produces. Fink calls it a CarFax for the home, and CarFax reports to buyers and insurers, not to the company that sold the brake pads.

Run the scale in your head. Digs already hosted projects worth an estimated $3 billion back in 2024, when the company was two years old. If even a tenth of Builders FirstSource's 140,000 customers put twenty homes a year through the platform, that is 280,000 homes a year of structured build data flowing through one vendor's database, distributed through the sales reps who already visit every one of those builders. Nobody is building a bigger single repository of how American homes get built, and the company that supplies the lumber gets to sit on top of it. Maybe nothing bad ever comes of that. A foreman plans for the weather he cannot see.

Why This Could Still Work

The strongest case for the deal is the distribution problem, and it is real. Small and mid-size builders are drowning in disconnected PDFs, and they do not have IT departments evaluating construction software. The Builders FirstSource rep who walks onto the site every week is, for better or worse, the only software salesperson most of these builders will ever meet. If the tool reduces cycle times and the rep installs it, that is adoption that no amount of conference booths would achieve.

Fink has also done this before, twice, with two exits, which is two more than most construction-tech founders can claim. And the graveyard lesson cuts both ways: Katerra died trying to own materials, software, and construction all at once, burning $2 billion, and Veev followed with $647 million. Builders FirstSource is not building homes here. It is building the data layer under homes, which is a thinner bet, though the instinct, own the builder's workflow end to end, is the same one that killed the others, and instincts do not change just because the spreadsheet is smaller.

What To Do Before the Rep Shows Up

Four things, in order. First, get the data terms in writing: who owns the project record, what export looks like, and what survives if you change suppliers. Second, price it honestly: $59 a seat is the sticker, but the real cost is five years of your institutional knowledge living on your supplier's platform, so treat the contract like a lease with an exit clause, not a subscription. Third, pilot it on one production plan set, not your flagship custom, because the 700-to-1 takeoff claim only matters if takeoffs are actually your bottleneck, and for most custom builders the bottleneck is decisions, not counting. Fourth, get a competing quote from a standalone tool before you bundle your software with your lumber order, because you would never accept a single bid on framing, and this is the same decision with a longer tail.

Limitations of This Analysis

The 700-takeoffs figure and the 80 to 90 percent time-reduction claim come from the CEO in interviews; no independent audit or third-party benchmark of Digs' takeoff accuracy exists. Pricing figures come from software aggregator sites, not the company, and are subject to change. The press release and the five-year commercial agreement contain no published data-ownership, portability, or aggregation terms, so the conflict-of-interest analysis is inference from the deal structure, stated as such, not a claim about the companies' intentions. The ROI math uses typical industry figures for estimator loaded cost, about $80,000 a year, and rework rates of 2 to 5 percent of contract value; your shop's numbers will differ, and custom builders with low takeoff volume will see thinner returns. Katerra and Veev are invoked as structural comparisons about vertical integration of materials and software, not as financial analogies. Builders FirstSource is not building homes, and that distinction matters.
