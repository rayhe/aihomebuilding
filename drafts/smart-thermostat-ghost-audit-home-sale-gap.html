Your Smart Thermostat Recorded Three Years of Data About How Your Home Actually Performs. When You Sold the House, Nobody Showed the Buyer.

By Priya Greenwood · July 27, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

A Nest on the hallway wall has been running for 1,247 days. Every five minutes it logs the indoor temperature, the outdoor temperature, how long the compressor ran, when someone walked past the motion sensor, and what the setpoint was. That is 359,136 readings, give or take a firmware update. It knows, in a way no inspector ever could, exactly how this house breathes when the temperature drops 15 degrees overnight, how quickly the living room recovers after a three-hour setback, whether the air conditioner cycles for 14 minutes or 40 to hold 73 degrees on a July afternoon in Dallas.

Last month the house sold, and the buyer never saw a single data point.

Nineteen Million Ghost Audits

Around 19 million connected smart thermostats were installed in American homes as of 2025, according to IMARC Group. Google Nest holds roughly 27% of the market, about 15 million households, with Honeywell and Ecobee rounding out the top three. Growth runs at 15% a year and shows no signs of slowing down, buoyed by utility rebate programs that hand out $50 to $100 per device and by the simple fact that a $130 Nest Learning Thermostat tends to sell itself once a neighbor mentions the app.

Each of those 19 million devices is running what amounts to a continuous energy audit. Not a simulation, not an estimate generated from county tax records and a satellite photo. An instrument physically measuring how the building envelope responds to weather, how the mechanical systems perform under real load, and how efficiently the ductwork delivers conditioned air to the rooms where people actually live.

19M Connected smart thermostats installed in US homes as of 2025. Each one records HVAC runtime, indoor temperature, setpoint, and occupancy every five minutes. That is a continuous energy audit nobody reads.

A professional home energy audit costs $300 to $500. It takes two to four hours on site, requires a blower door, an infrared camera, and a trained technician, and delivers a report the buyer reviews once and files in a drawer. Fewer than 5% of home sales include one. Meanwhile, the smart thermostat on the wall has been doing a version of the same work for years, at five-minute resolution, for free, and the data vanishes when the seller resets the device or transfers the account.

What the Data Actually Reveals

In 2021, researchers at the University of Dayton published a peer-reviewed study in Energies demonstrating that machine learning models trained on smart thermostat data could predict a home's insulation R-values, furnace efficiency, and air conditioning SEER rating. Using gradient boosting on thermostat-derived features, their models achieved R-squared values above 0.99 on validation data for attic insulation, wall insulation, and furnace efficiency.

Underlying physics makes the method work. When outdoor temperature drops, the rate at which indoor temperature falls reveals how well the building envelope retains heat. A well-insulated attic loses heat slowly, while a poorly insulated one bleeds it. Because the thermostat captures both sides of that equation at five-minute intervals, three years of data across all four seasons provides enough thermal response curves to fingerprint the home's energy characteristics with remarkable precision.

On held-out test homes the models had never seen, prediction accuracy dropped to R-squared values between 0.59 and 0.68, a meaningful decline that reflects the difficulty of generalizing across building types, climates, and occupant behaviors. Still useful, though, especially for flagging homes whose measured performance deviates sharply from what their age, size, and climate zone would predict. A 2015 colonial in suburban Philadelphia whose thermostat data suggests attic insulation equivalent to R-11 when code requires R-49 is a finding worth $8,000 to $15,000 in remediation costs, regardless of whether the model pins the exact R-value.

The Transaction Gap

Energy is the single largest recurring cost of homeownership that receives no formal evaluation during a real estate transaction. Think about that for a moment. Property taxes are public record, insurance premiums are quoted before closing, and your mortgage payment is calculated to the penny. But annual energy cost, which the Insulation Institute and RESNET note exceeds both property taxes and insurance for the average American homeowner, gets a one-line estimate from the seller's agent based on whatever the current occupant happened to spend, which tells you almost nothing about the building and almost everything about how warm they like it in December.

Redfin, working with analytics provider Tendril, now displays algorithmically generated energy scores for roughly 110 million homes on its platform, according to the Rocky Mountain Institute. That is a meaningful step. But those scores are statistical estimates built from county records, square footage, climate zone, and vintage. They have never measured the actual house. An RMI study of nearly 8,000 homes across 27 states found that remotely generated estimates are "accurate enough" for broad screening, while acknowledging that individual-home accuracy varies widely.

Smart thermostat data bridges that gap. It is not an estimate. It is measured performance, recorded over years, under real weather conditions, with real occupants. Measured, not modeled. And nobody surfaces it.

Why Nobody Built It

Google, Ecobee, and Honeywell control the data. All of it. Each company provides homeowners with app-based energy reports, monthly summaries, and seasonal comparisons designed to help them save money on their own utility bills. None of them offers a buyer-facing export, a standardized performance report formatted for a real estate transaction, or an API that an appraiser could plug into a valuation model.

Sellers have no incentive to share. If the thermostat data reveals an aging furnace that cycles excessively or an attic that bleeds heat, that information works against the sale price. Disclosure law in most states requires sellers to reveal known material defects, but nobody classifies thermostat runtime analytics as a known defect, even when three years of data make the pattern unmistakable.

Meanwhile, the MLS, the backbone of residential real estate data, has no standard field for smart thermostat performance metrics. There is a checkbox for "smart thermostat" as a feature, the same way there is a checkbox for "granite countertops," but no structured data field for average heating runtime, seasonal energy performance, or measured thermal decay rate. Portland, Oregon, now mandates a Home Energy Score at point of listing. Massachusetts has proposed similar legislation. Both rely on modeled estimates, not measured data.

And when the house changes hands, the thermostat account typically resets. Google's Nest setup process encourages new owners to create a fresh account, wiping years of historical data from the device's cloud storage. But the physical instrument stays on the wall, while the institutional knowledge it accumulated walks out the door with the seller's Google account.

The Math Nobody Ran

Multiply 19 million smart-thermostat homes by the $300 to $500 cost of a professional energy audit. That is between $5.7 billion and $9.5 billion in diagnostic value sitting in existing cloud databases, collected passively, at no marginal cost, by devices homeowners already own. All the data exists, the machine learning to interpret it has been published and peer-reviewed, and the real estate transaction that could use it happens 5.6 million times a year in the United States.

ENERGY STAR-certified smart thermostats save households approximately 8% on heating and cooling bills, about $50 per year on average. That is the savings from using the thermostat. Surfacing what the thermostat knows about the building itself could be worth an order of magnitude more, especially in transactions where a $12,000 insulation remediation or a $6,500 HVAC replacement is hiding behind a seller's disclosure that reads, in its entirety, "No known defects."

What Would Have to Change

A standardized export format, something like a thermostat-generated energy performance certificate with seasonal heating and cooling runtime hours, average recovery rates, and equipment cycling patterns, would need to exist before appraisers, inspectors, or buyers could use the data systematically. That format does not exist today. Not even close.

Thermostat manufacturers would need to offer a transaction mode, an opt-in data package the seller authorizes at listing, anonymized for occupancy patterns but transparent on building performance. Google, Ecobee, and Honeywell would each need to decide whether this feature drives hardware sales (because buyers learn to demand homes with documented performance) or depresses them (because sellers resist installing devices that might reveal problems). In a market where 15% annual growth depends on utility rebates and app-store reviews, that calculation is not straightforward.

MLS operators would need to add structured fields. Appraisers would need a methodology for incorporating measured thermal data alongside comparable sales, something RESNET has been working toward with HERS scoring but has not yet connected to smart thermostat output. Disclosure law would need to evolve, or at least clarify whether three years of thermostat data showing a furnace that runs 40% longer than its rated capacity constitutes a known material defect.

The Counterargument

Thermostat data reflects occupant behavior as much as building physics. A family that opens windows in October, keeps the house at 65 degrees through winter, and runs the oven as supplemental heat will produce thermal response curves that look nothing like the same house occupied by someone who sets 72 and forgets it. Researchers at the University of Dayton partially controlled for this by incorporating setpoint data and occupancy readings, but the gap between validation accuracy (R-squared near 1.0) and real-world testing accuracy (R-squared of 0.59 to 0.68) suggests that occupant variability remains a confounding factor that current models do not fully resolve.

Privacy is the other objection, and it is legitimate. Thermostat occupancy data reveals when people are home, when they sleep, when they leave for vacation. A transaction export would need to strip behavioral data while preserving building-performance metrics, a technical challenge that is solvable but not trivial.

What to Do About It Now

If you are buying a home with a smart thermostat on the wall, ask the seller for the Nest, Ecobee, or Honeywell energy summary reports. These are available in each manufacturer's app and take two minutes to generate. They will not give you R-values, but they will show you average monthly heating and cooling runtime, seasonal patterns, and whether the HVAC system cycles more frequently than you would expect for the home's size and climate. A system that runs 18 hours a day in moderate weather is telling you something no inspection will catch in a two-hour visit.

If you are selling and your thermostat data shows efficient, consistent performance, share it. Voluntarily. Frame it as a competitive advantage in a market where buyers increasingly care about energy costs. A home with three years of documented low-runtime data is a stronger listing than one with a checkbox that says "smart thermostat."

If you are building, install a smart thermostat and activate it during the warranty period. Twelve months of post-construction performance data, collected before the first buyer moves in, creates a baseline that protects both the builder and the homeowner. It is the cheapest form of commissioning data you will ever collect.

That device on the wall knows more about this house than any inspection report, any energy model, any disclosure form. Nineteen million homes have that data right now. Nobody asks for it.

Limitations

This analysis relies on a single peer-reviewed study (University of Dayton, 2021) for the machine learning prediction accuracy claims. That study used a limited sample of homes in a specific climate region, and the generalizability of its models across the full range of US housing stock, climates, and HVAC system types has not been independently validated at scale. IMARC Group's 2025 market report provides the 19-million-device installed base figure, aggregating manufacturer and shipment data rather than counting active connected devices. Some fraction of installed thermostats may be disconnected, factory-reset, or operating without cloud connectivity, reducing the actual pool of data-generating devices. National averages from consumer publications inform the $300 to $500 audit cost range, which may not capture regional variation or the cost of specialized diagnostics like duct blaster testing. Finally, the "fewer than 5% of transactions include an energy audit" figure is an industry estimate, not a rigorously surveyed statistic.
