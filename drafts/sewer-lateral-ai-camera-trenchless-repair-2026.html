Your Sewer Lateral Is Older Than Your Mortgage. The $299 Camera Knows Where It'll Break.

By Jake Kowalski September 12, 2026

Picture inspection day on a 1968 ranch, where the general inspector spends three hours crawling the attic, tapping outlets, and writing up a garage door sensor for a five-hundred-dollar fee. Nobody puts a camera down the sewer lateral, an inspection that costs $250 to $500 and takes about an hour, yet examines the single most expensive pipe on the property. Buyers skip it anyway, every day, in every market, closing on houses with a ticking pipe they never looked at.

Here is the part nobody tells you at closing: the sewer lateral, the pipe running from your foundation to the city main under the street, belongs to you, not the city, and standard homeowners policies exclude it entirely, a coverage gap that surprises roughly every homeowner exactly once, usually at the worst possible moment. When it fails, and the materials under most older neighborhoods are past their engineered lives, you are writing a five-figure check while your yard becomes an excavation site.

Know your enemy by name, because the pipe's material decides its obituary. Cast iron laterals last 50 to 75 years, though corrosion usually wins before the top of that range. Vitrified clay runs 50 to 60, with every joint serving as a welcome mat for tree roots. Orangeburg, the bituminized fiber pipe laid by the mile during the postwar housing boom, was rated for 25 to 50 years under ideal conditions and has been known to fail in as little as 10. The company that made it closed in 1974, while thousands of miles of it remain in the ground, quietly turning back into wood pulp.

Do the subtraction and it gets uncomfortable: a 1966 house with original Orangeburg is 60 years old on a pipe built for 50 at most, which means that pipe is not aging, it is expired, a verdict that sounds dramatic until you remember the material is literally wood pulp sealed in coal tar, doing its best impression of a pipe for six decades. A 1978 house on cast iron is 48 years in, entering the window where rust starts winning. PVC, the modern material, runs 75 to 100-plus years, so if your lateral was replaced after about 1990 you can probably relax, and everybody else should keep reading.

The Camera Got a Brain

Sewer cameras are not new, since plumbers have been shoving them down cleanouts for decades, but what happens to the footage afterward is. A company called SewerAI trained computer vision on 100,000 inspection videos, roughly three million minutes of pipe, and taught it to tag cracks, holes, offsets, and root intrusions automatically, building on a dataset that has since grown past 135 million feet of inspected pipe, the kind of training corpus no human inspector could watch in ten lifetimes. One contractor client now inspects 200,000 feet of pipe a month, and jobs that used to take weeks take a day.

WinCan, the outfit behind the inspection software most of the industry already runs, bakes AI defect coding straight into its reports, following NASSCO's PACP standard, which defines more than 200 defect codes with severity grades, clock positions, and footage markers. NASSCO itself published a position paper calling AI-driven automated defect recognition the future of condition assessment, a claim that makes sense at the scale involved: roughly 800,000 miles of public sewer and 500,000 miles of private laterals in the US, far more than any workforce could ever watch by hand, a scale argument that lands harder when you learn the American Society of Civil Engineers once priced the wastewater repair backlog at $129 billion against a fraction of that in actual funding.

A homebuyer should care because a PACP-coded report is a document, not an opinion. In the old world, the plumber surfaces, wipes his hands, and says the line "looks okay for its age," a sentence worth exactly nothing at the negotiating table. In the new world, the report reads code 41, fracture, at 38 feet, severity grade 4, with a still frame attached, and you hand that to the seller and ask for the $9,000 credit. That is the difference between a vibe and an exhibit.

The Math, Per Foot

When the camera finds trouble, two fixes compete. Trenchless methods, cured-in-place lining (CIPP) or pipe bursting, run $80 to $250 per linear foot, with a full residential lateral typically landing between $4,000 and $12,000. There is no trench across the yard, the job usually finishes in a day or two, the relined pipe is rated for 50-plus years, and pipe-burst HDPE replacements run up to a century, which quietly reframes the purchase from a repair into a capital improvement with a service life in the same league as a new roof.

Traditional dig-and-replace runs $50 to $250 a foot, a number that looks cheaper until the excavator pulls out and the second bill arrives. Restoration, driveway, lawn, sprinkler system, fence section, adds $3,000 to $8,000, pushing the all-in total to $10,000 to $25,000, while the yard sits as a construction zone for weeks as concrete cures and sod takes.

Work the break-even on a 60-foot lateral: lining at $150 a foot comes to $9,000, done, while digging at $125 a foot comes to $7,500 plus restoration, which means the dig wins only if restoration stays under $1,500, a threshold so low that one section of replacement driveway concrete, typically $2,000 to $4,000 on its own, blows past it before the sod quote even arrives. Now name a suburban lateral that crosses nothing worth restoring, because the driveway alone kills the dig's case. One industry analysis out of Utah put trenchless at 30 to 50 percent cheaper once restoration is counted, and their test market was Ogden, where the median home was built in 1966 and half the housing stock predates 1970, which is cast iron, clay, and Orangeburg territory. Sound familiar? It is most of America.

The $30 Phone Call

Now the part that makes insurance agents smile: a service-line endorsement on your homeowners policy, the rider covering the lateral, typically costs around $30 a year. One carrier's filing spells it out plainly, $10,000 coverage limit, $250 deductible, $30 annual premium, covering wear and tear, corrosion, root invasion, and hidden defects. Against a five-figure failure mode, thirty dollars is the rare insurance product an agent in Michigan called worth recommending near-universally for homes more than a couple decades old.

Run the expected value honestly before you dial: $30 a year for 30 years is $900 in premiums against a $10,000 cap. If your lateral sits past 60 percent of its material lifespan, or a mature tree stands within 20 feet of the line, that is cheap money; if your house went up in 2015 with PVC and no trees, the insurer is getting the better end. Both things can be true at once. The endorsement is not magic. It is arithmetic with a deductible.

What the Skeptics Get Right

Clark Howard has rained on this parade for years, and his math deserves its day in court. Service-line plans stay profitable for sellers precisely because most lines do not fail on schedule, and paying $6 to $13 a month into a utility plan for decades can easily exceed the cost of simply fixing the thing once, which is exactly why Clark tells readers to price the coverage against the failure odds rather than the nightmare scenario in the brochure. Stashing that money in a repair fund beats the policy for disciplined savers, he argues, and he is right, with one asterisk the size of a backhoe: the discipline part, since most people do not maintain a lateral repair fund. They maintain a vague intention.

Trenchless has limits too, and contractors who sell it sometimes mumble past them: a collapsed pipe cannot be lined, severely misaligned sections cannot be lined, and lining shaves a little diameter off the pipe, which matters on marginal 3-inch lines, so the camera inspection is not just a sales tool but the engineering gate that decides whether trenchless is even on the table. The AI inspection story needs the same honesty, because the technology is proven at municipal scale but nobody has verified how many residential plumbers hand you a PACP-coded report instead of a verbal shrug. Ask for the coded report by name before you book the scope, and if the dispatcher does not know what PACP is, book someone else.

Limitations

What this article did not prove, stated plainly: the cost ranges come from contractor-published pricing in Ohio, Washington, and the Bay Area, your market will differ, and no national third-party survey of residential lateral replacement costs exists, which means every per-foot figure here is a regional data point wearing a national costume until someone funds the real study. SewerAI and WinCan sell to municipalities and large contractors, so residential adoption of AI-coded reporting is real but unmeasured. We found no reliable statistic on what share of homebuyers order sewer scopes, and we refused to invent one. Endorsement terms vary by carrier and state; the $30 premium and $10,000 cap are one carrier's filing, not a universal price. Pipe lifespans are industry rules of thumb, and soil chemistry, installation quality, and root pressure move individual pipes well off the averages.

What To Do Monday

Buying a house built before 1990? Order the sewer scope. At $250 to $500 it is a rounding error next to the general inspection, and it is the only look you will ever get at a pipe you are about to own. Demand the coded defect report rather than a verbal summary, because a grade-4 fracture at 38 feet is a negotiating document while "looks okay for its age" is a shrug in a polo shirt.

Already own the place? Then figure out your pipe material and its age: anything Orangeburg is living on borrowed time by definition, cast iron past 50 and clay past 55 with trees nearby deserve a camera before they deserve your optimism. Then call your insurer about the service-line endorsement, confirm root intrusion is covered, and check the deductible. Thirty bucks a year will not fix your pipe, but it will fix your month when the pipe decides to renovate your savings account.

The lateral is the only part of your house you cannot see, your policy does not cover, and your inspector skipped. For $299 you can finally look at it. Bring a camera. The pipe has been waiting sixty years for someone to ask how it is doing.
