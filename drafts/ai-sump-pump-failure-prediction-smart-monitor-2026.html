Your Sump Pump Is 9 Years Old. A $200 Outlet Can Hear It Dying.

By Priya Greenwood · September 23, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Somewhere under your house, a machine the size of a wastebasket is holding back the water table. You have never looked at it, and if I asked you its age you would guess, because nobody memorizes the birthday of a machine that lives in a concrete hole. If it quits on the wettest night of the year, the average insurance claim is $15,400, and there is a decent chance your insurance pays nothing at all.

That last part should rearrange your weekend plans. A standard HO-3 homeowners policy, the most common kind in America, explicitly excludes water "which overflows or is discharged from a sump, sump pump or related equipment." Read that again. Of all the ways water gets into a basement, the most likely one is the exact peril your policy names as not its problem. Coverage exists, as a water-backup endorsement costing roughly $40 to $300 a year, but you have to ask for it, and most sump-pump owners never do; they find out standing in two inches of water.

Nothing about the machine is mysterious. A sump pump lives about 10 years, according to the Department of Housing and Urban Development, and industry guidance says to start planning replacement at seven. Zoeller, the cast-iron workhorse brand, rates its upgraded float switches for three million cycles, and its failure modes make a short, boring list: the power goes out, the float switch sticks, the motor burns out, or nobody ever tested it. FEMA calls sump pump failure a leading cause of basement flooding in homes that already have drainage systems, not the homes without protection, but the homes that had it until it quietly expired. Check the date.

The outlet that takes its pulse

A company called PumpSpy sells a Wi-Fi outlet, $199.99 at Menards, that sits between your pump and the wall and does something no homeowner can: it learns what your pump's normal heartbeat looks like, then watches for arrhythmia.

Every time the pump runs, the outlet records the cycle count, the run time, and the current draw, and PumpSpy's servers compare each run against the pump's own history. A pump that starts short-cycling, firing every ninety seconds instead of every twenty minutes while the pit barely refills between runs, is telling you its switch is going bad and will stick permanently at the worst possible moment. Runs that stretch longer each cycle, with the motor laboring audibly while the water level barely drops, mean the pump is undersized for the inflow or the discharge line is freezing shut outside. Current draw creeping upward means the impeller is wearing or jamming, and zero cycles during a rainstorm means the power is out or the float is stuck, and when the pattern breaks your phone gets a text, an email, and a push notification. No basement stakeout required.

This is anomaly detection, the same family of math that flags fraudulent credit card charges within seconds of the swipe, now applied to a $300 machine sitting in a concrete pit nobody has looked at since the home inspection. It is not magic, and the company makes no peer-reviewed claims about prediction accuracy, which I will come back to, but the concept is sound: pumps rarely die instantly, they degrade, and degradation has a signature nobody recorded until now because nobody wanted to sit in the basement with a stopwatch.

PumpSpy also sells a battery-backup system, the PS2000, which monitors the primary pump, the backup pump, and the battery around the clock and test-fires the backup three times a week to prove it still works. No monthly fee, the monitoring cost is baked into the hardware, and a comparable battery backup in Zoeller's Aquanot line runs the backup pump for 33 to 166 hours depending on how often it cycles, which covers the vast majority of outage-driven floods.

$15,400 the average homeowners water-damage claim, per Insurance Information Institute data for 2019-2023. Water damage is 22.6% of all claims, second only to wind and hail, and carriers pay out roughly $13 billion a year.

The math nobody runs

Now the part I could not find anyone publishing: the expected-value arithmetic of an aging pump. Take a pump in year nine of a ten-year expected life, and suppose, conservatively for a machine past its design life that works hardest exactly when you need it most, that it has a one-in-four chance of failing in the next twelve months. That 25% is a scenario I am stipulating, not a measured rate, because nobody publishes residential pump hazard curves, which is itself a scandal for a $13-billion-a-year problem.

Expected annual loss: 0.25 times $15,400, which is $3,850.

Cost of prevention: roughly $200 for the smart outlet plus roughly $300 for a quality replacement pump installed before it dies instead of after. Call it $500 all in.

You are being offered $3,850 of expected risk for $500. Even if you think my 25% is double the truth, a 1-in-10 annual failure chance still prices the risk at $1,540 against the same $500 fix. Few home-improvement projects beat that risk-adjusted return, because the alternative is not a slightly worse pump; it is a finished basement full of water.

The insurance stack

The monitor is only one leg. Four legs make the full financial shield, and almost nobody owns all four. One, the monitor: about $200, no subscription, texts you when the rhythm changes. Two, the battery backup: roughly $490 for PumpSpy's monitored system, covering the number-one failure cause, power loss, for 33 to 166 hours. Three, the water-backup endorsement: $40 to $300 a year for $5,000 to $25,000 of coverage, the only leg your insurer sells you, and the leg that pays the claim the other three prevent. Four, the premium discount: carriers from Chubb to the regionals offer 3 to 15% off for monitored leak detection, which on an $1,800 premium is up to $270 a year back. Ask your agent before you buy. Many carriers require professional installation or an auto-shutoff valve for the top tier, and a sump monitor alone may not qualify everywhere.

Notice the asymmetry: manufacturers will happily sell you the $200 gadget and the $490 battery, while the $40 endorsement, cheapest by an order of magnitude, is the one you discover yourself, usually ankle-deep.

The strongest case against the gadget

All of that deserves its counterweight, stated at full strength. A $200 monitor screwed onto a twelve-year-old pump is denial with Wi-Fi, so spend the money on a new pump and a battery backup instead. Full stop. Most sump failures are power outages, which no monitor prevents; it only narrates. Those failure-prediction claims are vendor-described, never independently validated, and the system needs a baseline learning period during which it protects nothing, while your alerts depend on your home Wi-Fi and the vendor's servers surviving the same storm that kills your power, and your pump's heartbeat now lives on someone else's computer. If your pump is past year ten, skip the outlet, buy the pump, and thank me during the next atmospheric river.

What we did not prove

Honest accounting. That 25% annual failure figure is a stipulated scenario, not a measured rate; real hazard curves for residential sump pumps are not published by anyone I could find, which limits the math to illustration rather than prediction. PumpSpy's anomaly-detection accuracy has no independent verification. My $300 replacement figure is typical retail for a quality 1/3-horsepower cast-iron unit with DIY installation; hiring a plumber roughly doubles it. Insurance discounts vary by carrier and state and often require professional installation, and I did not cover sewage ejector pumps, whose failures are a different and more biological problem. And none of this helps homes without basements, which is a fine reason to feel quietly smug.

The call

If you have a sump pump and do not know its age, go look for the date code stamped on the housing or the cord tag, and if it is past year seven, order the replacement now, not during the storm. Bucket-test it quarterly by pouring water into the pit until the float trips and watching it pump, then test the battery backup separately and replace its battery every two to three years whether it looks tired or not.

Then call your insurance agent and ask, by name, for the water-backup endorsement, and ask what limit your finished basement actually needs, because the default $5,000 will not rebuild a finished basement with a bathroom. Forty dollars a year is the cheapest catastrophic coverage in homeownership. Buy it. Consider the monitor the cherry on top: buy it for the 2 a.m. text that tells you the power is out and the water is rising while you still have time to act. What matters is not how smart your basement is. It is that something down there is finally paying attention.
