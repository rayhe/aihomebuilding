Your Crew Gets Hurt at 85. The Stalled Rule Set the Alarm at 90.

By Marcus Washington · September 27, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Tuesday, 2:40 in the afternoon in Riverside County, where a stucco crew is browning out the second floor of a production home and the heat index on the foreman's phone reads 87.

Nobody stops, and somewhere below, the mixer is running, because the schedule does not pause for the weather and everyone on the wall knows it. That brown coat goes up before the sun moves past the garage wall, or it does not go up at all. One of the hawk men, call him Danny, has been on the wall since six. His shirt is dark to the belt line. He is twenty-six years old, strong as a bridge cable, drinking when he remembers, and at this exact moment he is standing in the most dangerous five degrees in American construction.

Not the 104 on the gang-box thermometer, and not the 90 degrees that would have triggered mandatory rest breaks under the federal heat rule that stalled in Washington. But the number that matters is 85. At a heat index of 85, workplace injuries start climbing, and that single number quietly redraws every site safety plan that was written around a thermometer instead of a heat index. That is not a union talking point. It is a Harvard and George Washington University finding, built from 28,000 geocoded 2023 injury records, and it means Danny is working inside a band of heat where bodies are already failing and no law that was ever proposed would have made him sit down.

Five degrees, and that is the whole story.

The study that moved the line

Researchers at Harvard and George Washington took OSHA's 2023 Injury Tracking Application data, the big-employer reports drawn mostly from high-hazard industries, and did something nobody had done at that scale. They geocoded all 28,000 injuries linked to hot weather and matched each one to high-resolution weather data for the exact date it happened. Then they watched what the injury curve did as the heat index rose.

The curve moved at 85: at a heat index around 85 degrees Fahrenheit, injury risk started climbing, and at 90 or higher, it climbed faster, which is the entire argument of this article compressed into a single line of data. These were not just heat stroke and cramps. They were the whole catalog: falls, struck-bys, the mistakes a body makes when it is running hot and the brain is running on fumes. They showed up indoors too, in shops and warehouses, anywhere the air went still and heavy, which means the superintendent who thinks of heat as an outdoor problem is working from a map of a different planet.

Two more findings matter for anyone who runs a crew: workers in states with their own heat-exposure regulations had lower injury risk on hot days. Where heat rules existed, they worked. And the senior author, David Michaels, an occupational health professor at GWU who used to run OSHA, said the 28,000 figure is almost certainly low, because most heat-related injuries never get recorded as heat-related in the first place.

Hold that thought, because it comes back with teeth.

The rule that stalled before it lived

In August 2024, federal OSHA proposed the first national heat standard for outdoor and indoor work, and the package started with written heat injury prevention plans for employers with more than ten people, water and shade beginning at an 80-degree heat index, and, at 90 degrees, the serious machinery: mandatory fifteen-minute rests every two hours, buddy-system symptom monitoring, warning signs in the blast zones, check-ins with lone workers, training for every supervisor on the company's dime, and six months of temperature logs.

Labor lawyers called it the most significant worker-protection rulemaking in a generation, and it never became a rule: public comment closed in January 2025, a post-hearing comment round closed that October, and then there was nothing. No target date for final action appears anywhere in the federal regulatory agenda, and the analysts who read those agendas for a living have stopped expecting one. Meanwhile the enforcement program that was supposed to hold the line, the National Emphasis Program that sent inspectors onto jobsites on high-heat days, expired on April 8, 2026, with no announced renewal. Before it died, it issued sixty citations under the General Duty Clause, mailed nearly 1,400 hazard alert letters, and pulled roughly 1,400 workers out of dangerous heat. That was the entire federal heat enforcement apparatus, and now there is no successor and no replacement.

In November 2025, Representative Mark Messmer of Indiana introduced a bill to kill the proposal outright, the Heat Workforce Standards Act, calling the rule sweeping and unworkable. Meanwhile the US Chamber of Commerce wants it withdrawn in favor of a performance-based approach. As of this writing, the rule is stalled, the repeal is pending, and the only federal heat protection on an American jobsite is the General Duty Clause. That is the legal equivalent of "do not let your people get hurt," enforced after the fact, which is another way of saying there is no heat rule at all until somebody is already in an ambulance.

The five-degree gap

Now line the two documents up next to each other, because one says injuries climb at 85 while its companion document required mandatory rest breaks, buddy monitoring, and lone-worker check-ins at 90. Read that again. What safety advocates spent two years mourning had a five-degree blind spot built into its spine: a band of heat, from 85 to 90 on the index, where the data says workers are getting hurt and the proposed federal standard required no mandatory rest at all.

Down at the 80-degree initial trigger, the rule did require water, shade, and rest breaks "as needed." But "as needed" on a production schedule is a phrase with no enforcement mechanism and no foreman brave enough to invoke it when the brown coat has to go up before the sun clears the garage wall. Protections with teeth, the scheduled, paid, documented rests and the second set of eyes on every worker, started at 90, while the injury curve started at 85.

This is the uncomfortable part, and I will say it plainly because nobody in the safety press has: the rule everyone is eulogizing would not have saved Danny. At 87 degrees on the index, the full machinery of the proposed federal standard, had it survived every court challenge and taken effect tomorrow, would have required his employer to have water on site and a written plan in a binder. It would not have required him to sit down.

Doing the math Washington never published

Remember the underreporting. Federal OSHA's own rulemaking record estimates that heat-related illnesses and injuries are underreported by a factor of 7.5, and heat fatalities by a factor of 14. Those are not activist numbers. They are the agency's numbers, published in the rulemaking record, which means the government priced its $7.8-billion compliance estimate against a death count it knew was a fraction of the truth.

Run the agency's own factor against the agency's own counts, and construction's share is the worst in the economy. CPWR's analysis of BLS data found construction accounted for 34 percent of all fatal heat-related injuries in 2023, 18 deaths on the books, more than a third of the national total, from an industry that holds a single-digit share of jobs. Multiply by 14, and that is on the order of 250 construction workers a year, dying of heat, against an official count of 18.

I want to be careful with these numbers, because they are extrapolations built from other people's estimates, and I label them as such in the fine print below, where the honest accounting lives. But the direction is not in doubt, and neither is the order of magnitude. We are not missing a few cases. We are missing most of them.

On the money side, the math is cleaner, because it comes from a state that bothered to count. New Mexico's occupational health office tallied 1,150 likely heat-related workers' compensation claims from 2022 through 2024, costing the system $7.4 million. Divide it out: about $6,400 per claim on average, and that average folds in the minor cases, while a hospitalization costs multiples.

Now price the alternative: the SlateSafety armband, an upper-arm sensor that estimates core body temperature and buzzes a worker and his foreman before heat stress becomes a medical event, sells for $500 a unit with software and cellular included, and its algorithm has been validated within three-tenths of a degree against ingestible temperature pills. One average New Mexico heat claim buys about thirteen of them, which means outfitting Danny's whole twelve-man crew costs less than a single average claim, and a fraction of one bad one. At a Texas Air Force base that had been logging more than thirty serious heat-stress injuries a year, the injuries went to zero after the system went in, which is the kind of before-and-after number that makes actuaries sit up straight. Zero is a number that gets a safety manager's attention the way nothing else does, because every other safety intervention in this industry's history has come with an asterisk and a confidence interval.

The case for killing the rule

I am going to state the repeal argument at full strength, because it deserves better than a straw man, and because my father carried a union card and taught me that the people who do the work usually understand the work better than the people who write about it.

On that, the Chamber has a point. OSHA's own economic analysis puts the proposed standard at $7.8 billion a year in compliance costs, and the rest-break provisions, the ones at the 90-degree trigger, are the most expensive part. A fixed national threshold is climate-blind in a way that borders on unserious: a Phoenix framing crew works in 105-degree heat for four months and acclimatizes to it. A 90-degree day in Seattle can put a roofer in the hospital because his body has never seen it, which is why one rule for both is not protection; it is paperwork with a thermometer.

And the paperwork is not trivial: written heat plans for every employer over ten people, six months of temperature logs, paid training time for every new hire and every returning worker, with acclimatization schedules to document. For the two-to-five-million-dollar residential shops this publication writes for, that is a part-time compliance job grafted onto a business that already runs on fumes in August, and every hour spent documenting acclimatization schedules is an hour not spent building houses. Representative Messmer's repeal bill says the quiet part out loud: a lot of builders experience this rule as the federal government discovering their jobsite exists and immediately making it more expensive to run.

What the repeal argument cannot get past is this: the injury data does not care about the rulemaking, and in the Harvard/GWU data, workers in states with heat regulations had lower injury risk on hot days. Where rules exist, they work. And the 85-degree inflection is a fact about human physiology, not a fact about federal overreach, which means nobody in Congress can repeal it. A general contractor in Riverside County can act on it Monday morning without a single piece of federal paperwork, which is more than the stalled rule ever offered him.

What this analysis does not prove

The honest accounting, because the numbers above deserve it.

For one, the Harvard/GWU study used injury reports from establishments with 100 or more employees, mostly in high-hazard industries, which means it captured the big commercial contractors and missed almost everyone who frames a house for a living. That excludes the small residential general contractors and subcontractors who frame, roof, and stucco most of America's houses, which is to say it excludes the entire readership of this publication. Its blind spot is precisely the reader of this article, which means if anything, it undercounts the world you work in.

Geocoded weather matching shows that injuries rise with the heat index, which is an association across tens of thousands of cases, not proof that heat caused any individual injury on the list. Some of those 28,000 would have happened on a cool day, and those 7.5 and 14 multipliers are the agency's rulemaking estimates, not measured counts. My "~250 construction deaths" figure is an extrapolation, built from CPWR's 18 and OSHA's 14, so treat it as an order-of-magnitude correction, not a census.

And 28,000 is injuries linked to hot weather in the dataset, and the senior author says the true number is higher. Nobody has the true number. That is the point.

Finally, this article does not compute how many days per year any specific metro spends in the 85-to-90 band, because that requires market-by-market heat-index climatology I did not verify for this piece. The gap is structural, and the day counts are yours to pull from your local National Weather Service climate data.

What to do Monday morning

No federal rule is coming to save anyone. The repeal bill may or may not pass. None of that changes what the data says, and the data hands you four things you can do without permission from anyone in Washington.

First, move your mandatory-rest trigger to 85 on the heat index, not 90, because the stalled rule set the alarm five degrees too high and you do not need a federal register notice to fix that on your own site safety plan. Write it down, brief it at the morning huddle, and mean it, because an unwritten trigger is a suggestion and a written one is a policy.

Second, watch the index, not the thermometer, because heat index is temperature plus humidity, and humidity is the multiplier that kills. A 92-degree day at 60 percent humidity reads about 100 on the index, while a 95-degree day at 20 percent humidity reads about 96. Your crew's bodies feel the index, not the air temperature, which means your gang-box thermometer is lying to you by omission, and every safety plan built on air temperature alone is a plan built on the wrong number.

Third, keep the temperature logs anyway, because the proposed rule wanted six months of heat-condition records and the General Duty Clause is still the law, the NEP's sixty citations prove OSHA will use it on heat, and a logbook is the cheapest legal defense a small contractor will ever buy. A thermometer and a clipboard, and that is the whole investment.

Fourth, price the armband against the claim, not against zero, because nobody buys safety equipment in a vacuum and the only honest denominator is what the injury costs when it happens. One average heat claim in New Mexico's data cost about $6,400, twelve armbands cost $6,000, and the math is not subtle, and the Air Force base that went from thirty-plus serious heat injuries a year to zero did not do it with a binder full of plans.

Danny is still on the wall at 2:40 in Riverside County, the index is 87, and no law ever written would make him sit down, because every law ever written about heat at work either set its trigger in the wrong place or never became a law at all. Those five degrees between the data and the rule are yours to close, and no one in Washington is coming to close them for you. Somebody's kid is up there.
