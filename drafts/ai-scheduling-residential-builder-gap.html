McKinsey's AI Partner Cut a Data Center Schedule by 40%. Your Builder Manages Sixteen Subs in a Group Text.

By Marcus Washington July 28, 2026

Danny Ochoa builds four custom homes a year in Marin County. Each one runs between $1.2 million and $2.8 million. He coordinates an electrician, a plumber, two framers, an HVAC crew, a tile installer, a roofer, a drywall team, a painter, a landscaper, a concrete finisher, a cabinet maker, a countertop fabricator, a finish carpenter, an insulation contractor, and a gutter guy who only works Tuesdays. His scheduling system is a yellow legal pad, an iPhone group text with 43 unread messages, and a magnetic whiteboard in his garage that his dog knocked over in April. He has not updated it since.

Across the Bay, in Menlo Park, ALICE Technologies has raised $51 million in venture capital to solve construction scheduling with artificial intelligence. Its platform ingests BIM models and Primavera P6 project files, then simulates millions of sequencing and resource-loading combinations to find the fastest, cheapest path through a build. McKinsey formalized a commercial alliance with ALICE after five years of joint client work. Thirty-five clients. Schedule reductions of up to 20 percent across the board. In one documented case involving a global data center operator, the AI cut the construction program by roughly 40 percent by simplifying schedule logic and optimizing sequencing across more than thirteen identified inefficiencies.

Danny has never heard of ALICE Technologies. He would not be their customer if he had.

Where the Money Went

ALICE's platform requires two inputs that most residential builders do not produce: a Building Information Model with sufficient geometric and trade-sequencing data, and a baseline schedule in Primavera P6 or Microsoft Project format. BIM adoption in residential construction remains largely confined to subdivision-scale production builders and high-volume tract developers who can amortize the modeling cost across hundreds of identical units. A custom builder running five projects with five different architects rarely has a BIM model at all. His architect hands over a PDF set. His structural engineer marks up a few sheets. He prices it from there.

Primavera P6 is even rarer. Oracle's scheduling platform is standard on $100 million infrastructure jobs. It is not standard on a $1.8 million kitchen-and-addition project in Walnut Creek. Oracle's license alone starts around $2,200 per user per year, and that assumes someone on the team knows how to build a critical path schedule with resource leveling, lag relationships, and constraint dates. Most residential builders learned scheduling the way Danny did: by watching their foreman do it for a decade, then doing it themselves when the foreman retired.

ALICE's own marketing confirms the target. Their documented case studies involve Bouygues Construction, Skanska, and Zachry Construction, which used the platform to shave 28 days off a highway project. Toloka.vc, a Ukrainian syndicate that backed ALICE in late 2025, cited average savings of $12.1 million per $100 million project and 128 days cut from the construction timeline. Scale those numbers down to a $1.5 million custom home and the economics evaporate. A 12.1 percent savings on $1.5 million is $181,500, but the BIM modeling, P6 scheduling, and platform subscription required to get there would eat most of that on a single project.

What Seventy Days Late Actually Looks Like

A Procore-commissioned survey of 505 construction project owners conducted by IDC found that 77 percent of projects finish late. Average overrun: 70 days. Projects experience an average of six budget changes and five schedule changes before completion, resulting in a 15 percent average cost increase. McKinsey's broader analysis is worse: 98 percent of megaprojects experience cost overruns, delays, or both. KPMG found only 25 percent of projects complete within 10 percent of their original deadline.

Those statistics pool commercial and residential work together. But residential scheduling failures hit differently because nobody is paying the homeowner for the delay. A data center that runs 70 days late triggers liquidated damages clauses, draws on performance bonds, and generates insurance claims. A custom home that runs 70 days late means a family paying rent and a mortgage simultaneously, storing furniture in a pod, and explaining to their kids why Christmas is happening in an apartment this year.

Census Bureau data from the 2024 Survey of Construction puts the average US home at 9.1 months from permit to completion. In the Pacific region, where Danny builds, it stretches to 10.8 months. In the Middle Atlantic, the average stretches to 13.7 months. Those are averages. The tails are uglier. And the Houzz 2026 U.S. Home Study found 37 percent of remodeling projects exceeded their planned budget, with a quarter of homeowners admitting they never set one to begin with.

The $59-a-Month Alternative

Residential builders have options. Just not AI-powered ones. Buildertrend absorbed CoConstruct and now offers scheduling, client communication, and selection tracking for custom builders. Procore has a residential tier. PlanRadar claims 64 percent of US general contractors were using cloud-based construction software by mid-2025. But "using cloud-based software" and "running AI-optimized scheduling" are separated by several orders of magnitude in capability.

Clear Estimates charges $59 a month and targets small contractors "moving off spreadsheets for the first time." That qualifier tells you where most of the market still sits. Dataintelo's 2034 forecast report puts the construction scheduling software market at $4.6 billion. Residential construction accounts for 28.3 percent of that market. Small and medium enterprises hold 31.8 percent. Large enterprises take 68.2 percent. Follow the money and you find it flowing uphill, toward the commercial operators who already have BIM models, P6 schedules, and six-figure technology budgets.

A University of East London study published in Frontiers in Built Environment in 2026 reviewed 60 peer-reviewed papers on AI in construction management and found a persistent disconnection. Risk prediction systems and schedule optimization systems operate in parallel but almost never talk to each other. Lead author Dr. Jawed Qureshi put it plainly: "Projects generate enormous amounts of warning data every day, but nothing in the schedule actually changes when these signals appear." His proposed framework would connect risk detection to automated schedule adjustment. It remains a framework. Nobody has built it for a $1.5 million house.

What Closing the Gap Would Require

An AI scheduling tool for residential builders would need to work without BIM models, because those models will not exist for custom homes anytime soon. It would need to ingest a PDF plan set, a materials list, and a list of available subcontractors with their typical lead times and scheduling constraints. It would need to learn from historical project data that most builders keep in their heads or in filing cabinets full of change orders from 2019. And it would need to cost less than the margin it saves, which on a $1.5 million project at a 15 percent gross margin is $225,000 total, with the builder hoping to keep $112,000 of that after overhead.

Nobody is building this. VC funding flows to ALICE, which targets $100 million projects. SaaS companies target Gantt charts and client portals. Academic researchers propose frameworks and test them on Palestinian high-rises. The residential builder, managing the single largest financial transaction most families will ever make, coordinates it with the same tools a catering company uses to schedule a wedding.

Danny's current project is running six weeks behind. The cabinet maker waited on the countertop template, which waited on the tile installation, which waited on the waterproofing inspection, which failed because the shower pan had a low spot that the plumber disputed. Danny found out about the failed inspection from a text message at 9:47 PM on a Tuesday. An AI could have flagged the sequencing risk before the tile went down. But the AI that exists needs a BIM model Danny does not have, a P6 schedule Danny has never built, and a subscription Danny cannot justify for four houses a year.

So he updated the whiteboard. The dog has not knocked it over again. Yet.
