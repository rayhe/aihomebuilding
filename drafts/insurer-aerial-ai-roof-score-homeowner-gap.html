Consumer Protection & AI

Your Insurer’s AI Has Scored Ninety Million Roofs From the Sky. It Won’t Show You Yours.

By Marcus Washington · July 26, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Joan Van Kuren had been a CSAA customer for nearly 40 years. She paid roughly $80,000 in premiums over that span and recently spent more than $200,000 remodeling her California home. Then a letter arrived: her policy would not be renewed. Her insurer cited hazards visible in aerial images of the property. Van Kuren says she was never told what the hazards were, never shown the photos, and never given a chance to fix anything before the decision was made.

She is not unusual.

Across the country, homeowners are receiving non-renewal notices triggered by aerial photographs they didn't know were being taken, analyzed by algorithms they didn't know existed, and scored against thresholds they have no way to see. Over the past decade, a sophisticated AI-powered surveillance apparatus has taken shape, one that covers virtually every residential rooftop in America. Homeowners are the subject of that system. They are not its customers.

Ninety Million Buildings, Eighty Property Attributes, Zero Homeowner Access

The dominant vendor in this space is CAPE Analytics, a San Francisco company that applies machine learning and computer vision to geospatial imagery. CAPE's platform covers more than 90 million residential and commercial buildings across the United States. Its clients include more than 80 insurance carriers, among them half of the top property insurers in the country.

CAPE's flagship product is the Roof Condition Rating, introduced in 2017. The system ingests high-resolution aerial imagery from partners like Nearmap and EagleView, then uses convolutional neural networks to classify roofs on a six-point scale: Severe, Poor, Fair, Good, Excellent, or Unknown. It also extracts more than 80 property attributes, from tree overhang and roof geometry to covering material and structure area. All of it is available on demand via API, in batch, or through a web application that underwriters use to make coverage decisions in seconds.

15+ States Number of U.S. states where CAPE's AI-derived roof condition scores have been approved by insurance regulators for use in ratemaking. Arizona, Colorado, Illinois, Indiana, Iowa, Kentucky, Michigan, Mississippi, North Carolina, Ohio, Pennsylvania, South Dakota, Tennessee, Virginia, and Wisconsin have all signed off.

Loss data backs up the scores. A CAPE-led analysis using millions of carrier-contributed loss records found that roofs rated Severe or Poor generate 2.5 times the wind and hail claims of roofs rated Excellent. Homes with significant tree overhang see 90% higher wind-related losses. Forty percent of all homeowners insurance claims involve the roof.

Those numbers explain why insurers are willing to pay for the data. They do not explain why homeowners cannot buy the same information about their own property.

The Imagery Pipeline You Never Consented To

The aerial images feeding these models do not come from a single source. The Geospatial Insurance Consortium, an industry-backed imagery program, claims to cover 99% of the U.S. population using drones, manned aircraft, and high-altitude balloons. EagleView, Nearmap, and satellite providers contribute additional layers. Combined, these sources produce a continuously refreshed visual record of nearly every residential property in the country.

Insurers examine these images for four categories of risk: roof problems like missing shingles or sagging, undeclared structures such as pools or trampolines, overhanging trees, and excessive yard debris. An AI model scores each attribute. An underwriter reviews the score. A letter goes out.

The homeowner's first encounter with this process is usually the letter.

When the Algorithm Gets It Wrong

In Northern California, a homeowner named Cindy Picos received a non-renewal notice after aerial photos allegedly showed her roof in poor condition. She asked her insurer to see the images. Her insurer refused.

At Farmers Insurance, a former employee named Nichole Brink described a system that used aerial photographs to drop policyholders for issues as minor as moss on the side of a house or tree branches that appeared to overhang a roof. Some of those photographs were more than two years old. In at least one case, Farmers reportedly mistook shadows on a roof for tree limbs.

Albert Fox Cahn, the founder of the Surveillance Technology Oversight Project in New York, had his homeowners insurance revoked after an AI-powered aerial assessment of his Hudson Valley property. His coverage was restored only because the insurer failed to send the legally required cancellation notice. Without that procedural error, Cahn said, "any leak, fire, or tree branch falling onto the hundred-year-old house that's been in my family for nearly 40 years could wipe out my bank account."

California Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara confirmed the pattern is systemic. His department has "investigated numerous complaints where flawed aerial imagery led to wrongful cancellations or nonrenewals," he said in a statement. "In several cases, insurers used imprecise drone or satellite photos to assess roof conditions, resulting in policies being incorrectly dropped due to erroneous data."

"Some of the aerial images that we've seen used to take adverse actions against policyholders barely identify the structure of the home, much less the detailed condition of the roof."
— Pennsylvania Insurance Commissioner Michael Humphreys

A Regulatory Response That Barely Exists

A handful of states have begun to notice. Connecticut issued a bulletin in March 2024 requiring carriers to submit their underwriting guidelines when coverage decisions are based on roof age or condition. Pennsylvania followed in May 2024, reminding insurers that aerial imagery alone is insufficient grounds for cancellation. If the images don't clearly show material damage, the state said, a physical inspection or licensed contractor report is required. Massachusetts issued similar guidance the same month.

California has pushed further, at least on paper. Assembly Bill 1559, introduced by Assemblymember Lisa Calderon, would require insurers to notify homeowners before capturing or obtaining aerial photographs of their properties, prohibit the use of images older than 180 days for coverage decisions, mandate that carriers share images with homeowners on request, and require that photos accompany any non-renewal notice.

None of these measures address the core asymmetry. Even if every state adopted California's bill tomorrow, homeowners would still have no way to access the AI scores that determine their premiums, no independent tool to assess their own property using the same technology, and no mechanism to proactively address issues before an insurer flags them.

Amy Bach, executive director of United Policyholders, a nonprofit consumer advocacy group, drafted a legislative proposal more than a year ago. It recommended sharing aerial photos with policyholders, detailing the precise basis for adverse decisions, and creating a formal pathway to challenge them. No state legislature has taken it up.

"This tech thing is really a very slippery slope for homeowners," Bach said. "We really don't have rules in place to protect the consumer."

The Tool That Could Help You Doesn't Exist

Here is the part that should bother anyone who has ever paid a roofer, trimmed a tree, or replaced a gutter in hopes of keeping their insurance premiums stable.

The same AI that scores your property for your insurer could score it for you. The same model that detects a sagging ridge line from 1,500 feet could tell you about it before your carrier's underwriter sees it. The same tree-overhang detection that adds 90% to your wind-loss risk estimate could flag the branch you need to cut before your renewal comes up.

Nobody sells that tool to homeowners.

CAPE Analytics serves insurance carriers, mortgage lenders, and real estate companies. Its API is priced for enterprise buyers processing millions of lookups. Betterview, another aerial property intelligence firm, markets to insurers and property managers. EagleView sells roof measurement reports to contractors and adjusters, but not AI condition scores to the people who live under those roofs.

A consumer version of this product would be straightforward. A homeowner enters an address. It returns a roof condition rating, tree-overhang risk score, and a prioritized list of maintenance items, cross-referenced against insurer underwriting criteria. Cost to build: modest relative to the enterprise platforms that already exist. Market size: every homeowner with a policy.

But the incentives run the wrong direction. Insurers pay for information that helps them shed risk. Homeowners would pay for information that helps them keep coverage. The former is a proven enterprise SaaS sale. The latter is a consumer product with a harder path to revenue.

What This Means if You Own a Home

A non-renewal for roof condition does not just cost you one policy. It can affect your eligibility with every other carrier. Insurers share loss histories and underwriting flags through databases like the Comprehensive Loss Underwriting Exchange. A roof-related non-renewal becomes part of your property's record, visible to every company you apply to next.

If you have been non-renewed based on aerial imagery, start by requesting the photos your insurer used. Not every state requires disclosure, but most insurers will provide them if asked directly. Compare those images against the current condition of your property. If the images are outdated, if shadows were mistaken for damage, if a repair completed since the photo was taken changed the condition, document the discrepancy and submit it to your carrier in writing. Connecticut and Pennsylvania explicitly require insurers to give homeowners an opportunity to challenge or remedy issues before coverage is dropped.

If you have not been non-renewed but want to know what an insurer's aerial review might find, the options are limited. Climbing onto your roof with a camera or hiring a licensed inspector to produce a written condition report gives you documentation you can submit proactively. Some insurers will accept a contractor's inspection as evidence that overrides an aerial assessment. It is an imperfect and expensive substitute for a $5 API call the insurer's underwriter makes in a fraction of a second.

Doug Heller, director of insurance at the Consumer Federation of America, put the asymmetry plainly: "Using images that were gathered without consumer awareness, or let alone consent, is really problematic."

Carmen Balber, executive director of Consumer Watchdog, was more direct. She called the current regulatory landscape "a Wild West" and said the technology insurers use "is all over the map and frequently inaccurate."

There is no federal law governing the use of AI-derived aerial property scores in insurance underwriting. There is no FICO-like right to see your score. There is no requirement that the same tools available to the party setting your premium be available to you. The industry's position, articulated by Bob Passmore of the American Property Casualty Insurance Association, is that aerial imagery is "just another method of doing something that insurers have always been doing."

He is right about the continuity. Insurers have always assessed risk. What changed is that the assessment now happens continuously, at scale, without the homeowner's knowledge, using tools the homeowner cannot access. The AI did not create the information asymmetry between insurer and policyholder. It industrialized it.
