The Rainfall Table Behind Your Gutters Stops at 1978. Your Roof Gets 2026 Rain.

By Frank DeLuca · September 24, 2026
← Back to articles

I have watched this scene from a garage doorway more than once. The storm lasts eleven minutes, and that is enough.

Skies on the Gulf Coast open all at once and without asking permission, and the brand-new five-inch gutters on a half-million-dollar house fill in about ninety seconds. Then the water goes over the front edge in a sheet, runs behind the fascia, and pours down the brick. The builder calls it a freak storm. The gutter was clean.

Nobody wanted to say the awkward part out loud, so I will. It did exactly what it was designed to do. Its design assumed Carter-era rain.

A table that ends in 1978

There is a document that governs how gutters and downspouts get sized in American construction. It is published by SMACNA, the sheet metal contractors' association, and it is the closest thing the trade has to a gutter engineering standard. Table 1-2 in that document lists rainfall intensities for design purposes, city by city: New Orleans at 8 inches per hour for a five-minute, hundred-year burst, and Los Angeles the same. Read the fine print: the table is based on records through 1978, forty-eight years ago. Somehow, the disco era is doing your drainage math.

SMACNA's own formula is simple enough to check. It takes 96.15 square feet of roof, at one inch of rain per hour, to make one gallon per minute of flow. From there it is multiplication: a 1,500-square-foot roof plane in a four-inch-per-hour storm sends 62 gallons per minute at the eave. A forty-foot run of standard five-inch K-style gutter moves about 48 gallons per minute under ideal conditions: correct slope, no debris, no ice, no excuses. The arithmetic is not close. The water does not care about your warranty.

Step up to six-inch K-style. The same forty-foot run moves roughly 80 gallons per minute, with forty percent more storage volume while it drains. That is the entire upgrade decision in two numbers: 48 versus 80.

Rain moved on without the table

Meanwhile the sky has been busy. According to the Fifth National Climate Assessment, precipitation falling on the heaviest one percent of days rose from 1958 to 2021 by 60 percent in the Northeast, 45 percent in the Midwest, 37 percent in the Southeast, and 21 percent in the South. EPA's climate indicators page puts it more bluntly: heavy precipitation events have been happening more often across the contiguous United States since the 1950s, and warming gets the credit. Your gutters were sized for the climate your grandfather's roofer worked in.

NOAA knows: its precipitation-frequency bible, Atlas 14, is being replaced by Atlas 15, which drops the old assumption that the climate stands still and folds observed trends into the statistics. Volume 1, the trend-adjusted historical record, was slated for 2026 and Volume 2, the forward-looking projection, for 2027, until the Commerce Department paused Volume 2 in the summer of 2025 before it was finished, a low-cost project nearly complete, then reversed itself within weeks and put the work back on. The historical correction may ship. The forecast your roof gets over its service life is back on the schedule.

This is not abstract: First Street Foundation warned back in 2023 that infrastructure designed on Atlas 14 data is already out of date, and that in the worst cases a supposed one-in-a-hundred-year flood is really a one-in-eight-year flood. An engineering firm following the transition put it plainly: the new numbers will require reevaluating and likely upsizing stormwater systems, because design storm volumes will generally increase, which means bigger pipes, bigger basins, and bigger gutters. Probably.

Nobody sizes residential gutters with any of this

Now for the part that should embarrass the industry. Nowhere does the International Residential Code require gutters at all. Section R801.3 asks for a controlled method of roof drainage only where expansive or collapsible soils exist, discharging five feet from the foundation. No national code specifies a gutter size, anywhere, for any house. Residential gutters are sized by contractor rule of thumb, and the thumbs do not agree with each other.

One widely quoted rule says a five-inch K-style gutter handles up to 5,520 square feet of roof, while SMACNA's own downspout pairing table says a 2x3 downspout, the standard mate for a five-inch gutter, drains about 600 square feet at one inch per hour. At a six- or eight-inch-per-hour design intensity, that 600 becomes 75 to 100. Those two sets of numbers disagree by nearly an order of magnitude, and the reconciliation, every time, is that the rougher number wins because nobody is standing on your lawn with a flow meter. Field rules fill the gap: no single-terminal discharge on runs over forty feet, about twenty-five feet of gutter per downspout on five-inch systems, maybe thirty-five on six-inch. These are heuristics, not engineering, calibrated to 1978 rain.

This is where the AI story actually belongs. Drone and AI roof inspection is already a real business: EagleView runs drone-powered assessments over a three-billion-image library, Loveland Innovations' IMGING platform does on-site drone measurements with deep-learning damage detection, and Hover turns a homeowner's smartphone photos into a 3D model with material takeoffs. Insurers photograph your roof from orbit to price your policy. Nobody, so far, photographs your gutters to run the sizing math. Six measurements settle it: gutter run length, downspout count and size, roof plane area, roof pitch, valley concentration points, and the local design intensity from the nearest NOAA table. Any phone camera and a tape measure can do it. Contractors just never bothered. Gutters are the part of the house nobody photographs until they fail.

What the upsell actually costs

Seamless aluminum in 2026 runs roughly $8 to $12 a linear foot installed for five-inch, $10 to $16 for six-inch. On a typical 150-foot house that is $1,400 to $2,200 against $1,800 to $3,000, which puts the upgrade delta around $400 to $800, plus a few larger downspouts at $60 to $120 apiece against $40 to $80 for the standard size. That is the number to hold in your head while the salesman talks.

On the other side of the ledger: a single fascia board replacement after overflow damage runs $600 to $6,000, per Angi's 2026 data. One prevented incident pays for the upgrade on most houses. And here is the design-life wrinkle that makes me grind my teeth: aluminum gutters are sold as twenty- to thirty-year components. They are being sized with intensity tables that are forty-eight years old. A twenty-year component is supposed to outlive the data it was designed from, and that data predates the trend entirely.

When to buy it and when to walk away

Buy the six-inch on a steep roof, seven-in-twelve or more, because steep roofs shed water faster and hit the gutter as a wall rather than a stream, and buy it on the Gulf Coast, the Southeast, and anywhere your design storms top six inches per hour, because there the math above is not theoretical. Buy it when a single run exceeds forty feet or a valley dumps two roof planes into one short section, because concentration is what kills gutters, not total roof area. And whatever size you buy, pair it with the bigger downspouts: a six-inch gutter on 2x3 downspouts is a wide river feeding a straw, and the outlet is the bottleneck that matters.

Skip it when you are in a moderate rainfall zone with short runs, downspouts every twenty-five feet, and gutters you actually clean, and I mean that last part: twice a year, spring and fall, ladder or blower or a service. Twenty years of overflow calls. Most were maintenance failures wearing sizing costumes.

Your six-measurement audit (do this before you sign) Measure each gutter run and count downspouts: five-inch systems want one 2x3 downspout per 25 feet, six-inch one 3x4 per 35 feet. Estimate each roof plane's square footage and pitch, and mark every valley that concentrates flow into a short run. Look up your local five-minute design intensity in the nearest NOAA Atlas 14 table, then run the inflow: roof square feet times inches per hour, divided by 96. If the number exceeds 48 gallons per minute on any forty-foot five-inch run, get the six-inch quote. Get the spacing in writing. Not the brochure.

Honest objections

The strongest case against everything above goes like this, and it deserves a fair hearing. Most gutter overflow is a maintenance failure, not a capacity failure. A clean five-inch gutter, correctly sloped, with downspouts every twenty-five feet, handles nearly every real storm outside hurricane country. That six-inch upsell is often contractor margin dressed as engineering, and the forty-percent capacity gain vanishes the moment leaves enter the picture. Furthermore, Atlas 15 is municipal stormwater policy, not residential practice: no code ties gutter size to intensity curves, so calling the data stale for a component nobody engineers is arguably a category error. At the subdivision and storm-sewer level the data gap is real and expensive. At the eave level, cleaning your gutters twice a year buys more capacity than an extra inch of aluminum.

There is truth in that, and I have watched enough clogged gutters pour over pristine fascia to respect it, but it proves too much. Clean gutters matter, and the maintenance argument explains why; it does not explain why the published intensity tables are old enough to collect Social Security, or why the industry's two sizing rules disagree by a factor of eight, or why insurers fly drones over your roof to price your risk while nobody runs the six measurements that would tell you whether your gutters survive the next eleven-minute storm. The rain changed and the tables did not; the least we can do is the math.

What this article did not prove

The capacity figures, 1.2 and 2.0 gallons per minute per foot, come from contractor whitepapers, not independent lab tests; treat them as directional. SMACNA Table 1-2 is a commercial standard, so describing residential gutters as sized to 1978 data is shorthand: the only published intensity tables in common use are stale, not a claim that your contractor consults Table 1-2. Those climate figures describe daily extremes, the heaviest one percent of days, which do not convert one-to-one into five-minute gutter-design intensities. No peer-reviewed study links gutter undersizing to claim frequency, and the fascia-repair cost is Angi's 2026 per-foot table, not a claims database. Treat the audit method in the box above as a back-of-envelope screen, not a substitute for a drainage calculation on a complex roof.
