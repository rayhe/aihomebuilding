Your Inspector Signed Off and Kept the Paperwork. Your Walls Kept Everything Else.

By Catherine Chen · October 8, 2026

With the wall still open, the cavity reads like a diagram: studs running plumb, orange PEX snaking through bored holes, a gray cable bundle dropping to a blue plastic box where the kitchen island outlet will eventually live. Somewhere in that cavity a copper elbow was not soldered quite right, and nobody can see it, not really, because the joint looks finished until pressure finds the flaw. Three weeks later the drywall crew tapes and textures the whole room, sealing a space you can never enter again, and fourteen months after closing a stain blooms across the ceiling below. When the builder's warranty administrator asks for proof that the leak came from original work and not something the homeowner did, the homeowner has proof of nothing, because the wall has been sealed for over a year and the evidence is entombed.

That opening scene is a composite built from the failure patterns in the sources below, not a single documented job. Documented everywhere, though, are the mechanics: a Houston inspection firm that has run thousands of new-construction inspections reports 45 percent of new homes carrying plumbing defects, 45 percent carrying electrical defects, and 60 percent with HVAC problems, most of them sitting in wall and ceiling cavities at the moment the drywall crew arrives. Fixing framing or rough-in defects after the walls close costs $5,000 to $30,000 per problem, according to the same firm's field data, and walls are timers: while they stand open, fixes are cheap and provable, but once they close, the same defects become expensive arguments.

The Code Requires Looking, Not Remembering

Oddly specific about timing and oddly silent about evidence, the International Residential Code tells you exactly when to look and nothing about keeping what you saw. Section R109.1.2 demands rough inspection of plumbing, mechanical, gas, and electrical systems before covering or concealment. Section R109.1.4 requires the framing inspection after the roof, framing, firestopping, and bracing are in place and after every rough inspection is approved. InterNACHI describes the framing inspection as the final opportunity for the inspector to view everything that will be concealed inside the structure. Read those sections closely and notice the absence: nowhere does the code require that the condition of the wall cavity be documented for the person who will own the house. Inspectors sign the card, the card goes to the jurisdiction's file, and you get the house.

Strip away the construction language and this is a records problem. Municipal interest in your wall cavity ends the day drywall goes up; the approval is a point-in-time judgment that the work complied, rendered by a person who will never live in the house. Your interest in the cavity lasts as long as the wall stands, which is decades. When a concealed defect surfaces in year two, the standard warranty covers delivery systems like plumbing and electrical for two years and structural items for ten, but coverage is not payment, and payment requires proving the defect was in the original work. For that $10,000 repair fight, the code hands you an inspector's memory and a signed card you have never seen: a strange evidentiary foundation.

The Camera That Remembers What the Inspector Forgot

Years ago, construction reality capture companies learned that the most valuable photograph on a job site is the boring one. OpenSpace, whose 360-degree camera workflows map imagery to floor plans with spatial AI, published the playbook: a claim-defensible record is consistent, captured on a fixed rhythm so the timeline has no blind spots; complete, covering the whole site including the routine corners nobody thinks will matter; automatically dated and pinned to the plan; and permanent, held in a centralized tamper-resistant archive so the evidence still exists when a claim surfaces months after the work is covered. Insurers have started pricing this in. Shepherd, an OpenSpace partner, recognizes well-documented projects with premium savings, which is the market's way of saying documentation is not overhead but risk pricing.

Most instructive is a detail from a hospital project, not a house. On an acute care job packed with copper lines and medical systems, Kitchell's field engineer Jeff Manders described scanning utility positions before wall closure, and the discipline worth stealing is his team's sequencing: average teams capture the ceiling once, right before the drywall crew arrives, by which point the firestopping sits behind ductwork and nobody can see the joint, while the good teams capture on the trade sequence instead of the calendar, walking after the bracing goes in, again after the mechanicals, again before the grid closes. Three extra walks across a couple of months buy the difference between answering an inspector's question in five minutes and cutting a hole in a finished corridor. On a custom home the sequence has the same shape, after framing, after rough trades, after insulation, because the wall cavity is the corridor.

From the other direction comes Buildots, the Israeli computer-vision firm that raised $130 million in September at roughly a $1 billion valuation, attacking the same window with helmet-mounted 360 cameras feeding a model that compares the site against the plan and the schedule. Its entire pitch is catching deviations during the rough-in phase rather than after drywall goes up, and the self-reported numbers are large: half the delays, about three months saved, revenue tripling year over year across more than a hundred large firms. Read those numbers the way a code reviewer reads a vendor's test suite, as claims about the vendor's best customers, which are data-center and commercial owners like Intel and Digital Realty, almost none of it residential. On a house, the current version of this technology is a project manager with a phone and a $50-a-month subscription, and nobody has published numbers on that configuration.

Run the Arithmetic, Slowly

Start with the Houston defect data at something like face value, applying the discount it deserves as marketing content from an inspection firm: 45 percent of new homes with plumbing defects, 45 percent with electrical defects, 35 percent with framing errors. Add ARC's post-drywall repair range of $5,000 to $30,000. Add a pre-drywall independent inspection at a typical $350 to $500, which is the going rate in most major markets. Now run the expected-value calculation nobody in residential construction runs, because the industry prices inspections as a cost and treats defects as bad luck.

Assume, conservatively, that only 5 percent of homes carry a concealed defect expensive enough to fight over, roughly a tenth of the inspection firm's headline plumbing number and deliberately pessimistic about the underlying data. Assume the average fight costs $10,000, the low-middle of the repair range, and that a pre-drywall inspection plus your own photo documentation catches or resolves the evidentiary question half the time it matters. Expected loss per build without documentation: 5 percent of $10,000, or $500, against a $400 inspection, which is barely positive at these deliberately harsh assumptions. But the moment you use the inspection firm's own numbers instead of my discounted ones, it stops being close: at a 20 percent defect rate the expected loss is $2,000 against a $400 inspection, a five-to-one return before counting the value of knowing where your pipes run when you hang a shelf in three years.

Sharper still is the price of the photograph itself. A 360-degree camera walk of a 2,400-square-foot house takes about thirty minutes. Software indexing it to the floor plan runs around $50 a month. For a small GC, the entire program costs less than the dumpster. For a buyer, the free version is a phone camera, a systematic walk, and a folder named with the date. What the AI layer adds over the phone album is retrieval: when the warranty administrator asks in month fourteen where the shower valve sits, the indexed archive answers in a search box instead of a scroll through 400 unsorted photos. Whether that convenience justifies the subscription on a single-family home is genuinely unproven. That the thirty-minute walk is worth it is not.

The Parts Nobody Puts in the Brochure

Skepticism first, because vendors sell certainty and wall cavities are full of uncertainty. Those headline ROI numbers, 41 percent fewer claims, five times more issues captured, ten times faster documentation, come from large commercial and data-center programs and are self-reported by the vendor. No independent study validates them, and nothing in them transfers cleanly to a residential GC running six custom homes a year with a superintendent who is also the safety officer, the scheduler, and the owner's nephew. Extrapolating data-center results to a cul-de-sac in Fresno is the kind of reasoning the code does not permit, and journalism should not either.

Second, a photograph is not an inspection. A 360-degree image of a perfectly photographed panel can hide a miswired breaker, an undersized conductor, a neutral shared across circuits. Cameras see geometry; inspectors' judgment sees compliance. Any buyer who substitutes a camera walk for the licensed pre-drywall inspection has purchased a very detailed record of a defect nobody qualified ever evaluated. Archives are evidence, not analysis. Confuse the two and you end up with 4K footage of your own mistake.

Most uncomfortable for buyers is the ownership question, because who holds the camera matters more than what the camera sees. When the builder runs the reality-capture program, the archive belongs to the builder, and your access to it in a dispute is a discovery process, not a download. Most purchase agreements impose no duty on the builder to share pre-drywall documentation with the buyer, and the IRC requires the builder to create none of it at all. A builder who refuses to let you photograph the rough-in during your own pre-drywall walk is giving you information, just not the information you asked for. Treat it accordingly, and get the right to document written into the contract before the slab is poured, because after the walls close, leverage is gone.

Where This Analysis Stops Short

Several disclosures belong here. The defect percentages come from one Houston inspection firm's marketing pages, not a peer-reviewed study, and a firm that sells inspections has every incentive to find defects. The $350-to-$500 inspection price is a typical range, not a quote, and it varies widely by market. OpenSpace's and Buildots' performance claims are vendor-reported with no third-party audit, and commercial results should not be read as residential promises. My expected-value math uses deliberately conservative assumptions, stated openly, because nobody publishes per-build concealed-defect rates for single-family homes. The composite opening anecdote is illustrative rather than documented. Builder photography-access rules vary by contract and jurisdiction; getting documentation rights in writing is general guidance, not legal advice, and a real estate attorney in your state will have sharper opinions.

What to Do Before the Drywall Crew Arrives

Buyers of new construction should hire their own inspector for the pre-drywall stage instead of relying on the municipal rough inspection, which is a compliance check, not an advocate. Walk the house yourself with a phone camera and photograph every wall cavity, every ceiling run, every panel, because the free version of this archive beats the $10,000 version of not having one. Get written permission to document into the purchase agreement before construction starts. Ask the builder where the rough-in photos live and who owns them; the answer predicts how the warranty conversation will go.

Builders should run the camera walk at rough-in on the trade sequence, after framing, after mechanicals, after insulation, not on the calendar. Thirty minutes, $50 a month in software, and you own the answer to every concealed-defect claim across ten years of warranty exposure. When the homeowner's attorney asks what sat behind that wall, "here is the timestamped archive" ends conversations that "our guys do good work" never will. The inspector's card sits in the jurisdiction's file while your evidence should sit in yours, and the drywall crew arrives Thursday, after which the wall keeps your secrets without keeping them for you.
