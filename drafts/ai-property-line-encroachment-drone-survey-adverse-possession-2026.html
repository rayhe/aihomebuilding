Your Neighbor's Fence Is 14 Inches Over the Line. The Drone Proved It in an Afternoon. The Law Gives It Five Years.

By Catherine Chen · September 28, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Somewhere in the suburbs there is a fence, built in 2019 by a man who meant no harm, that sits fourteen inches past his deed line. His neighbor found out this spring, when a refinancing appraisal ordered a survey and the surveyor's pins landed in the flower bed. Nothing about the fence had changed in seven years. Nobody had ever checked the map. For seven years. A refinancing appraisal is a strange way to learn where your land ends.

Fourteen inches of side yard is worth, in Bay Area terms, roughly the price of a used car. It is also, in California terms, further along a legal clock than most homeowners realize. Code of Civil Procedure section 325 does not care that the fence builder was polite.

The Survey Nobody Orders

HomeAdvisor's national data puts the average residential survey at $543, most landing between $376 and $768. In California the range runs higher, $1,000 to $5,000 per Angi, which is real money and exactly why buyers skip it. A Georgia agent's field note shows the cost of skipping: without a current survey, title policies carry a "survey exception" that excludes encroachments, fence-line disputes, and setback violations, the precise failures a survey exists to catch. That buyer paid for title insurance and then declined the one document that would have made it cover the boundary.

There is a right moment for the survey, and it is early. A survey contingency in the offer lets a buyer renegotiate or walk if the pins land somewhere surprising, but the contingency only works if the survey is ordered immediately, because surveys take days to months to schedule and complete. The deed, the old plat from the previous sale, and the fence that "everybody knows" marks the line are not substitutes. Old surveys were drawn with less precise instruments, and the fence that everybody knows about was built by somebody's predecessor on a Saturday.

What the Drone Changed

On February 23, 2026, the American Land Title Association and the National Society of Professional Surveyors updated the Minimum Standard Detail Requirements that govern ALTA/NSPS Land Title Surveys, and the update formally recognizes drones as standard practice. Sandberg Phoenix, the law firm that wrote up the change, notes the practical consequence: surveyors can finish faster with less risk, and the savings are real, especially on large tracts and rough terrain where a field crew costs a fortune to deploy. It stops short of what drone vendors imply. A drone collects data. A licensed surveyor still renders the legal opinion on where the line sits. Nothing in the February update lets a pilot replace a PLS stamp.

Memorize the numbers from aerial-survey practice, because they are the ones that will be quoted at you. Drone photogrammetry starts around $1,500 for a small site and delivers one-to-three-centimeter accuracy when the flight uses RTK corrections and ground control points, with orthomosaics and point clouds turned around in one to two business days. Traditional total-station and GPS work still resolves to millimeters and remains the instrument of record for legal boundaries, but for finding the fence before you build the fence, the drone is an afternoon where the survey crew was a week. Urban airspace is the catch that marketing skips. Class B, C, and D airspace needs FAA coordination, so the drone is not instant everywhere.

The Five-Year Clock, and Its Cousins

California's adverse possession statute reads like a dare. Five elements, all required: claim of right, open and notorious occupation, exclusivity, hostility, five continuous years, and payment of every tax levied on the land during those five years. State guidance from the Board of Equalization quotes the language straight, citing Dimmick v. Dimmick: no tax payment, no claim. Full stop. Five elements. Miss one, lose. This is the paradox most explainer articles miss. Almost always, the recorded owner of an occupied house is the one paying the tax bill, which means a squatter's path to title through adverse possession is blocked by element five before it begins. Element five does the killing. Where the doctrine really works is at fence lines, where a neighbor in good faith built fourteen inches over and maintained the strip for years, and even there the tax requirement is doing quiet demolition work on most claims.

Other states run their own clocks, and the variation is the point. Texas stacks four tiers: three years under color of title, five with tax payment, ten without, twenty-five at the long end. Florida asks seven years with color of title, or seven without it plus tax payments. Illinois wants twenty years, or seven with color of title and taxes paid. New York gives you ten years and then, since 2008, takes the small stuff back off the table: RPAPL section 543 says de minimis non-structural encroachments cannot ripen into adverse possession, so the fence that creeps six inches over a Westchester lot is a trespass claim, not a title claim, forever.

Notice what this table implies for generic advice, and how quickly a confidently quoted "five years" turns into a liability when the lot sits in Texas, Florida, or New York instead of the state the quoter had in mind. "Adverse possession takes five years" is wrong in forty-nine states and incomplete in the fiftieth. Anyone who quotes you a number without naming a statute is guessing, and the fence does not grade on a curve.

The Math That Should End the Argument

Take the Iowa figures, because Iowa published them. An Iowa law firm's boundary and easement dispute table puts legal fees at $2,500 to $15,000 over three to twelve months, with another $500 to $5,000 for the surveying itself and $1,000 to $7,000 if the case detours through mediation. Call the median case roughly $11,500 all-in. Against the $543 average survey, the asymmetry is about twenty-one to one. Frame that survey as a twenty-one-to-one insurance contract against the most boring dispute in residential real estate, and the purchase order writes itself. Homeowners will still skip it, the way drivers skip the umbrella they own. Skip it anyway and you will pay the twenty-one-to-one premium yourself.

What the Drone Cannot Do

Three limits, stated plainly. First, no drone reading is a legal boundary. The surveyor's opinion, rendered under state licensing law and sealed with a PLS stamp, is the instrument that moves title. The orthomosaic is evidence, excellent evidence, but evidence. Second, the adverse possession horror stories are mostly fiction. California's tax requirement and New York's de minimis bar mean the doctrine claims far fewer backyards than the internet claims, and the genuine risk is the good-faith fence, not the scheming neighbor. Third, flying a camera over your neighbor's lot to document their encroachment raises its own questions about privacy and trespass by aircraft, and the FAA's airspace rules do not disappear because the dispute is sympathetic.

What I Did Not Prove

Those dispute costs come from one Iowa law firm's published table, and coastal markets run hotter. Drone accuracy figures here are practitioner- and vendor-reported; no independent lab audited the one-to-three-centimeter claim for residential boundary work. State statutes move, so treat the five-state table as a starting point and confirm the current text with local counsel before relying on any clock. This article is not legal advice. It is an argument that a $543 document deserves to outrank a $15,000 lawsuit, which is a claim about arithmetic, not about your fence.

What to Do With This

If you are buying, put a survey contingency in the offer, order the survey the day the offer is accepted, and get the survey exception off the title policy. Removal requires a current survey done for this closing, not the previous seller's 2014 plat.

If you are building a fence, pool, or addition, survey before the contractor prices the job. The $543 pin-setting is the cheapest line item in any project that touches a property line, and the neighbor conversation that follows, awkward as it is, beats the demand letter by every measure that matters.

If you are already living next to a wrong fence, document it, date it, and talk to a real estate attorney in your state before the clock does your thinking for you. Five years sounds long until the fence has been there for four. Check the clock.
