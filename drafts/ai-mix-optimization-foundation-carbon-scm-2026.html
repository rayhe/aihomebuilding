Your Foundation Released 9.8 Tons of CO2 Before You Moved In. An Algorithm Shrank the Mix Without Touching the Strength.

By Frank DeLuca · September 6, 2026

Every concrete truck that rolls onto your job site carries a paper ticket on the dash. Mix design number, batch time, slump, water-cement ratio, yardage. That ticket is the truth of what is in the drum, and in twenty years of watching foundations get poured, I have learned to read one line that almost nobody else reads: the cement content. More often than not, it is higher than it needs to be, not because the batch man is wasteful but because everyone in the chain is a little bit afraid, and fear pours heavy.

That fear has a price, and for once the price is printed in kilograms. Cement manufacturing drives roughly 90 percent of concrete's carbon footprint, which means every extra sack of cement in the mix is mostly buying insurance, not strength. Engineers overspecify, plants batch rich, and drivers add water at the chute while the plant compensates by starting richer still. Concrete.ai, the UCLA spinout founded by professors Gaurav Sant and Mathieu Bauchy, looked at this whole chain of small cautions and put a number on the pile: concrete overdesign, they claim, costs the industry $2.4 billion a year and produces 10 million metric tons of CO2 nobody needed to emit. Treat those as company figures, because that is what they are, but the direction of the claim will be familiar to anyone who has ever watched a 4,000-psi ticket get ordered for a 3,000-psi slab.

The arithmetic nobody runs at the pre-pour meeting

Take a standard 2,000-square-foot home on a slab. Four inches of concrete over 2,000 square feet is 666 cubic feet, call it 25 cubic yards, and once you add the thickened edge footings around the perimeter you are looking at roughly 30 yards total. Now open the National Ready Mixed Concrete Association's industry-average environmental product declaration, the closest thing this trade has to an official scorecard, and read the 3001-to-4000-psi row, which is exactly the strength band your foundation lives in.

A straight-cement mix, no industrial byproducts, carries 326 kilograms of CO2 equivalent per cubic yard. Thirty yards times 326 kilograms is 9,788 kilograms. Nine point eight metric tons, before the house has walls, before it has a roof, before anyone turns on a light. Now read the same row with half the cement replaced by fly ash or slag, the supplementary cementitious materials your supplier probably stocks already: 200 kilograms per yard. Thirty yards at the leaner number is 6.0 tons. That difference is 3.8 tons of CO2, about what an average car emits in ten months, with nobody changing the strength, the schedule, or the price. In most markets the SCM mix costs the same or less, because fly ash and slag are cheaper than cement.

Those four rows are the whole story in one glance, which is why I keep coming back to the uncomfortable question they raise: if the 39 percent reduction is sitting in a published table and costs nothing, why is the straight-cement ticket still the default order on most residential jobs?

What the algorithms actually do

The answer is partly habit and partly math that used to be too tedious for humans, and that second part is where the AI enters, not as a robot on the pour but as software at the plant. AICrete, founded by Berkeley structural-materials PhD Parham Aghdasi, trained its models on producer batch records and trial data, then handed precast giant Clark Pacific four AI-optimized mixes to choose from; the winner cut cost 4 to 5 percent per yard and trimmed the carbon footprint 17 percent against the mix Clark Pacific was already using. Seventeen percent is not a revolution; it is a rounding error that survived a real trial, which in this business is the harder thing.

Giatec, the Ottawa construction-tech firm, took the bigger-data route: its Roxi engine trained on more than 300,000 unique mix designs and sensor data from 75 million cubic meters of placed concrete, then wrapped the predictions around its SmartRock job-site sensors so the model keeps learning from how the concrete actually behaves after it leaves the plant. Converge, out of London, built Mix AI to forecast strength gain, temperature, and carbon for a proposed mix before a yard is batched, and Stanley Black & Decker signed on to push it to North American contractors. Different doors into the same room, and the room is the batch plant's office, where the job is to stop designing every mix like the aggregate is going to betray you.

None of this replaces the spec writer, because every one of these platforms is an optimizer sitting on top of decisions a human already controls: which cement, how much byproduct, what strength target, what safety factor. What each platform finds is the leanest mix that still hits the numbers. It does not decide what the numbers should be; that part is still yours.

The honest doubts

Now the other side, stated at full strength, because a trade that skips this part is selling something.

First, the byproducts are running out: fly ash comes from coal plants, which are closing, and slag comes from blast furnaces, which are thinning out too. An optimization model is only as good as the materials the local plant can actually buy, and in markets without SCM supply the leanest achievable mix may be the straight-cement one. Uncomfortably, the map of low-carbon concrete is a map of industrial leftovers.

Second, the safety margin exists for a reason that no model erases. Aggregate varies pile to pile, and curing gets skipped on a hot Friday. A driver adds water at the chute because the slump died in traffic, and that added water is the oldest cheat in the book. Engineers overspecify partly from habit, yes, but partly because they are pricing real uncertainty, and an algorithm that trims the margin has not removed the uncertainty; it has just bet against it.

Third, the AI does not pour the concrete. A perfect mix design still meets the same driver, the same pump operator, the same weather. Field discipline, sensors in the drum, someone who actually reads the ticket, matters more than any model. Even the vendors' own best numbers, that 17 percent at Clark Pacific, come from precast trials, which are the controlled laboratory end of this business, not a residential slab poured at 2 p.m. in August.

And fourth, the cheapest moves predate all of this. Specifying 30 to 50 percent SCM content, or Type IL portland-limestone cement, requires zero AI, zero software, zero startups. It requires one line in the spec and a supplier who stocks the material. If your plant cannot tell you its mixes' GWP per yard, the algorithm conversation is premature; you have a data conversation first.

Big buildings are the practice round

California is already moving, which matters even if you build nowhere near it. California's CALGreen code added embodied carbon limits effective July 2024 for buildings over 100,000 square feet, with three compliance paths: reuse 45 percent of an existing structure, meet prescriptive GWP caps set at 130 percent of the NRMCA industry averages, or run a whole-building life-cycle assessment. As of January 2026 those rules reach non-residential buildings over 50,000 square feet, and Los Angeles is weighing a version that covers residential projects. Federal procurement is in the game too: GSA procurement under the Inflation Reduction Act sets a top-20-percent concrete benchmark of 284 kilograms of CO2 per cubic meter. Note the honesty here: none of these are residential mandates yet. Residential is where most concrete volume actually goes, and the code is warming up on the big buildings first. Anyone pouring foundations for a living should read that trajectory the way you read weather.

What to do on Monday

Put a number in the spec. "Foundation concrete shall not exceed 275 kg CO2e per cubic yard per supplier EPD" is one sentence, and it does more than any sustainability mission statement your company has ever printed. NRMCA's published average gives you the benchmark, and the supplier's own EPD gives you the bid comparison.

Ask for the EPD with every bid. Two tickets, same strength, different carbon, and you pick the leaner one. If a supplier cannot produce a product-specific EPD, that tells you everything about how that plant is run.

Specify the byproducts where the market allows: thirty to fifty percent SCM, or Type IL cement. Check local availability first, because the SCM map is uneven, but where the material exists this is the free move: it costs the same and cuts a third of the carbon.

Ask the plant about optimization software, Giatec SmartMix, AICrete, Concrete.ai, Converge, whatever the name. If the batch man looks at you like you asked for the wine list, the mixes have fat in them and now you know.

Do not buy the algorithm before you buy the basics. SCM content and Type IL cement are proven, cheap, and sitting in a published table. Treat AI as the second step, not the first. Anyone selling you the second step while you have not taken the first is selling software, not concrete.

Limitations of this analysis

The 9.8-ton figure comes from the NRMCA industry-average EPD, which covers cradle to plant gate only. It excludes trucking the concrete to your site, pumping it, and finishing it, so real delivered numbers run higher depending on haul distance. Thirty cubic yards assumes a simple slab on grade with thickened edges; a home with a basement, stem walls, or deep footings can use 40 to 80 yards, and the carbon scales with the volume. SCM availability varies enormously by region, and the 50-percent number is not achievable everywhere. Those AI performance figures, AICrete's 17 percent and Concrete.ai's $2.4 billion overdesign claim, are company-reported with no independent third-party audit I could find, and the precast trials do not map cleanly onto residential ready-mix. For the car comparison, the EPA's 4.6-metric-ton annual figure for a typical passenger vehicle is the reference.

Nine point eight tons, sitting under a house, before the house exists. Read the ticket, and write the number in the spec. A lean mix costs nothing extra, and the carbon it skips is the easiest kind there is: the kind you never had to pour.
