Your 1948 Colonial Has a 90% Chance of Lead Paint. The $339 Inspection Is the Cheapest Money in the Deal.

By Frank DeLuca · September 11, 2026

You are standing in the foyer of a 1948 colonial with good bones, original trim, and a kitchen that smells faintly of someone else's lemon cleaner. "Charming." "Priced to sell." And your agent is already talking about waiving the inspection contingency to compete. Before you sign anything, let me give you the one number that matters on this house. Ninety percent.

That is the probability this house contains lead-based paint somewhere on its surfaces, per the EPA's national survey of pre-1980 housing. For houses built between 1960 and the 1978 ban, the number drops to 76 percent, which is not the relief anyone hoped it would be. Across the country, 34.6 million homes still carry lead paint, per HUD's American Healthy Homes Survey II, and 93 percent of them predate the 1978 ban. These are not edge cases: this is the mainstream housing stock in half the suburbs in America, and your 1948 colonial is the median case, not the exception.

I have spent twenty years, across three states and two full housing cycles, watching buyers spend more time picking tile grout than evaluating six-figure liabilities, and lead paint is the quietest liability of them all. Lead paint is the quietest of them: it never shows up on a home inspector's clipboard unless you pay for the right test. It does not show up in an appraisal. But it shows up in the renovation budget the moment you sand a window sill without the EPA's lead-safe work practices, and it shows up in a pediatrician's bloodwork six months after your toddler starts crawling.

The Models That Predict It Before a Child Gets Poisoned

For decades, the system found lead paint exactly one way: a child got an elevated blood lead test, and a health department sent an inspector. That is backwards in every sense that matters, and the people who run public health departments knew it. So they built machine-learning models to find the risk before the poisoning.

In Chicago, the Department of Public Health partnered with University of Chicago data scientists to train a random-forest model on two decades of blood lead tests, home lead inspections, property value assessments, and census data, with a simple goal: predict which children are at risk of being poisoned so inspectors can prioritize houses on an intractably long list. CDPH described it as pioneering in the use of machine learning and predictive analytics in public health, and the city is now piloting risk scores inside pediatric medical records so doctors see the housing risk at well-child visits. It is open source, which means any city with housing-age data and inspection history can replicate it.

A separate model out of Warwick and NYU, trained on Illinois lead test records linked to birth records and housing data, estimated that 10,613 untested children had blood lead levels at or above 5 micrograms per deciliter on top of the 18,115 already detected. Read that again. Missing more than a third of the poisoned kids: that is what the current targeted screening policy did.

And then there is the result that actually belongs in a homebuyer's pocket. A 2022 study in GeoHealth, using crowdsourced dust samples from the DustSafe project, built a model that predicts whether a home has high or low lead dust concentrations at 75 percent accuracy using exactly two variables: the age of the house and whether paint is peeling on the interior, and that is the entire list. Two things you can determine yourself at an open house, with your eyes, in about ninety seconds. The researchers did not stop at the paper; they even built a mobile screening app around it, and the registration flow signs users up for free dust and soil lead screening, which is the rare case where an academic model quietly turns into an actual public-health intervention you can download.

The Math Nobody Does at the Kitchen Table

An XRF lead inspection, the gold-standard test where a licensed inspector presses an X-ray fluorescence gun against every painted surface and hands you a room-by-room lead map that doubles as your renovation plan for the next decade, costs $240 to $439. HomeAdvisor puts the national average at $339. Full risk assessments, which evaluate actual hazards rather than just mapping the paint, run $450 to $900. Meanwhile, HUD grant data shows lead hazard control running $500 to $15,000 per unit, with a median around $5,960 for stabilization, enclosure, and clearance work.

Run the expected value, because this is not complicated math, which is precisely why it is embarrassing that nobody does it.

Pre-1960 ~90% 0.90 x $5,960 = ~$5,360 $339 16:1
1960-1978 ~76% 0.76 x $5,960 = ~$4,530 $339 13:1
Post-1978 Low Near zero $339 Skip it

The inputs: prevalence figures from the EPA's national survey of lead-based paint in housing, and the $5,960 median hazard-control cost from HUD's grant program evaluations. Multiply. A pre-1960 home carries roughly $5,360 in expected lead-remediation exposure against a $339 test. That is a sixteen-to-one payoff on information, and it is the single highest-ROI line item in the entire purchase process, which is saying something in a transaction where buyers routinely pay $600 for a home warranty that covers almost nothing. Buyers routinely spend $500 on a general inspection of the HVAC and waive the contingency that would have found the five-figure paint liability.

If anything, the ratio is conservative, because HUD's grantee costs cover low-income program work, which skews toward interim controls, not the full gut abatement a market-rate renovation sometimes requires. If your 1948 colonial needs real abatement instead of encapsulation, the bill lands in the five figures and the payoff ratio on that $339 inspection gets even uglier for the people who skipped it.

What the Models Cannot Do

Now the other side, because I have watched buyers outsource their judgment to a phone screen before, and it never ends well.

A risk score is not an XRF reading. A model that says "low risk" did not scan the window troughs. It did not test the soil by the back steps where the exterior paint flaked off for forty years. Two variables, 75 percent accuracy: the GeoHealth model is wrong one time in four, and the exposure route that matters is a toddler's hand-to-mouth contact with dust you cannot see. HUD's own guidelines note that the most common cause of poisoning is ingestion of lead-contaminated dust, not eating paint chips. A visual guess at an open house cannot measure dust.

Encapsulation, painting over the hazard, is legal, cheap, and frequently the right call, but it is not permanent. Disturb those surfaces in a future renovation and the hazard comes back, along with the EPA's requirement that pre-1978 renovations use certified lead-safe work practices. An AI risk app that gave a house a green checkmark in 2026 will not stop a contractor from dry-scraping lead trim in 2028.

And there is a nastier failure mode. A buyer who sees a low model score and waives the inspection contingency has turned a $339 decision into a $15,000 surprise. That is the gamble. Use the model as a triage tool for deciding which houses to test first. Never as a substitute for the test.

The Playbook

If you are buying a pre-1978 home, here is the sequence that has served every buyer I have ever advised, and it has never failed anyone who followed it.

At the open house, run the two-variable test yourself. Build year from the listing, interior peeling paint with your eyes, which together take ninety seconds and cost nothing. Pre-1960 with any peeling paint anywhere is an automatic full inspection, and post-1960 pre-1978 with peeling paint is a strong yes.

Use the 10-day window. Federal law requires sellers to disclose known lead paint, and buyers get a 10-day opportunity to conduct a lead inspection during the sale. That contingency exists for exactly this purpose. Waiving it to win a bidding war on a 1948 colonial is how you end up financing someone else's deferred maintenance, except this particular maintenance item can run five figures and it shows up in a pediatrician's blood panel, not just your renovation budget.

Order the XRF, not the swabs. A licensed inspector's $339 XRF pass is definitive on every surface tested. If the budget is tight, a $75-to-$150 per-room XRF pass on the highest-risk rooms, kitchens, window frames, and any room a child will sleep in, beats a full-house swab kit.

Negotiate from the numbers. If the inspection finds lead, you now have a median $5,960 remediation exposure documented by a licensed inspector. That is not a scare tactic. That is a line item on your renovation budget, and it is negotiable, whether through price, seller credit, or a closing escrow for the work. Sellers who know their pre-1978 home has lead paint have either priced it in or are quietly hoping you walk into closing without ever doing this math, which is exactly the hope your $339 inspection is designed to ruin.

Limitations

The EPA's 90-percent and 76-percent prevalence figures come from the 1989-90 national survey, the most rigorous cohort breakdown ever published; the housing stock has turned over since, and no newer survey breaks prevalence down by build-year cohort. That 34.6-million-home figure is current to 2026 and confirms the scale, but it does not give me cohort-level percentages, so the expected-value table mixes survey vintages. Remediation cost data comes from HUD's low-income grant programs, which bias toward cheaper interim controls; market-rate full abatement routinely exceeds $15,000 and would make the inspection payoff ratios even larger. Those Chicago and Illinois models were built to triage public-health inspections, not to certify individual houses; their risk scores are not available to private buyers, and the buyer-facing evidence rests on the two-variable GeoHealth model, which tops out at 75 percent accuracy. No model replaces the XRF gun.
