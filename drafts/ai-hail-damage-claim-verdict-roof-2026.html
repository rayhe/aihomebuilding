The Hail Lasted Nine Minutes. The Fight Over Your Roof Will Last Nine Months.

By Jake Kowalski, September 15, 2026, Construction Technology

At 2:14 in the morning the ice came down sideways. Golf-ball hail, maybe a little bigger, rattling off the skylight like somebody was throwing rocks at the house. Nine minutes. Then quiet. Then, at 7:40 the next morning, the first knock on the door. A roofer with a ladder rack and a tablet, already holding an aerial photograph of your roof with every suspected hail mark circled in red.

He was not the first machine to look at your house. By the time the coffee was done, your insurer had one too. Somewhere above your neighborhood an autonomous drone was flying a grid, imaging your roof facet by facet, feeding the pictures to a damage-detection model trained on millions of shingle impacts. Same roof. Same dents. Two completely different answers about what they mean, and roughly $17,631 riding on the difference.

That number is not a guess. Verisk's 2026 U.S. Roof Report puts the average residential roof replacement at $17,631 last year, up 33 percent over the prior four-year average, while the average repair hit $4,699 (Verisk). Roofing line items now make up around 30 percent of all residential property claim dollars. And the driver is not hurricanes. Non-catastrophic wind and hail claims climbed from 17 percent to 25 percent of all residential claims between 2022 and 2024. Ordinary weather. Extraordinary bills.

Hail, specifically, is having a moment nobody headlines. Cotality's 2026 hail report counts 43.5 million U.S. properties at moderate or greater hail risk, with $17.84 trillion in potential reconstruction exposure. Last year saw 142 days with damaging hail, seven more than 2024 and well above the twenty-year average of 122. More than 600,000 homes were hit with stones two inches or larger (USA Today). Severe convective storms produced $42 billion in U.S. insured losses in the first nine months of 2025 alone, a figure Moody's has started calling the new normal (Insurance Business).

So the stakes are set. Now here is the part nobody tells you at the door: the machines that scanned your roof are very good at finding marks, and the marks are not the damage.

An AI that counts. A human that decides.

EagleView's Assess platform is the thing to understand, because it sits on both sides of this fight. It is an autonomous drone paired with AI and machine-learning damage detection that identifies hail, wind, and aging anomalies and generates automated reports. Insurers use it to settle claims faster, in beta testing they resolved claims after reviewing an average of six roof facets per home and cut loss-adjustment expense by up to 20 percent (GlobeNewswire). Roofing contractors use the same family of products for what EagleView's own marketing calls "lead canvassing." Same sensors. Same algorithms. Opposite paychecks.

Last September's update advertised higher accuracy and fewer false positives, plus a dedicated underwriting report for non-claims workflows, meaning the scan of your roof now happens before you ever file anything (GlobeNewswire). Your carrier may have imaged your roof while you were still sweeping ice off the porch.

Understand what the AI actually outputs: a count of anomalies. Dents, scuffs, displaced granules, spatter. It does not, and cannot, render the one verdict that matters, because the industry has never agreed on it. What counts as hail damage?

Feel for the bruise

Ask a Haag engineer and you get the crispest definition in roofing: functional damage is the diminution of watershedding capability or the reduction of a roof's expected service life. A hailstone that fractures the shingle mat leaves a bruise you can feel with your thumb. That is damage. Granule loss alone, without a fracture, is not, because Haag's research found that minor granule loss that does not expose the underlying bitumen "has not been found to cause any measurable loss of service life" (Haag Global). NRCA, the roofing contractors' trade association, draws the same line: impact-caused fractures start on the shingle's underside and are always detectable as a bruise; chipped edges and surface scuffs do not count.

Then ask IBHS, the insurance industry's own research institute, and the picture gets messier in a way that favors homeowners. IBHS built a hail cannon, a machine that fires ice with the mass and density of real hailstones at installed roofs, and discovered that frequent small hail does cumulative harm. In a 2025 study, the cumulative granule loss from a series of sub-severe impacts exceeded the loss from a single two-inch hailstone, after a year of natural weathering (Carrier Management). Federal forecasters only call hail "severe" above one inch, but the engineers now say the one-inch line understates shingle risk. Wear accumulates like mileage.

Both institutions are describing reality. That uncertainty lives in the middle, in the gray zone between "cosmetic scuff" and "mat fracture," and that gray zone is exactly where your claim lives. Independent adjusters and claimants' attorneys have been fighting over it for years, with photos of granule-stripped shingles the carrier called weathering and the adjuster called replacement-worthy.

This is where the AI hype deserves a hard look. The brochures promise "unbiased, independent data." But the algorithm only counts marks. Interpreting those marks, cosmetic or functional, still happens in a conference room, and it happens differently depending on who bought the software license. When the insurer's adjuster and your roofer both wave drone imagery at you and say opposite things, neither is lying about the pixels. They are arguing about what the pixels mean, and both of their employers have a financial interest in the answer.

What the adjuster knows that you do not

Before the adjuster ever calls you, they have three things you do not. First, the storm's hail swath: radar-derived maps show which streets got two-inch stones and which got pea gravel, so the carrier knows the maximum plausible damage before seeing your roof. Second, your roof's pre-storm condition: Verisk's Roof Condition Score grades every property, and roofs graded moderate or poor show roughly 60 percent higher loss costs, which gives the carrier a documented reason to attribute half of what they see to age. Third, your shingle's impact rating: IBHS publishes impact-resistance ratings for shingles covering about 95 percent of the impact-resistant market, graded Excellent, Good, Marginal, or Poor, and the institute says flatly that Marginal or Poor products should not be used in hail regions (Morningstar). If your builder installed a Poor-rated shingle in Texas, the adjuster knows. You probably do not.

That asymmetry is the whole game. So borrow the tools.

Run the Functional-Damage Test

Before you file a hail claim, run these five checks. They will not guarantee an outcome, but they will stop you from walking into the fight unarmed.

1. Get your own scan first. A drone roof inspection runs $150 to $300 in most hail markets, and any roofer with an EagleView-style report can show you the anomaly count and the test squares. Know the number before the carrier's number arrives. Photographs with timestamps beat memories in every dispute that follows.

2. Demand the bruise test, not the dent count. When the adjuster presents their report, ask one question: show me the mat fractures. Per Haag and NRCA, a shingle without a bruise is not functionally damaged, no matter how ugly the scuffs look. One exception is worth arguing: IBHS's accumulation finding: if your roof has taken multiple sub-severe storms, granule stripping across a slope can be a real service-life hit. Make them address that finding by name.

3. Look up your shingle's IBHS rating. Those ratings are public at ibhs.org/Impact Ratings. If your shingle grades Marginal or Poor, your case for replacement is stronger and you should say so out loud. If it grades Excellent, be honest with yourself: the adjuster's "cosmetic" call has peer-reviewed science behind it.

4. Price the claim before you file it. Hail deductibles are often a percentage of dwelling coverage, commonly 1 to 2 percent, which means $4,000 to $8,000 out of pocket on a $400,000 home before the carrier pays a dollar. On older roofs, carriers pay actual cash value, meaning depreciation, and the check can land well below the $17,631 average replacement. Do the subtraction: likely payout minus deductible minus the hassle. Sometimes the math says pay for the repair yourself.

5. Remember the file follows you. A filed claim, paid or denied, goes on your loss history. In hail states, where carriers are already replacing roofs faster than anywhere else (57 percent of hail-state roofs are nine years old or newer, versus 38 percent elsewhere), underwriters are watching roof-claim frequency. A denied claim that saved you nothing still costs you leverage at renewal.

A fair case against filing

There is a respectable version of the carrier's position and it deserves a fair hearing, because "the adjuster is ripping me off" is not always the right read.

Roofs are wearing assets. A twenty-year-old shingle has twenty years of UV, thermal cycling, and foot traffic in it, and hail does not get to take the blame for all of it. Haag's insistence on the bruise is not corporate cruelty; it is the only way to separate storm damage from aging without handing every homeowner a new roof every spring. IBHS's accumulation research is real, but it describes a process measured over years, not the morning after a storm, and adjusters who demand visible fractures are asking for evidence of the event you are claiming. That is a reasonable evidentiary bar.

And the storm-chaser economy is genuinely predatory. Door-knocking roofers who materialize after every hail cell are not all craftsmen; some are claims machines that file first and inspect later, and their business model depends on turning every dent into a $17,631 replacement. When an insurer pushes back, it is sometimes pushing back on fraud, not on you.

Which is why the test above cuts both ways. It protects you from the roofer who wants to bill a replacement for scuffs, and from the carrier who wants to call fractures weathering. A bruise does not care who is holding the camera.

What to do this week

If hail just hit your house: photograph everything from the ground, including collateral damage like dented gutters, downspouts, and AC fins, because adjusters treat collateral as corroboration of hail size and direction. Pull your policy's wind/hail deductible now, before anyone is on your roof. Then get your own drone scan. It is the cheapest leverage in this entire process.

If you are buying in a hail state: look up the IBHS impact rating of the roof before you close. A Poor-rated shingle in Denver or Dallas is a future claim waiting to happen, and the seller's disclosure will not mention it. Factor a Class 4 impact-resistant replacement into your offer math; some carriers discount premiums for them.

If your roof is over fifteen years old in hail country: understand you are carrying a depreciating asset that insurers increasingly treat as your problem. Verisk's data shows the industry is already pricing that in. The AI watching your roof is not going away, so the least you can do is learn to read what it sees.

Hail took nine minutes. The argument about your roof does not have to last nine months, but only if you walk in knowing the difference between a dent and a bruise. The machines can count. You have to judge.
