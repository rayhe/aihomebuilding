Your Camera Trailer Costs $15,750. Your Expected Theft Loss Is $3,000.

By Jake Kowalski · October 7, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

An electrician walked onto his residential job one morning and found his rough-in gone. Not damaged. Gone. Somebody had slipped into a sheetrocked new build overnight, pulled 75 feet of 12/3 and 100 feet of 8/2 out of the walls, and disappeared before the coffee truck arrived. Already paid for the rough-in, he got to do it twice, and the builder who hired him got to host the argument about who eats the second one, a scene that plays out on residential jobs every night in America. The only question is whether the camera watching it happen is worth what it costs.

Right now the security industry has an answer it really wants you to buy. It is a trailer with a mast, solar panels, and AI cameras that tell the difference between a person and a raccoon, and it rents for $1,500 to $3,000 a month with a live human verifying alarms in about ten seconds. Sounds great on the sales call. I ran the math for a normal custom home anyway, because nobody in the sales deck bothered, and the numbers came back rude enough to print.

How big the problem actually is

Start with the scale, because it is real. The National Insurance Crime Bureau and the National Equipment Register put annual job-site crime losses between $300 million and $1 billion. Less than a quarter of stolen material is ever recovered. California, Texas, and Florida take the worst of it, with the ten hardest-hit states accounting for more than 63 percent of all US construction theft. Copper and other metals top the shopping list, followed by lumber, hand tools, power tools, and heavy equipment, with August as peak season. None of this is controversial: every super with five years in has a story, and 70 percent of construction workers say they have witnessed theft on a job site in a given year.

Residential sites get hit in a specific, pickup-truck-shaped way that the equipment statistics, built for excavators and skid-steers, mostly miss. Commercial thieves steal excavators, and the average stolen machine is worth $29,258. Residential thieves steal what fits in a pickup: copper rough-in before drywall, wire, tools, a condenser unit. Last week in Portland, thieves stripped $50,000 of copper from three unoccupied houses. In South Carolina, a hospital job lost $100,000 in copper pipe plus a 2026 Bobcat out of locked Conex boxes. Your exposure on a single-family build is smaller per incident but the pattern is the same: the valuable stuff sits outside, unguarded, all night, for months.

What the trailer actually buys you

A full remote-monitoring program for a mid-size site runs $1,500 to $4,000 a month, hardware and monitoring included. A bare trailer rents for $1,500 to $3,000 a month; buying one costs $25,000 to $75,000. Compare that against a night guard, which is the alternative the industry quotes: $8,000 to $12,000 a month for round-the-clock coverage, or $130,000 to $280,000 a year per post. Against a guard, the trailer is a steal, but that comparison is doing a lot of quiet work, because almost no residential builder hires a night guard in the first place. Forget the guard. The real comparison is the trailer versus nothing, and nothing costs nothing.

One thing the AI part genuinely fixes is the false-alarm problem, which used to make monitored sites unusable. Old motion-triggered systems fired on headlights, branches, rain, and every ambitious raccoon in the county, and monitoring teams learned to ignore the noise. AI person-and-vehicle classification cuts false alarms by up to 90 percent versus plain motion detection, a figure multiple independent analyses corroborate. That matters because human attention on camera feeds collapses after about twenty minutes, so a system that only wakes somebody for an actual human is a fundamentally different product than a system that cries wolf at 2 a.m. over a plastic bag, which is why the tech itself was never the question, only whether the price of the tech matches the price of the risk. Vendors like Spotter now promise ten-second alarm verification by a live agent, flat monthly pricing, and no long-term contract past a three-month minimum, and on those specs the hardware delivers.

The math nobody in the sales deck ran

Take a typical $650,000 custom single-family build with a seven-month construction window, rent the trailer at the $2,250 midpoint for the full build, and you spend $15,750 before it prevents a single theft. Now price the risk it insures against, because residential theft data does not exist in clean form and I will show every assumption: call the chance of at least one theft incident during the build 12 percent, which splits the difference between commercial surveys and the reality that a single house is a smaller target, and put the average residential incident at $9,000 in materials and rework, covering a copper re-pull, a lumber hit, or a tools loss. Expected material loss: 0.12 times $9,000, or about $1,080.

Then add the delay, because stolen material is never just stolen material: it is a hole in the schedule, a crew standing around billing hours, and a reorder lead time stacked on top of the loss. One-third of projects report delays from criminal activity, and a two-week slip on a $650,000 build at current construction-loan rates burns roughly $1,750 in interest carry plus about $2,100 in extended supervision and overhead. Weight that by the chance a theft actually slips the schedule and you land near $1,925 in expected delay cost. Total expected loss without any monitoring: roughly $3,000, set against $15,750 in trailer rental. You are paying five dollars to insure one dollar of risk, which is a bad trade even before you remember that insurance this expensive usually comes with a deductible.

$15,750 vs. ~$3,000 Seven months of camera-trailer rental at the $2,250 midpoint costs $15,750. Modeled expected theft-plus-delay loss on one $650K custom build is roughly $3,000. The trailer loses by more than 5 to 1 on a single house.

When the trailer starts winning

None of that means the product is a scam. It means a commercial product is being upsold to the wrong customer, because moving it to a production builder running five concurrent sites and sharing two trailers drops the cost per site to about $6,300 while the chance of at least one incident across the portfolio climbs to roughly 47 percent. Expected portfolio loss approaches $15,000 against $12,600 in trailer cost, and that is before any insurance-premium reduction the carrier grants for monitored sites. In California, Texas, or Florida, where the theft density runs far above average, the math tilts further. Nobody says you rent for the whole build either: rough-in through trim is when the copper is exposed and the finishes are stealable, so three targeted months at $6,750 beats seven blanket months at nearly $16,000. The trailer is a fleet product, so price it like one.

The case against the trailer, stated fairly

Now the strongest argument the other way, because it deserves full weight. Cameras mostly produce evidence, not prevention. Ten-second alarm verification is real, but suburban police response to a nonviolent property alarm runs thirty to sixty minutes on a good night, and organized theft rings, which the equipment register notes often pre-sell what they steal, are not deterred by a mast camera. Nobody has run an independent randomized study of camera trailers on residential sites; every deterrence claim traces back to a vendor. And the most common residential loss, copper stripped from rough-in overnight, has a cheaper fix than any camera: do not put the copper there yet, lock the Conex, and bring appliances and condensers only when the building locks. Better still, specify PEX, because cross-linked polyethylene has essentially zero scrap value, which makes it the only plumbing material that is burglar-proof by chemistry. Zero scrap value. Zero motive. A material swap that costs nothing beats a $15,750 monitoring contract at the only game that matters, which is the thief's resale math, not yours.

What this model does not prove

Plenty. My 12 percent incident probability is an assumption, stated openly, because no residential-only theft probability exists in published data and the commercial surveys it leans on skew toward bigger sites. Nine thousand dollars blends copper re-pulls, lumber hits, and tool losses that vary wildly by market, so treat it as a placeholder with a defensible center, not a quote. Delay math uses full-draw interest as the peak case, while average-draw cuts it roughly in half. Vendor marketing stands behind the 90 percent false-alarm reduction figure across the board, and as one analytics vendor honestly notes, a big reduction number means little without a published detection rate alongside it. Insurance premium credits for monitored sites are claimed by monitoring companies without published actuarial tables. Recovery statistics describe equipment, not materials, and material recovery is worse in ways nobody quantifies. Treat every number here as a worked example with visible assumptions, not a quote.

What to do Monday morning

Building one custom home: skip the trailer. Your expected loss is around $3,000 and the rental is $15,750, so spend a fraction of the difference on a locking Conex, roughly $150 a month, schedule copper rough-in as late as the sequence allows, and take delivery of appliances, fixtures, and condensers only after the building locks. Specify PEX over copper wherever code allows and watch your most-stolen material category evaporate. Running three or more concurrent jobs: price one shared trailer across the portfolio, rent it for rough-in through trim only, and demand the vendor's detection rate and false-alarm rate in writing before you sign, because AI person classification is what separates a deterrent from an expensive raccoon camera. In a high-theft state, double your assumed incident probability and rerun the math; geography is the biggest variable in this whole model. And whatever you buy, remember the cheapest deterrent ever invented: after 6 p.m., nothing stealable visible from the street.
