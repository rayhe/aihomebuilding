Your Lot Could Legally Become Two. The AI Checked in 40 Seconds. The County Needs 14 Months.

By Catherine Chen · September 10, 2026

In 2021, California passed a law that was supposed to end single-family zoning, the most entrenched land-use regime in American housing, and for a few news cycles it genuinely looked like Sacramento had rezoned the suburbs by fiat. Senate Bill 9 let any qualifying homeowner split their lot in two and build up to four homes where one stood, with ministerial approval, meaning no discretionary review and no environmental lawsuit. Editorial boards called it historic; opponents called it the death of the suburbs. Five years later, the actual scoreboard is almost comically small: in all of 2023, California issued building permits for 140 homes under SB 9, not 140,000, and that figure comes from YIMBY Law's 2025 analysis, and the sting is that YIMBY Law is a pro-development nonprofit. This is the movement grading its own homework and handing itself an F.

Meanwhile, the newest AI land tools will tell you in under a minute whether your parcel qualifies. Type your address into Symbium Build and the parcel lights up with zoning, lot area, and exactly what the code allows; drag a second house onto the backyard and the model turns red where the setback fails, with an explanation attached. Acres.com launched plain-language AI zoning search in February 2026: describe the parcel you want in a sentence, get feasibility answers on a map, including decoded timelines and costs. The information problem that used to take three calls to the planning counter now takes 40 seconds.

So why has almost nobody done it? Because the feasibility question was never the binding constraint, which sounds like a consultant's slogan until you read the parcel-by-parcel feasibility math that Berkeley's Terner Center published before the law even took effect, at which point it reads less like analysis and more like a coroner's report.

The model predicted all of this in 2021

Before the law even took effect, UC Berkeley's Terner Center for Housing Innovation, working with MapCraft Labs, modeled the financial feasibility of SB 9 development on every single-family parcel in California. Their finding: the law makes development newly financially feasible on about 110,000 parcels. That is 1.5 percent of the state's single-family lots. On the other 98.5 percent, construction economics fail no matter what the statute allows, because land values, construction costs, and achievable rents simply do not pencil out. Nearly 97 percent of existing homes would be retained. No demolition wave. No transformation. Just arithmetic.

The model also predicted the shape of what little would get built, and reality has been obedient. Duplex conversions of the existing house, not lot splits, would dominate. Los Angeles reported 569 duplexes permitted in single-family zones in its 2024 housing element progress report, alongside 4 residential lot splits, four, in a city of four million people. The lot-split provision, the headline half of the law, the part that supposedly ended single-family zoning, is nearly dormant statewide. By May 2024, fewer than 500 homeowners in all of California had even applied for a lot split or new units under SB 9, according to state data cited by the Wall Street Journal, and only dozens of projects had been completed. One engineering firm, Riechers Engineering, says it holds 65 approvals statewide, roughly a third of all SB 9 approvals in California. Do that division. Somewhere around 200, statewide.

Here is the calculation nobody in the press releases ran. Take the 140 permitted units from 2023 against the Terner analysis's theoretical ceiling of roughly 700,000 enabled units statewide, and the realization rate is 0.02 percent. Take the fewer than 500 applicants against the 110,000 newly feasible parcels, and it is 0.45 percent. SB 9 changed what is legal. What got built barely moved.

What the AI screen gets right, and what it cannot touch

To be fair to the machines, the eligibility screen is genuinely useful at the margin. SB 9's disqualifiers are mostly map layers: very high fire hazard severity zones, floodplains, earthquake fault zones, wetlands, prime farmland, historic districts, conservation land, protected-species habitat, parcels with tenant occupancy in the prior three years. A parcel tool checks all of them in seconds, along with the geometric tests, each resulting lot at least 1,200 square feet, split no worse than 60/40, in an urban area. Symbium's product grew out of Stanford's Complaw research project and now serves Bay Area cities including San Mateo County, which means a homeowner in unincorporated Menlo Park can run the screen for free before spending a dollar.

What the model cannot do is the part that actually stops people, and this is where every vendor demo gets quiet. It cannot hire the licensed surveyor to draw the parcel map, a five-figure line item in the Bay Area that no software license replaces, nor shepherd that map through a county surveyor's office that still runs on paper and patience. It cannot sign the owner-occupancy affidavit the statute demands, a three-year commitment to live in one of the resulting homes that single-handedly kills the investor play and, per Terner's estimate, erased about 40,000 feasible units, roughly 6 percent of the total, all by itself. It cannot separate the utility connections or make a $400,000 backyard build pencil out where rents do not support it. Austin's residential Pre-Check tool is refreshingly honest about the boundary: the city states outright that it is a rules-based engine, not generative AI, and that staff review still decides. Every AI zoning product should carry the same disclaimer in the same font size.

The uncomfortable counterargument

A fair critic would say the AI angle of this story is a sideshow, and they would be mostly right. Information was never the bottleneck; money and occupancy rules were. The legislature seems to have figured this out: SB 450, signed in 2024, capped city review of SB 9 applications at 60 days, barred cities from imposing design standards on SB 9 projects that do not apply to ordinary single-family homes, and confirmed that charter cities cannot exempt themselves. That law attacked obstruction, not ignorance, because obstruction was the actual friction. And the duplex provision, the quiet half of SB 9, is doing nearly all the real work with no lot split, no parcel map, and no AI required. A faster "no" is still a no, and for 98.5 percent of lots the honest machine answer is "no." The tools matter only for the thin slice of genuinely feasible parcels, where they compress four to eight weeks of code research into an afternoon. That is a real saving, but it is not a housing revolution, and nobody selling you the software will volunteer the difference.

What to actually do with this

If you own a California single-family lot and the SB 9 daydreams have crossed your mind, here is the sequence that respects both your time and your wallet. First, screen the parcel before spending anything: Symbium Build is free in its partner cities, and everywhere else your county's GIS maps plus HCD's SB 9 fact sheet get you the same answer with more clicking. You need a lot splittable into two pieces of at least 1,200 square feet each at no worse than a 60/40 ratio, in an urban area, clear of every exclusion overlay, from very high fire severity zones to wetlands to the three-year tenant-occupancy tripwire that quietly disqualifies half the rental stock in older neighborhoods. Most lots fail here, which is precisely the point of the screen.

Second, if the screen says yes, price the unsexy costs before the exciting ones: the parcel map and survey, separate water, sewer, and electrical services, and the three-year owner-occupancy affidavit, which is the term sheet's poison pill for anyone who does not plan to live there. Note that SB 1123, passed in 2024, opened a ministerial small-lot subdivision path for up to ten parcels with no occupancy requirement, but it is a more complex instrument aimed at a different player, and meaningful uptake data does not exist yet.

Third, seriously consider the duplex conversion instead of the split, because it requires no subdivision, no parcel map, and no separate legal parcels at all, just a building permit for the second unit on the lot you already own, and Los Angeles's 569-to-4 ratio is the market telling you, in its bluntest voice, where the approvals actually are. Fourth, keep an honest calendar: SB 450 caps the city's review at 60 days, but survey, mapping, utility work, and construction still mean 12 to 18 months from "yes" to a rent check. An AI answered in 40 seconds; the calendar does not care.

Limitations

This analysis mixes uptake figures from different years and sources: 2023 permit counts, May 2024 application counts, and 2026 firm-level claims. No single HCD-published statewide SB 9 time series was located, so the roughly 200-approval inference drawn from one firm's "one-third of statewide approvals" marketing claim is rough by construction. Terner's 700,000-unit ceiling is a theoretical maximum from a 2021 feasibility model, not a forecast, and the 110,000-parcel figure is the more defensible denominator. Soft-cost ranges for parcel maps are Bay Area practitioner figures, not a surveyed dataset. SB 1123 is too new for uptake data, so its impact is speculative. AI tool capabilities are described from vendor announcements and press coverage, not independent testing, and none of these tools were run against a live parcel for this article.
