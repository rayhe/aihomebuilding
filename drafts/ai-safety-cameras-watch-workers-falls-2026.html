The Camera Caught the Missing Harness. It Couldn't Clip It On.

By Marcus Washington · September 7, 2026

My uncle framed houses in the Bay Area for thirty years. Both knees are shot. He tells a story about a guy named Pete who stepped backward off a second-story deck in 1994 and never came home for dinner. Pete's death certificate says one thing. My uncle says the real cause was simpler: nobody on that crew had any business being up there without a harness, and everybody on that crew knew it.

Thirty-two years later, a camera would have watched Pete skip the harness. It would have flagged the violation, pinged the superintendent's phone, and logged the whole thing to a dashboard that nobody on a 1994 residential crew would have known how to read, because that dashboard is built for a safety department that does not exist on most homebuilding sites. Then Pete would have kept walking. A bounding box is not a hand on a shoulder, and nobody has ever been saved by a push notification.

That gap sits at the uncomfortable center of the fastest-growing product category in construction tech. AI safety cameras now watch jobsites for OSHA violations in real time: missing hard hats, unguarded edges, ladders set at the wrong angle, workers at height without fall protection, all of it fed to a phone in somebody's pocket. Demos of this stuff are impressive, but nobody in the demo asks the question every worker asks first: is this machine here to keep me alive, or is it here to watch me?

Numbers that make the argument

Falls have been the leading cause of death in construction my entire life, and they still are, despite OSHA's National Emphasis Program on falls, despite the annual stand-downs and the toolbox talks, because gravity does not negotiate and production pressure does not pause for safety briefings. In 2024, the Bureau of Labor Statistics counted 1,032 construction and extraction worker deaths, with 370 of them from falls, slips, and trips. Go back one year and the picture gets worse: construction accounted for more than one in five of all workplace deaths in America, and nearly two in five of those construction deaths were falls. BLS's 2023 breakdown puts residential building construction at 62 fall deaths and roofing contractors at 110, a full quarter of all construction fall fatalities: roofers, the people nailing shingles over your head.

Then there is the money, and the money is stark. Per the National Safety Council, the average workers' comp cost of a fall or slip claim is $49,971, medical bills plus lost wages, and a workplace death averages $1.15 million. Nobody buys a camera hoping to amortize a fatality, but every builder with a spreadsheet should know the $49,971 figure by heart.

What the cameras actually do

DroneDeploy, the drone mapping company, built the most serious product in this space. Safety AI is a visual language model trained to look at a jobsite image and reason about it the way a safety inspector would. According to MIT Technology Review's 2025 reporting, the company assembled a "golden dataset" of tens of thousands of real OSHA violation images, then had human safety professionals grade the model's answers until it learned to think like them. Its specialty is ladder setups, which matters because ladders account for roughly a quarter of construction fall deaths.

EarthCam has shipped AI that detects workers at height since 2023, alerting when someone climbs a ladder or works a scissor lift without visible fall protection, and the company's edge-compute cameras push those alerts in near real time, which sounds like salvation until you ask who is on the receiving end at 6:45 on a Tuesday morning when the super is already juggling a concrete delivery and a no-show framer. Researchers are further along than the vendors admit: a 2025 Safe-Construct paper, built with Japan's YKK AP, showed AI catching violations a human spotter would miss, like one worker carrying a window rated for two, or a ladder being climbed while nobody is footing it, or two people sharing a platform rated for one. This is not a motion sensor; it is a machine that understands the choreography of a jobsite well enough to know when the dance is wrong.

Run the builder's math

Picture a mid-size residential GC with three crews, about 30 field workers, a couple of supers splitting their time between estimating and firefighting, and nobody whose full-time job is safety, because that is what the middle of this market actually looks like. Four to eight cameras, edge computing, a monitoring subscription: roughly $2,500 a month at current pricing, or about $30,000 a year. Treat that as a planning figure from vendor playbooks, not a quote, and get real bids before you budget it.

Set $30,000 a year against the NSC's $49,971 average fall claim. One prevented medically consulted injury every nineteen months covers the whole system, and any three-crew residential builder doing roof work and second-story framing can clear that bar. One prevented serious fall funds nearly two years of monitoring. That arithmetic is almost embarrassingly favorable, which is exactly why the rest of this article exists: favorable arithmetic is how bad purchases get justified.

Nobody in the sales decks mentions the second piece of arithmetic, and it is the most damning alignment in this story. About a quarter of construction fall deaths involve ladders, and ladders are the violation these vision models catch best. If you set out to design a machine that saves the most lives per dollar of compute, you would accidentally design this one, because the cheapest, most common hazard on a residential site is exactly what the AI watches, and the thing it watches best is the thing killing the most people.

What the demos don't show

Every demo ends with a bounding box around a missing harness. No demo shows what happens next, because what happens next is the hard part: the Technology Review piece carries the most important sentence ever written about this technology, which is that the system "requires an experienced safety inspector as an overseer." Cameras detect, and that is the entire extent of their power. Humans have to walk across the site, find the guy, interrupt whatever he is doing, and have the conversation the algorithm cannot have, the one where a foreman looks a man in the eye and tells him his family needs him alive more than the schedule needs him fast. On a residential crew of eight, where the working foreman is also swinging a hammer, that human barely exists most of the day.

Then comes alert fatigue, the quiet killer of every monitoring system ever deployed, from the hospital monitors nurses learn to sleep through to the car alarms whole neighborhoods ignore, and construction is no exception. ForaSoft's 2026 playbook puts the number bluntly: once false positives cross about five per camera per day, the safety team stops trusting the alerts within a month. Most residential GCs have no dedicated safety manager at all, which means notifications pile up by week three and get ignored by week six. At that point $30,000 a year buys surveillance theater: footage of violations nobody watched in time, stored on a server nobody will subpoena until after the funeral.

Questions from the crew

My union-family upbringing is why I keep coming back to the part the vendors skip. Workers see this clearly, and the vendors do not, because the vendors sell to the person who signs the check while the person being watched is never in the demo, never in the pilot survey, and never asked whether a camera that can get him fired feels like a safety device. Who owns the footage? Can a clip of a guy without his harness be used to fire him, or to fight his comp claim after he gets hurt? Does the camera log who was on site and when, and does that log get shared with anyone who asks, including immigration enforcement? These are not hypotheticals; they are the first questions out of any steward's mouth, and right now the answers are being written into vendor terms of service instead of negotiated at a bargaining table.

There is a selection problem too, and it cuts against the people who need this most. Cameras land on crews whose builders can afford $30,000 a year and have the management structure to act on alerts, which means larger, better-run shops with lower baseline risk. Meanwhile the highest-risk work in residential construction happens on small, non-union crews with thin margins and no safety staff, the exact operations that cannot afford this and would have nobody to answer the alerts if they could. A 2026 NYCOSH report found 81 percent of investigated construction deaths in New York happened on non-union sites, which means the technology is being sold to the safest segment of the market while the deadliest segment cannot buy it.

Why you might buy it anyway

Fairness demands the other side at full strength, and the other side is not the vendors' marketing copy but the body count, because 370 workers died from falls in a single year and that number is not an abstraction to anyone who has ever carried a casket. Three hundred seventy workers died from falls in a single year, and enforcement has a real track record of moving that number. Federal OSHA's fatal-fall investigations dropped nearly 20 percent in fiscal 2024, from 234 to 189, which the Department of Labor credits to focused enforcement and collaboration with labor and management. A camera that puts a superintendent's eyes on every ladder setup, every single day, is a form of attention that never takes a day off, never gets tired, and never decides the harness talk can wait until tomorrow. For a builder who already has a safety lead willing to act on what the system finds, that is a genuine capability and not a gimmick.

Here is the honest pitch, stripped of the sales language: the camera is a force multiplier for a safety culture that already exists, and it cannot create one. Crews that already stop work over sketchy setups get a cheaper way to exercise that instinct. Crews that do not will produce a very well-organized archive of the violations that eventually hurt somebody, which is a grim product to pay $30,000 a year for.

If you are hiring a builder, or running one

Builders, here is the entire test: buy the cameras only if someone on payroll has "respond to the alerts" in their job description: no safety lead, no system. After installation, run a four-week tuning sprint to drive false positives down, and tell your crews in plain language, in their language, what the footage is and is not used for. If you cannot promise it will never be used for discipline, say so, and take the trust consequences with your eyes open.

Homeowners, ask your builder how they monitor fall safety on your project. You are not auditing a tech stack; you are finding out whether anyone on that site has the authority to stop work over a missing harness. A builder with cameras and a safety lead who answers the alerts is serious. A builder with cameras and nobody watching the dashboard bought a very expensive witness.

Limitations of this analysis

No randomized trial has shown that AI camera monitoring reduces injury rates on construction sites. Everything resembling evidence is vendor case studies, lab datasets, or research prototypes like Safe-Construct that have not been tested at residential scale, and the absence of that trial is the single biggest hole in the industry's claims. DroneDeploy's golden dataset of OSHA violation images is proprietary and cannot be independently audited, which means the training data behind the most-cited product in this category is a black box. That $2,500-a-month figure is estimated from vendor playbooks and will vary with camera count, connectivity, and site count, so get real quotes before budgeting. NSC cost figures are all-industry averages, and construction-specific claims data (one insurance analysis puts the average construction injury claim at $41,353) suggests the real number for this sector sits in the same range but is not identical. Residential-specific 2024 fall fatality counts lag a year behind publication, so the 62-death figure is from 2023 BLS data. Federal OSHA investigation counts reflect enforcement activity as well as incidents, so they are not the same thing as total deaths.
