Your Deck Ledger Needs 18 Bolts. It Has Nails. That's Where 90% of Collapses Start.

By Jake Kowalski · September 20, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

In September 2019, a deck on the Jersey Shore let go during a family gathering and sent 22 people to the emergency room. No storm. No earthquake. Just a Saturday afternoon, a crowd of people, and a connection to the house that had been quietly losing its grip for years.

More decks collapse in summer than in the rest of the year combined. That is not trivia. It is the load profile doing exactly what load profiles do: the deck fails when it is full.

NADRA, the North American Deck and Railing Association, counted more than 60 million decks in the U.S. in 2025, 50 million of them residential, and estimates that 30 million are past their useful life. InterNACHI puts the share of decks that are completely safe at 40 percent. However you slice it, tens of millions of decks are standing on hardware that no current code would approve.

About 90 percent of deck collapses occur as a result of the separation of the house and the deck ledger board, allowing the deck to swing away from the house, according to InterNACHI's inspector training materials. Floor joists almost never break mid-span. The board that kills people is the one nobody ever looks at: the ledger, the horizontal member that is supposed to bolt the deck to the band joist of the house.

NADRA's preliminary ledger checklist reads like a crime scene report: approved screws and bolts versus nails only, rusted fasteners, a spongy feel from moisture absorption. Underneath the bullet points is a simpler statement. The most important structural connection in backyard America gets inspected by telling a hex head apart from a nail head, and most decks were built before anyone considered that distinction load-bearing.

Eighteen Bolts

Here is the part nobody at the barbecue knows. The International Residential Code prescribes exactly how a deck ledger attaches to the house. Table R507.2 calls for half-inch-diameter lag screws or through-bolts, staggered vertically, two inches from the top edge and two inches from each end. Spacing depends on joist span: for a 14-to-16-foot span, lag screws go every 11 inches on center. The fasteners must be hot-dip galvanized or stainless steel. The ledger must be flashed per R703.4 so water never touches the band joist.

Run the numbers on the most common deck in America, a 16-foot ledger on a mid-range span. Sixteen feet is 192 inches. Divide by 11 and you get 17.45, which rounds up to 18. Eighteen half-inch lag screws, staggered in two rows, is what the code demands to hold up one side of your cookout. Now walk out back and look under a pre-code deck. There is a good chance you will find a row of 16d nails and no flashing at all. Code-compliant fasteners present: zero.

Eighteen required. Zero present. That gap is the whole story.

Now load it. Sixteen adults at a summer cookout, 160 pounds each, is 2,560 pounds of humanity hanging off the ledger connection. The code's 40-pound-per-square-foot live load on a 16-by-12 deck totals 7,680 pounds of rated capacity, which the framing handles without breaking a sweat. The framing is never the problem. The connection is the problem, and nails loaded in withdrawal, which is exactly how a ledger pulls away from a house, hold a fraction of what a lag screw holds in shear. The code does not recognize nails as a ledger connection at any spacing. Not 16 on center, not 8 on center, not any. The number of code-compliant nails in a ledger connection is zero, and it has always been zero.

What the Camera Sees

Two inspection-software companies shipped AI directly into this blind spot this year. Palmtech 11, from Porch Group, includes an AI Image Defect Detector that flags potential defects in inspection photos and drafts the report language automatically. Spectora's AI Report Assist matches an inspector's spoken observations and photos to pre-approved report comments, with early-access inspectors reporting roughly 25 percent less time per inspection. Drones add a second angle. A rust streak running down a band joist is exactly the kind of visual pattern a defect detector is built to catch, and nail heads versus hex heads in a close-up photo is a classification problem with a right answer.

None of that is snake oil. It is also not an inspection.

What the Camera Cannot Reach

The ledger is the one part of the deck the camera cannot reliably see. It hides behind the house siding on one side and the deck's own framing on the other, and on most decks there is no sightline to it at all without pulling boards. NADRA's checklist tells homeowners to feel for sponginess and distinguish screws from nails by sight, which means getting underneath with a flashlight, not standing on the patio with a phone.

The rot test is a hand test. NADRA's guidance: probe suspect wood with an ice pick or a flat screwdriver, and penetration past a quarter inch means decay. No vision model performs that test. An AI that auto-labels a ledger "looks OK" from a photo of the skirting around it is not an inspection tool. It is a confidence machine, and misplaced confidence is the load case that kills people here.

InterNACHI's data carries one more uncomfortable finding. There is no correlation between deck failure and whether a building permit was issued, and no correlation between failure and whether the deck was built by a homeowner or a professional contractor. Permits do not predict survival. Only the fasteners do.

One more statistic, because it changes what you should worry about first: more injuries come from railing failures than from full collapses. The guardrail that flexes when you lean on it is a more common threat than the ledger, just a less cinematic one.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The 90 percent figure is an industry estimate from InterNACHI training materials, not a peer-reviewed study. The CPSC publishes no product-code-specific count of ledger-separation collapses. Injury totals vary by source: about 6,000 a year in NADRA-linked industry data, roughly 8,250 a year in a separate four-year industry dataset. Different methods, presented as reported. The Palmtech and Spectora claims are vendor-reported with no independent audit of AI image defect detection on decks. Fastener spacing values come from IRC 2021 Table R507.2 and Table R507.9.1.3 as republished by Washington's building code council and county permit offices; local amendments vary, and the code cycle your jurisdiction adopted is the one that governs your deck.

The Checklist

If you are buying: make the deck its own inspection line item, $150 to $300 for a basic deck inspection. Ask the inspector to photograph the ledger fasteners from underneath. Hex heads or bolt heads with washers mean somebody did it right. Round nail heads mean the ledger is hanging on withdrawal resistance, which is not a structural connection.

If you are building: verify the ledger before the decking goes down. Count the lag screws. On a 16-foot ledger at an 11-inch spacing you should see 18 of them, staggered, with flashing above. Photograph it, because the ledger becomes uninspectable the day the deck boards go on.

If you already own one: run the NADRA checklist once a year. Look for rust streaks under the ledger line. Push a screwdriver into the ledger board and feel for sponginess. Check that flashing exists between the ledger and the house. A ledger retrofit, through-bolting an existing ledger and adding flashing, costs a few hundred dollars in hardware and half a day of labor. It is the cheapest structural insurance in residential construction.

Thirty million decks are past their useful life, and two million get built or replaced each year. The replacement cycle is fifteen years, which means the industry is exactly one deck lifetime behind. The fix for the deadliest failure point is eighteen bolts and a roll of flashing. The bad news is that on most of those thirty million decks, nobody has counted the bolts yet.

Start with yours.
