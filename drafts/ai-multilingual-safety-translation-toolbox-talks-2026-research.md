**Angle:** One-third of the residential construction workforce is Latino, overwhelmingly the fastest-growing segment on site — and the mandatory daily safety talk is still delivered in English, or worse, in mumbled Spanglish through a foreman who speaks neither well. OSHA doesn't require English; it requires *comprehension* (29 CFR 1926.21(b)(2), Michaels 2010 policy statement). AI real-time voice translation now costs less than a case of water a month. This is a workforce story about who dies and why — with a concrete, cheap fix.

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes — directly. Homeowners hire GCs; GCs staff subs; the comprehension gap is a liability pipeline that lands on the homeowner's project via injuries, OSHA citations on their site, and schedule-destroying stop-work. For small residential builders, this is a compliance requirement most of them are quietly violating.

## Original contribution (novel calculation)
The "comprehension-gap math": a small residential GC running one bilingual crew (12 workers) has two options —
  a) Hire a bilingual safety consultant to run weekly toolbox talks: market rate roughly $150–$250/hr → $600–$1,000/month for one 1-hour weekly talk across the crew.
  b) AI real-time speech translation on the foreman's phone (Spanish audio output, crew comprehension quizzes in Spanish): $0–$50/month for the subscription layer.
OSHA's penalty math: a single serious training-violation citation at $16,550 buys roughly 1.4–2.3 YEARS of option (a) and roughly 27–69 YEARS of option (b). The break-even on the AI option is preventing one citation per several decades — in other words, any crew that has ever been cited is losing money on paper. (Assumptions stated; consultant rate from current market postings; AI cost from consumer-tier translation app pricing.)

Second original point: OSHA's standard is comprehension, not delivery — which means the standard small-builder practice (one English talk + one bilingual laborer "translating" on the fly, no documentation) satisfies neither. The actionable bar is: delivered in Spanish + documented comprehension check. That check is the part AI makes nearly free.

## Limitations
- Could not obtain per-firm citation data tying language-gap training failures specifically to citations (OSHA citation data doesn't tag language as a factor); the penalty math is illustrative, not an observed outcome.
- Translation accuracy for construction jargon (e.g., "scaffold coupler," "trench shield") in real-time AI tools is unverified — mistranslation of safety-critical terms is a genuine risk, acknowledged in the skepticism section.
- Foreign-born mortality over-representation (2021 workforce vs. 2024 deaths) mixes years; direction is consistent across BLS releases but the exact ratio blends datasets.

## Strongest counterargument (steel-manned)
Translation is the easy part of safety culture. The crews dying fastest are disproportionately foreign-born, often undocumented, working for labor brokers and subs-of-subs — the exact layer where no toolbox talk happens at all, AI or otherwise. An English-only GC buying a translation app for a crew he already trains in English is solving HIS compliance problem, not the structural one: fear of retaliation, misclassification, and piece-rate pressure that make workers skip tie-off to hit the daily rate. If the foreman still pays by the square foot, the Spanish translation of "tie off" won't keep anyone tied off.
