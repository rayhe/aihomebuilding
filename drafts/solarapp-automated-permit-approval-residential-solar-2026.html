Your Solar Permit Sat on a Desk for Six Weeks. The Algorithm Approved Your Neighbor’s in a Day.

By Catherine Chen · September 10, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Two identical houses on the same street sign solar contracts the same week in March. One family flips their system on before the summer rate hikes hit. Its neighbor is still waiting on a building permit when the first $300 bill lands. Nobody installed anything wrong and nobody chose a bad contractor, which is precisely what makes the outcome feel rigged. Rigged by geography. What separates them is the city limit sign between them, and which side of it has a computer reviewing the plans, because one building department handed plan review to an algorithm that checks the National Electrical Code the same way every time while the other still routes every application through a human reviewer with a backlog, a lunch break, and four hundred other permits in the queue.

Solar permitting in America is a postcode lottery where the losers pay in both time and money, a fact that would be merely annoying if the prize for winning were anything other than the basic administrative competence every taxpayer is already funding. Some jurisdictions approve residential permits in days; others take three to four business weeks, and a Department of Energy survey of installers found that many companies either avoid towns with burdensome permitting altogether or charge a 10 to 20 percent price premium for the privilege of dealing with them. Your roof is the same and your panels are the same, which leaves exactly one variable to blame for the price gap, and it is the paperwork, the slow accumulation of review cycles, revision requests, and resubmission fees that quietly adds thousands to your project before a single panel touches your roof.

The Free Machine Nobody Installed

In 2021 the National Renewable Energy Laboratory released SolarAPP+, an online tool that automates plan review for code-compliant residential solar systems. An installer enters the project details, the software checks them against the National Electrical Code and model building codes, and if everything passes, the permit is approved instantly, inspection checklist included. It costs adopting jurisdictions nothing, since installers pay $25 per approved application, a fee structure that flips the usual government software procurement story on its head by making the regulated party fund the regulator's efficiency tool.

Then NREL ran a control trial to prove it worked, which is the part of this story where a federal laboratory did something unusual and actually tested its own invention against the status quo instead of declaring victory in a press release. Five jurisdictions with different staffing levels and politics, Menifee, Pleasant Hill, and Stockton in California, plus Tucson and Pima County in Arizona, put real applications through the system. Average permit review time fell to less than one day, and projects moved from submission to final inspection 12 days faster than the traditional process with inspection pass rates comparable to human-reviewed projects, which undercuts the most predictable objection, that faster review must mean sloppier review, before it can even get started. Projects were permitted, installed, and inspected with inspection passage rates comparable to traditionally permitted projects. Collectively, the pilot jurisdictions saved 236 hours of review staff time during the trial.

12 days Faster from submission to final inspection for SolarAPP+ projects in the NREL pilot, with permit review averaging under one day.

That was 2022, and four years later adoption sits at a few hundred jurisdictions out of roughly 19,000 nationwide, a penetration rate so low it raises an uncomfortable question about whether the bottleneck was ever really the technology. New Orleans became Louisiana's first adopter in January 2025, when the national count was barely past 250. Last October brought the most significant expansion, when Colorado's Department of Regulatory Agencies selected SolarAPP+ for electrical plan review across more than 200 jurisdictions, covering residential solar and battery storage together, at no cost to the state. A free federal tool that demonstrably works sits on the shelf while most of the country has simply never turned it on, and if you want to understand why American infrastructure moves slowly, you could do worse than to start with the building departments that were offered a free machine and said they would think about it.

What the Delay Actually Costs You

Nobody has published a clean national number for the homeowner cost of permit delay, so here is the arithmetic. Energy Department data puts SolarAPP+ projects finishing 13 days sooner, a number that sounds modest until you convert days into dollars, which is exactly what the next paragraph does, because policy stories that never do the arithmetic are just vibes with footnotes. A typical 8 kW residential system offsets roughly $150 to $250 a month in utility bills, depending on your rate. Thirteen days of earlier operation is worth about $65 to $110 in electricity you did not have to buy from the grid. That is the floor, not the ceiling, because it ignores the installer soft costs that get baked into your quote in slow-permit towns.

On fees, the math is even more lopsided than it looks. Traditional residential solar permit fees commonly run $200 to $500 or more. SolarAPP+ charges the installer $25 per approved application, with up to three free resubmissions for mistakes. In the pilot, jurisdictions cut their own fees enough that the average permit came out $6 cheaper even after the platform fee. Six dollars. Not life-changing, but the direction of travel is the point. Fifteen minutes of data entry with a spec sheet in hand, one day of review, done. Fast. Bureaucracy has never cared about your feelings, but your wallet should care about this, because the difference between a $25 automated review and a $400 manual one is not an abstraction in a think tank report, it is a line item on the quote sitting on your kitchen table right now.

The Fine Print, Which Is Substantial

Enthusiasm first, then the audit. SolarAPP+ only handles standard rooftop systems, plus storage and main panel upgrades in the newest deployments, which means the weirder your roof, the older your electrical service, or the more creative your local amendments, the faster you fall back into the human queue you were trying to escape. Ground-mount arrays, structural oddities, historic districts, and local amendments still need a human, which in California includes fire setback rules that vary by jurisdiction and trip up plenty of applications. Your city also needs to be on the 2017 National Electrical Code or later and the 2018 I-codes or later, which sounds universal until you meet the building departments that are not.

Then there is the matter of who did the proving. All five pilot jurisdictions volunteered, which means they were already the kind of places inclined to fix permitting. Results from early adopters in Arizona and California may not survive contact with an understaffed rural building department running its first digital workflow. Installers at Sunrun, Tesla, and SunPower all reported a learning curve getting permit technicians trained on entering single-line-diagram data correctly, and the training burden lands on the contractor, not the city. Free to the jurisdiction is not free to the ecosystem. Somebody always pays.

The Queue That Actually Matters

Here is the strongest case against getting excited, stated at full strength. Permitting was never the only bottleneck, and in many markets it is not even the binding one. After the city approves your permit, your utility still has to approve interconnecting your system to the grid, and interconnection queues routinely add weeks or months that no automated plan review can touch. SolarAPP+ optimizes the city queue beautifully while the utility queue sits there untouched, which means an instant permit followed by a ten-week interconnection study is a faster line item inside the same slow project.

This is a fair criticism, and it does not kill the tool, because the existence of a second bottleneck has never been a good argument for leaving the first one in place, any more than a flat tire means you should ignore the empty gas tank. Both queues matter, and only one of them currently has a free federal solution sitting on the shelf, which is either an indictment of how we build software for government or a compliment to the one lab that managed to do it, depending on how charitable you feel this morning. NREL's own 2023 cost benchmark interviews found residential installers naming increased SolarAPP+ adoption as one of the most frequently mentioned ways to cut costs, which suggests the people who actually price your system consider the city queue very real. Fixing one bottleneck does not obligate you to fix all of them first.

What We Did Not Prove

A few honest boundaries before the advice. That 12-day figure comes from a five-jurisdiction pilot in 2021 and 2022, self-selected, in two states; it is not a national average and should not be quoted as one. My $65 to $110 delay-cost estimate is a calculation from the Energy Department's 13-day figure and typical bill offsets, not a measured outcome, and your rate structure will move it. One advocacy brief claims SolarAPP+ projects fail inspections 37 percent less often than traditionally reviewed ones, which sounds wonderful and comes from a source with an interest in wonderful-sounding numbers, so treat it as directional. Adoption counts are point-in-time press figures; check the live adopter list before citing them. Verify everything.

What To Do Monday Morning

If you are getting solar quotes, ask each installer two questions before you compare prices: which authority reviews my permit, and does that authority use SolarAPP+? An installer active in an adopting jurisdiction can credibly promise days; one stuck in a paper-based building department cannot, and the 10 to 20 percent premium data point is useful leverage when a quote looks padded. If your city is the slow one, that is also worth telling your council member, because the tool is free to them and the delay is costing you actual money.

And if you are one of the roughly 250 installers' worth of jurisdictions that adopted early, congratulations. Your permit was reviewed by a machine that never takes lunch, never loses a folder, and checks the National Electrical Code the same way every single time. The code does not care about your feelings. For once, that is the good news.
