The AI Said Your Roof Was Failing. The Roofer Said It Was Dirty. Your Policy Was Cancelled Anyway.

By Catherine Chen · September 7, 2026 · 9 min read

A homeowner outside Galveston, Texas opened her mail in 2023 to find that State Farm was not renewing her policy, and the letter cited the condition of her roof. She had never seen an inspector, and nobody had climbed a ladder. A drone or a satellite, from thousands of feet up, had decided her roof was done.

So she hired a roofing company, whose verdict was that the roof did not need replacing at all, and the gap between a $20,000 replacement order and a few hundred dollars of cleaning is the entire argument for giving homeowners the right to see the evidence. It needed a thorough cleaning.

She asked State Farm for the inspection report, the aerial images, anything she could hand her roofer to rebut, and State Farm declined. Its position, as she tells it, was that it has the right to survey a property at any time and is not required to share inspection reports. Out of that silence came a $20,000 replacement demand, driven by imagery she had never seen, from an inspection she was never told happened, with no legal mechanism to see the evidence used against her.

She is not an edge case but an early signal of where the entire industry has already moved.

The machine that now prices your roof

In April 2026, Eagleview launched Horizon, an agentic AI engine built on more than two decades of aerial imagery covering nearly every property in America. Roofing contractors can now request a color-coded map of every roof older than 15 years within two miles of last night's hailstorm, filtered by roof type, area, age, and condition. Insurance claims managers can ask the system to rank which properties in a storm zone deserve inspection first, ordered by damage likelihood and claim severity. Marketed as efficiency, the product is surveillance, sold wholesale to every carrier in the country.

Eagleview's drone product, Assess, tells carriers they can resolve up to 1.5 times more claims per day and cut loss-adjustment expenses by 20 percent, using AI that identifies hail and wind damage from captures flown four feet above the shingles. Meanwhile Verisk's 2026 U.S. Roof Report, built on aerial analytics, classifies 38 percent of American homes as having moderate-to-poor roof condition, a bucket that carries roughly 60 percent higher loss costs. Between a roof under five years old and one aged 11 to 15 years, the premium gap has tripled in three years, from $49 in 2022 to $155 in 2025, while average deductibles rose 22 percent in 2025 alone.

None of this is necessarily wrong, since a roof's condition genuinely predicts its losses, which is exactly why the industry's embrace of the technology makes economic sense even as it creates an accountability vacuum for individual homeowners. The question is not whether aerial AI works at the portfolio level, where the statistics are clean and the averages behave, but whether it is fair at the level of one house, one homeowner, one envelope in the mail. That is the whole fight.

What the model gets wrong

Amy Bach runs United Policyholders, a consumer advocacy nonprofit, and she has been collecting the failure modes. A grainy aerial image flags a skylight as damaged roofing material. Solar panels get misread as structural problems. Moss on a neighboring structure ends up attributed to the wrong property's file. Vast as the training data is, the ground truth for any individual roof is one photo from very far away, interpreted by a model whose error rate no carrier publishes, which means every underwriting decision carries an unknown error budget that the homeowner is never permitted to audit, price, or appeal.

That last point deserves emphasis, because neither the imagery vendors nor the insurers disclose false-positive rates for underwriting flags. When an algorithm denies your claim or kills your policy, you cannot interrogate the model, you cannot see the image, and in most states you cannot even compel the insurer to send a human being to look at the roof. According to the J.D. Power 2025 U.S. Property Claims Satisfaction Study, the average claim cycle reached 44 days, the longest on record. AI has accelerated the easy claims while the contested ones, the disputes and appeals and damage-scope disagreements, now take longer than ever, and the decisions that turn into fights are the ones where the only evidence in the room belongs to the insurer.

The law is one storm behind

In most states, insurers can use aerial imagery in underwriting with no prior notice and no obligation to share what they collected, a legal vacuum that exists not because anyone designed it but because the statutes were written before anyone imagined a fleet of aircraft photographing every roof in America on a subscription schedule. Anything visible from public airspace carries no reasonable expectation of privacy. Your roof, photographed from 10,000 feet, is public information that you are not entitled to see, which is a sentence that sounds like a riddle until you are the person whose policy was cancelled because of a photograph you are legally barred from examining. Read that again.

A few states have started to object to a system in which a homeowner can lose coverage based on photographs she is forbidden from seeing. Here is the map, as of this writing:

  • California: Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara publicly backed legislation after his department investigated "numerous complaints where flawed aerial imagery led to wrongful cancellations or nonrenewals." Two bills are in play. AB 75 would require 30-day advance notice before aerial inspections and give homeowners access to the images plus instructions on requesting them. AB 1559, authored by Assemblymember Lisa Calderon, would bar insurers from cancelling, non-renewing, or reducing coverage based on images older than 180 days, require carriers to share the images on request and attach them to non-renewal notices, and create a right to in-person verification. A third bill, SB 260, would prohibit using drone imagery as evidence to terminate home protection contracts, a narrower intervention aimed at the post-claim world where carriers have historically reached for any available pretext to shed risk. All were proposed or in committee as of the source dates; none are enacted law yet.
  • Massachusetts: a proposed bill would give homeowners the right to see the images, know when they were taken, and use an appeals process.
  • Connecticut: the Division of Insurance issued guidance in March 2025.
  • Pennsylvania: issued a bulletin regulating aerial images in 2024.
  • Everywhere else: nothing at all. No notice, no access, no appeal.

Five states offer some protection, which means forty-five offer nothing, not even a right to see the photograph that condemned your roof. The code does not care about your feelings, and in most of the country, it does not care about your roof photos either.

What the industry says in its defense

To be fair, the strongest case for the technology is not hard to make, so let us make it properly, because dismissing the technology entirely would be as lazy as accepting it uncritically, and the honest version of the industry's case deserves a full hearing before we discuss what should constrain it. Property and casualty claim fraud runs about $32 billion a year, roughly 10 percent of losses, per PwC research. After a major storm, adjusters cannot reach every property, and sending humans onto damaged roofs is dangerous, which is why the catastrophe-response case for drones is the strongest part of the industry's argument and the part critics should concede first. Two human adjusters can disagree by 30 percent or more on the same roof, while the AI is at least consistent, a point worth taking seriously before concluding that the old regime of ladder-climbing humans was some golden age of fairness. Slowing or banning aerial assessment would mean slower payouts after hurricanes, higher premiums for everyone, and more fraud baked into the rates that honest homeowners pay.

That argument is real, but it is not an argument against due process, and nothing about fraud prevention requires keeping the evidence secret from the person being judged by it. This dispute is not technology versus tradition. It is transparency versus a black box.

The math of being wrong

Take the Galveston case as a unit of harm: a $20,000 replacement demanded where a cleaning, call it $500 to $800, would have sufficed, a ratio that turns one model's bad afternoon into a five-figure bill for a family that did nothing wrong. That is a 25-to-40-times multiplier on one household's bill, imposed by an unappealable image.

Now scale it, cautiously. Verisk says 38 percent of roughly 144 million U.S. homes are rated moderate-to-poor by the same class of aerial analytics now driving underwriting. Suppose just 1 percent of those flagged homes face a contested adverse action in a given year, and 10 percent of the contesters end up paying for unnecessary work averaging $8,000, a partial re-roof rather than a full one. Run the arithmetic: 144 million × 0.38 × 0.01 × 0.10 × $8,000, which lands at roughly $437 million a year in insurer-error-driven spending by homeowners.

This is a Fermi estimate, not a finding. Every assumption is labeled and the uncertainty is wide; the true figure could be a tenth of this or ten times it. What the calculation reveals is the asymmetry: the carrier's 20 percent savings in loss-adjustment expense is real money, and when the model misfires, that money comes out of a homeowner's pocket with no receipt and no refund process. The carrier's advertised 20 percent savings in loss-adjustment expense is real money, and when the model misfires, that money quietly migrates from the homeowner's pocket to the carrier's efficiency metrics, with no receipt and no refund process.

What to do when the letter arrives

If you build, buy, or simply own a home, the practical advice is specific and cheap, which is more than can be said for discovering your rights only after the non-renewal letter arrives, when the roof is already photographed, the model has already scored you, and the clock on your policy is already running.

First, ask for the images in writing. Even in states with no disclosure law, request the aerial photographs and the date they were taken. Carriers sometimes comply voluntarily, a written request creates a paper trail that matters if you later complain to your state's department of insurance, and the capture date alone can reveal whether the image predates repairs you already made.

Second, get your own documentation before the surfaces change. A local roofer's ground-level inspection with dated photographs is the counter-evidence that wins disputes, because in the Galveston case the roofer's report was the entire defense. If you can afford it, an independent drone inspection with high-resolution stills of the disputed areas costs a few hundred dollars and speaks the insurer's own language back to it.

Third, check your state's rules before you accept the decision. If you are in California, Massachusetts, Connecticut, or Pennsylvania, you may have rights the letter does not mention. File a complaint with your department of insurance; California's commissioner has said publicly that his department investigates exactly these cases.

Fourth, watch the calendar. If your state follows California's proposed 180-day freshness rule, an adverse decision based on stale imagery is challengeable on its face, so ask for the capture date. Old photos of a roof you have since repaired are not evidence of anything.

None of this should be necessary. A system that photographs your home from the sky and uses the photos to cancel your policy should, at minimum, show you the photograph, and the fact that this modest proposition is a contested legislative battle in 2026 rather than settled consumer law tells you everything about who the current system was designed to serve.

Limitations

What this article did not establish: the actual false-positive rate of aerial underwriting models, which no vendor or carrier publishes, so the error modes described here rest on advocacy-group reports and individual cases rather than a measured rate. The $437 million figure is an illustrative Fermi calculation with wide uncertainty bands, not an empirical finding. The cited bills (AB 75, AB 1559, SB 260, and the Massachusetts proposal) were in the legislative process as of the source dates; their enacted status was not independently verified for this article. Verisk's figures are drawn from trade-press coverage of the 2026 U.S. Roof Report rather than the primary report itself.
