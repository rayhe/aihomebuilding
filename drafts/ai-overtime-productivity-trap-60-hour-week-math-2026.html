Your Crew Worked 60-Hour Weeks to Save the Schedule. By Week Nine They Were Building Slower Than a 40-Hour Crew.

By Frank DeLuca · October 6, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Every GC I know has had the same meeting. Framing is two weeks behind, the roofer is booked, the homeowner is calling twice a day, and somebody says it: we go to six tens until we catch up. Sixty hours a week, Saturdays included, maybe a Sunday if the inspector cooperates.

It sounds like arithmetic: twenty extra hours is twenty extra hours. You pay the premium, you buy back the schedule, the homeowner stops calling.

It is not arithmetic. It is physiology, and somebody measured it forty-six years ago, and nobody in residential construction read the report.

The Report Nobody Read

In November 1980, the Business Roundtable's Construction Industry Cost Effectiveness task force published Report C-2, "Scheduled Overtime Effect on Construction Projects." They tracked actual work hours against fixed production standards across industrial projects, the kind of unglamorous measurement work nobody funds anymore, and they published the curves.

One finding belongs framed in every trailer office in America: where a 60-hour week runs longer than about two months, the cumulative productivity decline pushes the completion date beyond what the same crew would have achieved on a 40-hour week. Read that again. Not "diminishing returns." Beyond. Later. You pay double to finish behind.

Here is their week-by-week table for a 60-hour schedule, the one I want every superintendent to tape inside the job box:

Watch the fourth column decay. In the first two weeks, your sixty hours buy fourteen extra hours of real output. By weeks six to eight, they buy two and a half. By weeks eight to ten, they buy less than nothing: 39.6 effective hours from a 60-hour week, against 40 effective hours from a normal week, while you are paying for 70 to 80 hours of wages depending on your overtime premium.

Engineers phrase it with their usual delicacy: "the reduced productivity of workers for a week's work is equal to or greater than the number of overtime hours worked." Translation: past a certain point, the overtime hours do not merely get expensive.

They evaporate.

The Ledger

Numbers this old deserve a fresh audit, so I ran one. Take a five-person framing crew at $50 an hour fully loaded. Two scenarios, four weeks, starting with scenario A: four weeks of forty, which means eight hundred payroll hours, eight hundred effective hours, forty thousand dollars, fifty bucks per effective hour.

Clean.

Scenario B: four weeks of sixty, overtime at time and a half. Each man-week costs 70 pay-hours (40 straight plus 20 at 1.5x), so the crew burns 1,400 pay-hours: $70,000. Output, using the CICE weekly rates: two weeks at 54.0 effective hours per man plus two weeks at 51.6, times five men, equals 1,056 effective hours.

$117 per recovered hour The crash schedule buys 256 extra effective hours for $30,000. Each recovered hour costs $117 against a $50 normal rate. And that is the good case.

Weeks one through four, before the decay in the table above really bites.

Now compare the crash against the honest alternative. Five weeks of forty delivers 1,000 effective hours for $50,000. Four weeks of sixty delivers 1,056 for $70,000. Fifty-six additional hours of output for twenty thousand additional dollars.

That is $357 per marginal hour, seven times the normal rate, for less than a day and a half of extra production.

I have sat in the meeting where the Saturday push gets ordered, and I have never once heard anybody do this math out loud, because that conversation is always about the schedule, never about the unit cost of the recovered hour, which is the only number that tells you whether the push is a rescue or a donation, and so the most expensive decision on the job gets made without its most important input ever hitting the whiteboard.

The Injury Adder

The ledger above counts only wages, not bodies.

Dembe and colleagues analyzed 110,236 job records in Occupational and Environmental Medicine and found workers on overtime schedules were 61% more likely to sustain a work-related injury or illness than workers who were not, with sixty-plus-hour weeks carrying a 23% increased risk all by themselves. NIOSH and OSHA's joint fatigue summary puts twelve-hour days at a 37% increased injury risk, with evening shifts 18% worse and nights 30% worse than days.

One OSHA recordable on a residential job runs five figures in direct costs before you count the EMR hit, which follows your workers' comp mod for three years and quietly taxes every bid you write in that window. Tired crews also make the mistakes that become callbacks: the mis-set window flashing, the hurried waterproofing detail, the drywall screw pattern an inspector flags on the next phase. None of that shows up in the overtime line item. All of it shows up in the year-end margin.

Meanwhile the habit is growing: CPWR's September 2023 data bulletin found the share of construction workers pulling overtime rose 47.3% between 2011 and 2019, against 13.6% across all industries. Construction is leaning harder on a tool the data says is breaking, faster than the rest of the economy.

Your Scheduling Software Never Got the Memo

Open any mainstream construction schedule, the Gantt in Procore or Buildertrend or MS Project, and add ten overtime hours to a task, and it extends the output proportionally: sixty hours in, sixty hours out, minus nothing. Labor is modeled as a linear input, like concrete: pour more, get more.

Concrete does not get tired, but crews do, on a curve measured in 1980 and never refuted.

The serious schedule-simulation tools know this, starting with ALICE Technologies, the generative scheduling platform now allied with McKinsey on large capital projects, lets planners model overtime, weekend, and shift calendars as scenarios rather than assuming linearity. In one utility case study, ALICE modeled 10%, 20%, and 30% productivity loss on a zero-float scope and found the schedule "doesn't degrade in a straight line, it accelerates," with a cliff between 10% and 20% loss where the impact jumped from days to 195 extra calendar days. That is the CICE curve wearing modern clothes.

But ALICE sells to transmission-line owners and data center developers. I can find no residential scheduling product, not one, that ships a fatigue-adjusted labor model. A GC running a $1.2M custom home gets software that assumes his framers are machines, while the science saying otherwise sits in a free PDF older than his project manager.

That is the actual AI opportunity here, and it is unglamorous: not a robot that frames walls, but a scheduling engine honest enough to tell you that week seven of the push buys 2.6 hours at 37 extra pay-hours, and to suggest the second shift instead. The 1980 report endorsed exactly that remedy, along with alternating crews and periodic full shutdowns. One of its case studies showed a single Sunday shutdown lifting a 70-hour-week crew's performance from 0.86 to 0.99, which means a day off outperformed a day on. Software that tells a superintendent that, before he orders the Saturday, is worth more than the software that renders the Gantt in 3D.

When the Push Is Still Right

Fairness requires the other side, stated at full strength, because overtime is not always a mistake and the old report never claimed it was.

First, the data is industrial and old: pipe fabrication on power plants is not stick-framing a colonial, and nobody has rerun this study on residential crews, who are smaller, self-pacing, and working a more varied task mix. Extrapolating 1980 refinery curves to a five-man framing crew is an inference, and I have labeled it as one everywhere above.

Second, short bursts work, because the report's own curve shows productivity recovering by the end of week one after the initial drop. A two- or three-week push to beat a weather window or hit a crane date is not the trap. The trap is the push that becomes the plan, the "temporary" six-tens still running in month three because nobody scheduled the stand-down.

Third, sometimes overtime is the least-bad option on the table. Liquidated damages, a tower crane on monthly rental, a foundation pour that cannot wait for Monday: the report says so explicitly, and any foreman who has watched a slab cure through a rainstorm knows the feeling. Math informs the decision; it does not make it.

Fourth, the crew often wants the hours: overtime pay funds truck payments and tuition, and in a market where nearly half again as many construction workers are on overtime as a decade ago, taking hours away can cost you the crew itself. A policy that ignores what the paycheck means to the people earning it will fail on contact with the parking lot.

And the second-shift alternative has its own price. The MCAA's productivity-loss tables, the ones contractors use in court to price delay claims, assign supervision dilution its own 10 to 25% penalty. Splitting the crew across shifts means splitting the foreman's attention, and the foreman's attention is the scarcest resource on any job I have ever run.

What I Could Not Prove

The honest accounting. This analysis applies industrial productivity curves to residential crews; no residential-specific decay dataset exists, and the heavy-work numbers in the CICE report may overstate the decline for lighter tasks. The Dembe injury figures are all-industry, not construction-only. The $50 loaded rate is an assumption, stated plainly; the ratios are the point, not the dollars. ALICE's performance claims are vendor-published with no independent audit I could find. And the CICE premium math in the original tables assumes double-time; most residential overtime is time and a half, so I used the report's 1.5x figure (an 80% unit-cost increase at eight weeks) wherever the premium matters.

The Saturday-Push Checklist

1. Price the recovered hour before you order the push. Take your crew size, your loaded rate, and the CICE weekly table above. If the marginal hour costs more than 2x normal, you are not rescuing the schedule; you are donating to it. At 7x, which is where the four-week crash lands against the five-week honest schedule, the answer is no.

2. Put an expiration date on every overtime order. Write it on the whiteboard: "Six tens through Friday the 14th, then stand-down." The data says weeks one through three are defensible and week eight is destructive. A push without a sunset clause becomes the plan, and the plan is what the table punishes.

3. Steal the report's remedies instead: a second shift, alternating crews, or one full Sunday shutdown beat grinding the same crew into the 0.66 productivity band. The 1980 authors measured a Sunday shutdown lifting performance from 0.86 to 0.99. Rest is a schedule acceleration technique. Act like it.

4. Ask your software the rude question next time a vendor demos schedule recovery: ask what productivity decay curve it applies to overtime hours. If the answer is none, if ten extra hours always equal ten extra hours of output, you are looking at a drawing tool, not a planning tool. Buy accordingly.

5. Homeowners: read the delay claim with this table in hand. When your builder says the crew is working Saturdays to catch up, ask how many weeks the push has been running. Weeks one through three, you are getting your money's worth. Past week six, you are funding fatigue. It is a fair question, and now you have the numbers to ask it.

Twenty years of watching projects go sideways has taught me that the most expensive words on a job site are "just until we catch up." Catch-up has a half-life. It is printed in a table from 1980, it has never been refuted, and it says the hours stop counting around week eight.

Plan like it.
