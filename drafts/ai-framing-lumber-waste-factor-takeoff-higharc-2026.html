Your Framing Bid Pads 15% for Waste. An AI Just Read the Plans in 90 Seconds.

By Frank DeLuca, September 15, 2026, Project Management & Operations

It was a late-summer Friday, the kind when every framing crew in the county is racing to finish walls before the weekend, and I was walking a lot in Tracy with a builder I will not name. We stopped at the dumpster. A 30-yard box, half full of dimensional lumber. Not sawdust. Not splinters. Full studs, cripples with two cuts on them, a header eight inches too long, whole lengths of 2x4 that had been ordered, delivered, carried upstairs, cut once, and thrown away. He saw me looking and shrugged. "Lumber management," he said.

I have been doing this for twenty years. I know what the waste factor is supposed to cover. I also know what it covers in practice, and those two lists do not match.

The line nobody reads

Every framing bid you will ever sign has a waste factor baked into the lumber takeoff. According to Andrew Shipp, a building performance specialist with the IBACOS PERFORM Builder Solutions team, the industry average is 15 percent (ProBuilder). Fifteen percent of the framing lumber on your job is ordered with the quiet understanding that it will never become part of your house. It is the one line item in construction that everyone accepts the way they accept gravity, and it deserves more scrutiny than it gets.

On a framing lumber package in the $9,000 to $10,000 range, where a typical 2,000-square-foot home lands at recent price levels (about 16,000 board feet at roughly $400 per thousand, the 2024 Random Lengths composite), the waste factor alone accounts for $1,350 to $1,500 of the check. A line item bigger than most change orders, negotiated less than any of them.

Now ask where the lumber actually goes, because the research is more interesting than the bid sheets. A USDA Forest Service study with Virginia Tech researchers went to a new housing development and weighed the wood waste from actual homes. A 2,000-square-foot house produced 1,500 to 3,700 pounds of solid-sawn wood waste and 1,000 to 1,800 pounds of engineered wood waste, with spruce offcuts running 0.58 to 1.98 pounds per square foot of living space (Araman, Hindman, and Winn). That is a lot of lumber going nowhere. But notice the spread. Identical builder, same development, different framing subcontractors, and the waste per square foot more than tripled between the best crew and the worst. It is not a materials constant. It is a labor-management variable that your contract treats as physics.

Five percent, measured at the dumpster

The EPA's methodology for estimating construction and demolition debris puts the share of wood products discarded during construction at 5 percent (EPA, 2014). I should note that this figure leans on studies from 2004 and 1991, which is not nothing in a world where framing has not fundamentally changed, but it is dated, and the number measures only what is discarded, not what is ordered. Keep both caveats in mind, because the gap between the numbers is the entire story.

Five percent gets thrown away. Fifteen percent gets ordered. Those ten points are where the money hides: over-ordering to cover sloppy takeoffs, lumber damaged by sitting uncovered in the rain, material that "disappears in the back of a pickup truck," which is IBACOS's delicate phrase, and lengths that were never the right lengths in the first place. Shipp gives a tidy example. Ten rafters, each seven feet long. Order 8-footers and you manufacture ten feet of one-foot scraps. Order five 14-footers and cut them in half, and you manufacture nothing. The difference is not a better saw. It is a better list.

Much of the waste problem has nothing to do with technology and everything to do with paperwork. Estimators pad the take-off because the superintendent will not confirm quantities with the framer, the estimator knows this, and the cycle repeats. One builder I worked with shipped ten extra sheets of OSB per house as a matter of habit. Some came back to the supplier. Most became expensive landfill archaeology.

The machine that counts every stud

Which is why the takeoff itself is finally being automated, and the timing is not subtle. On June 30, 2026, Higharc, a cloud-based building-information company aimed at American timber-frame homebuilding, announced a $95 million Series C led by Insight Partners, taking its total funding to roughly $175 million (AEC Magazine). Alongside the raise came a partnership with US LBM, the largest privately held building materials distributor in the country, and a product called AutoTranslate AI. It ingests a flat 2D floor plan and converts it into a 3D spatial model using proprietary vision models plus deterministic construction logic, then produces a material takeoff aligned directly to US LBM's catalog. Every stud, every joist, every header, every drywall sheet, counted from the geometry instead of a man's eyeballs and a scale ruler.

US LBM's chief digital officer, Jonathan Greene, described the traditional takeoff as "laborious and unreliable," which is the polite way of saying it is the source of the over-ordering described above. Higharc's customers report cutting time-to-community-open by 25 to 50 percent and lifting margins by 10 to 15 percent (LBM Journal). The company is not alone. Bobyard launched AI takeoff and estimating in June 2026 with customer-reported results of a 65 percent reduction in takeoff time for flooring, drywall, paint, insulation, and doors and windows (GlobeNewswire). PrebuiltML has been selling purpose-built takeoff software to dealers and framers for years, with cut-logic reports aimed explicitly at minimizing jobsite waste, and its customer list includes Weyerhaeuser and 84 Lumber.

Now do the arithmetic that matters. A precise, model-based takeoff brings the waste factor from 15 percent down to a disciplined 6 to 8 percent. The savings on a $10,000 framing lumber package run roughly $700 to $900 per house. For a production builder closing fifty homes a year, that is $35,000 to $45,000 of margin that used to ride around in the dumpster. Recoverable without changing a single saw.

What the machine does not do

Honesty requires the other half. Higharc's model, for all its spatial awareness, does not yet do roof truss design or plumbing (AEC Magazine review). An AI takeoff covers what it covers, and the truss package, where lumber waste has its own arithmetic, remains outside the frame. A model-based takeoff also cannot fix a crew. Remember the USDA spread: the same plans, the same builder, triple the waste depending on whose hands held the saw. Counting precisely is not the same as cutting precisely, and no software stands in the rain with the cut man on a Friday afternoon.

The accuracy and savings claims are vendor and customer figures, and independent verification of the 10 to 15 percent margin improvements and the 65 percent time reductions is thin. I believe the direction of the claims. I would not bet a project budget on the exact numbers without seeing a builder's own data across ten houses. The EPA's 5 percent discard figure, as noted, leans on research old enough to vote. The point is not that these numbers are exact. They all point the same way, and the way is down.

The Waste Factor Audit

You do not need new software to start capturing this. You need three questions, asked before you sign the framing bid.

First, what waste factor did you apply to the framing lumber line? If the builder cannot quote the number, it is padded. Industry average: 15 percent. A builder running precise takeoffs on repeat plans should be able to justify 8 percent or better. Make them show their work.

Second, where do the takeoff quantities come from? Manual blueprint counts produce the 15 percent world. Model-based takeoffs, whether from a BIM system or an AI estimating product, produce the 8 percent world. Ask for the bill of materials and look at the lumber lengths. An estimator who ordered 14-footers for 7-foot rafters is thinking. An estimator who ordered 8-footers was in a hurry.

Third, who owns the leftover? If offcuts and unused bundles revert to the builder and get credited against your invoice, incentives are aligned. If they go in your dumpster, which you are paying to haul, incentives are not. This is a contract term, and it is negotiable, and almost nobody negotiates it.

That Friday in Tracy, the dumpster held roughly half a unit of lumber. At delivered prices, the foreman's "lumber management" was worth several hundred dollars in material alone, before haul-off and before the labor that cut it. Multiply by the number of houses your builder frames this year, then ask yourself why the waste factor is the one number in the bid that nobody ever asks about.

That machine does not care about your waste factor. It just counts, and counting, it turns out, was the whole problem.
