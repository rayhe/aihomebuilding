Your Furnace Is Suffocating in a 384-Cubic-Foot Closet. The Code Requires 6,000.

By Frank DeLuca · September 26, 2026
← Back to articles

On the second punch-list walk I opened the utility closet and found the furnace wearing a sweater. Cardboard boxes stacked two deep on three sides, a case of paint cans on the shelf above the water heater, and a folding louvered door somebody's cousin hung in 2009 to help the room "breathe."

Twenty years in this business and the closet is the most predictable room in the house, because the cast never changes no matter who owns the place: a furnace, a water heater, and everything the homeowner ran out of garage space for. That week it was the boxes, and next month it would be the paint fumes. The furnace notices neither. It just keeps trying to breathe.

Every gas flame in your house is a controlled fire, and a controlled fire running short of air does not politely go out. It backdrafts, spilling exhaust into the room instead of up the flue, and the exhaust carries carbon monoxide. CO is the leading cause of unintentional poisoning death in the United States: the CDC's surveillance counted roughly 430 non-fire deaths a year, 14,365 hospitalizations, and more than 100,000 emergency department visits annually across 2005 to 2018. Most victims never smelled a thing, because there is nothing to smell.

Fifty cubic feet per thousand BTU

The code has a number for this, and it is not shy about it. Section 304.5.1 of the International Fuel Gas Code, the standard method for indoor combustion air, requires 50 cubic feet of room volume for every 1,000 BTU per hour of appliance input rating. Add up everything in the space, then measure the room. If the room is smaller than the number, the space is legally confined, and the appliances inside it are not allowed to breathe the room alone.

Run the archetype with me, because this is where it gets uncomfortable. Take a completely ordinary house: an 80,000 BTU furnace and a 40,000 BTU water heater sharing a utility closet eight feet by six with an eight-foot ceiling. Total input: 120,000 BTU an hour, so the required volume is 120 times 50, which is 6,000 cubic feet.

Now the room: eight times six times eight is 384 cubic feet.

Three hundred eighty-four cubic feet against a requirement of six thousand. The closet holds six percent of the air the code says its own appliances need, and it is not a weird closet. It is every closet. Somebody measured a room that fits the equipment and a service guy's shoulders and called it a mechanical room. The code measured the fire.

Two openings, top and bottom, sized by the fire

A confined space is not a dead end: it borrows air from the rest of the house through permanent openings, and the code sizes those openings to the fire the same way. Section 304.5.3.1: two openings, one starting within 12 inches of the top of the enclosure, one within 12 inches of the bottom, because warm air rises and you need the loop. Each opening gets one square inch of free area per 1,000 BTU of input, with a floor of 100 square inches. Our archetype needs 120 square inches of free area per opening, top and bottom alike.

Free area. Not the hole in the wall, the air that actually gets through it. This is where most closets quietly fail, because the code de-rates whatever covers the hole. Section 304.10 says that unless the manufacturer stamps a number on it, wood louvers count as 25 percent free area and metal louvers as 75 percent. The math is suddenly physical: 120 square inches of free area through metal louvers means a 160-square-inch grille, say ten by sixteen inches, at the top and another at the bottom. Through wood louvers it means 480 square inches of grille per opening: a twenty-by-twenty-four-inch wood louver, twice.

And now the door: that folding louvered door from 2009, the one everybody assumes handles all this, typically carries a twelve-by-twelve-inch wood louver: 144 square inches times 25 percent, or 36 square inches of free area. Less than a third of one required opening, and it sits in the middle of the door, nowhere near twelve inches from the ceiling or the floor, so it fails the location rule before it even fails the arithmetic. The door breathes. The code is unimpressed.

The tighter the house, the hungrier the rule

Everything above is the standard method, and it rests on an assumption the code states plainly: the house leaks. Old houses leak enough that indoor air renews itself through cracks and gaps, and the 50-cubic-foot number prices that in. If the infiltration rate is actually known to be below 0.40 air changes per hour, and blower-door-tested new builds land there as a matter of routine, Section 304.5.2 throws out the standard method and requires the known-infiltration-rate calculation instead, which demands more volume as the house gets tighter. Every air-sealing upgrade makes the combustion-air math harder for the gas appliances still sitting in the closet. Tightness is a virtue everywhere except next to an open flame.

What starving looks like on a manometer

So what does a six-percent closet actually do wrong? The Building Performance Institute has a test for it, and it is worth knowing even if you never hire an auditor. Worst-case depressurization: dryer running, every exhaust fan on, interior doors shut, the air handler forced on. Measure the pressure in the combustion appliance zone against the outdoors. Then fire the smallest appliance first and watch the draft hood with smoke or a mirror.

Spillage has to stop within sixty seconds. Draft at the flue has to beat a minimum that scales with outdoor temperature, roughly the outside temperature divided by forty, minus 2.75 pascals. Undiluted CO in the flue gets action levels: up to 25 parts per million, proceed; 26 to 100, fix it soon; 100 to 400, do no other work in the house until it is repaired; over 400, emergency, shut the fuel off. These are not guidelines. They are the pass-fail line for whether a house may be weatherized at all.

Notice what the test punishes: pressure. A closet that passes the volume math on a calm day can still spill when the dryer, the range hood, and the bath fans all pull at once, because the flame is competing with the fans for the same house air, and when the pressure goes negative the manometer settles the argument in pascals. The code's volume number and the auditor's manometer are measuring the same shortage from two directions.

The honest exits

There are three ways out, and they run in order of price. Cheapest: cut the two openings to the calculated size, with real free area, top and bottom, and keep the boxes away from them forever. Mid-range: install a mechanical combustion-air supply, which the code allows at 0.35 cubic feet per minute per 1,000 BTU, interlocked so the burner cannot fire when the fan is off. Our archetype needs 42 CFM, about a good bathroom fan with a relay.

Most expensive and most final: stop feeding the fire from the house. Sealed-combustion and direct-vent appliances take their combustion air straight from outdoors through their own pipe and are exempt from the volume math entirely. And the honest long view is electrification: a heat pump and a heat-pump water heater have no flame, need no combustion air, and make the entire section of the code irrelevant. Every jurisdiction phasing out gas in new construction is, whether it says so or not, deleting this problem instead of solving it.

That is the strongest case against everything above: for a new build, the smartest combustion-air strategy is no combustion. But a hundred million existing homes have gas appliances in closets and are not getting rewired this decade. For those houses, the math still applies, the openings still have to be cut, and the boxes still have to move.

Two things the boxes were hiding

While you are in there, look at what shares the room. Section 303.3 says gas appliances do not belong in storage closets, and a utility room packed wall to wall with boxes is a storage closet with a furnace in it. Section 304.12 says corrosive and flammable fumes do not get to share the combustion air either, which rules out the paint cans, the fertilizer, and the pool chemicals. Vapors that eat burner components and anything that can flash are not decor.

And check the air's address. A county permit worksheet I found, the kind inspectors hand out with the application, states it in plain English: combustion air cannot be drawn from bathrooms, bedrooms, or garages. If the "opening" in your closet wall leads to the garage, you have not solved the problem. You have moved it next to the cars.

An audit that fits on an index card

A camera that can measure a room can run this entire check. Lidar phones and vision models already estimate dimensions from photos; reading a nameplate's BTU rating, classifying a louver as wood or metal, and doing the de-rating arithmetic is deterministic work a machine does without getting bored. Plan-review automation is already swallowing code checks in places like Honolulu and Toronto, and this one is pure table lookup with a tape measure. I have not found a vendor shipping automated combustion-air sizing yet, which is a gap, not a feature.

Until the software exists, the index card works. Frank's ten-minute closet audit:

Frank's ten-minute closet audit
  1. Read the nameplate input rating on every gas appliance in the space and add them up, in BTU per hour.
  2. Measure the room: length times width times height, in feet. If the total BTU divided by 1,000 times 50 is bigger than the room, the space is confined and the openings come next.
  3. Size two permanent openings at one square inch of free area per 1,000 BTU each, minimum 100 square inches, one within 12 inches of the ceiling and one within 12 inches of the floor.
  4. De-rate the grilles: wood louvers count as 25 percent free, metal as 75 percent, unless the manufacturer stamps a real number. Divide the free area by the percentage to get the hole you actually cut.
  5. Move the boxes, the paint, and the chemicals out, and make sure no opening draws air from a bathroom, bedroom, or garage.

What this article did not prove

The worked numbers belong to the archetype, not your house. Real furnaces run 60,000 to 120,000 BTU and real water heaters 30,000 to 50,000, so read your own nameplates. The 304.5.2 known-infiltration-rate equations are described qualitatively here; the code prints them as Equations 3-1 and 3-2 and I did not reprint them, so consult the text if your house tests under 0.40 ACH. I found no survey measuring how many existing closets actually fail the 50-cubic-foot rule, so treat the prevalence claim as reasoned from the math, not counted in the field. The BPI test figures come from field-guide summaries of the Building Analyst Professional standard rather than the standard document itself. And the AI-vision framing is a direction, not a product: no vendor was found shipping automated combustion-air opening sizing today. Local amendments may move any of these numbers; the adopted code and the AHJ have the last word.
