An Algorithm Predicts Whether a Damaged Building Is Safe to Enter. It Was Trained on Concrete Towers. Your House Is Made of Wood.

On the flight into the earthquake zone, a volunteer structural engineer was handed the ATC-20 field manual and told she would need it when they landed, because within hours of touching down she was expected to walk up to cracked and tilting houses and decide whether the families inside should stay or evacuate. This is not an exaggeration and not an edge case.

After the 1994 Northridge earthquake, the Applied Technology Council documented that inspectors "had little or no prior training in using the ATC-20 methodology," noting that some received only the assessment forms and placards before being told to begin tagging buildings green, yellow, or red. One group of Southern California volunteers was handed the ATC-20-1 field manual to read during their flight to the damaged area, stepping off the plane and immediately beginning safety evaluations that would determine whether families could re-enter their homes.

In that earthquake, 113,915 buildings were assessed across the damage zone, and 97,212 of them, fully 85.3 percent of the total, were residential dwellings. Residential homes are not a subcategory of post-disaster assessment in the United States. They are the overwhelming majority of the work.

What a Green Tag Actually Means

The ATC-20 rapid evaluation procedure allocates roughly three minutes per building, during which an inspector walks the exterior, notes visible damage to the foundation line and structural connections, checks for obvious failure indicators like racking or partial collapse, and places one of three colored placards on the front of the structure. Green means "INSPECTED" and apparently safe for continued occupancy, yellow means "RESTRICTED USE" with conditions on which portions of the building can be entered, and red means "UNSAFE" with no entry permitted until a detailed engineering review is completed.

Designed in 1989 with funding from the California Office of Emergency Services, the state health planning office, and FEMA, the procedure was originally written for structural engineers and building inspectors with professional training in construction and design. But experience with the Loma Prieta, Landers, Cape Mendocino, and Northridge earthquakes demonstrated that there were never enough trained professionals available in the immediate aftermath, forcing the system to expand its pool of evaluators far beyond its original design parameters.

That shortage has not improved in the three decades since. After the 2018 Anchorage earthquake, the municipality had inspected only half the buildings on its list for assessment and was forced to hire additional engineers to continue the work, a delay driven partly by budget constraints and partly by the fundamental arithmetic of inspector supply versus building stock demand. In Tokyo, 110,375 certified structural assessment experts are registered to evaluate a building stock of 1.9 million structures, a ratio that would become immediately untenable during a major seismic event in the metropolitan area.

ATC itself has documented four fundamental limitations of the rapid evaluation methodology it created: inspector bias and experience-based variability in judgment calls, lack of physical access to damaged structural locations or hidden members, time-intensive procedures that create bottlenecks at the scale most disaster events demand, and evaluations that are "qualitative in nature and not entirely quantitative or physics-based." Those are not outside criticisms lodged by skeptics; they are the procedure's own authors describing its structural shortcomings as a decision-making framework for life-safety determinations.

The Machine That Knows Better

In parallel with these documented shortcomings, researchers have been building machine learning models that predict post-earthquake structural safety without depending on a three-minute exterior walk-around by a variably-trained volunteer. A framework published in the journal Structural Safety used incremental dynamic analysis with sequential ground motions to evaluate the residual collapse capacity of damaged structures, then trained Random Forest and Classification and Regression Tree models to map observed damage patterns to a binary safety determination of whether the building remained safe to occupy. Using sensor-based response patterns, the model achieved 91 percent prediction accuracy, and using visible damage patterns alone, accuracy dropped only marginally to 88 percent, both figures that substantially exceed the consistency achievable by human inspectors operating under field conditions with minimal training.

After the 2023 Kahramanmaraş earthquake sequence in Turkey, a research team analyzed 16,611 building-level field observations collected from Kırıkhan in Hatay province, testing eight machine learning models against three operationally-defined damage classes: No-Damage, Slight-to-Moderate, and Heavy-to-Collapse. LightGBM achieved the joint-highest balanced accuracy across all damage categories, while CatBoost demonstrated the strongest sensitivity specifically for the Heavy-Collapse classification, which is the determination that matters most when human lives depend on whether the model correctly identifies a structure that has lost its capacity to resist further loading. SHAP analysis of the model identified the dominant predictive features as building age, underlying lithology, number of floors, structural system type, plinth area, and proximity to mapped fault lines and surface ruptures.

Separately, a vision-language framework developed at Dakota State University combined drone footage processed through a Video Restoration Transformer with Gemma3:27b, a 27-billion-parameter visual language model, to classify building damage from aerial and satellite imagery across four severity levels with 84.5 percent accuracy, validated against both the Turkey earthquake damage field and the 2013 Moore tornado in Oklahoma. Notably, the researchers wrote that "the system's accessibility allows non-technical users to perform preliminary analyses," a sentence that reads like a direct answer to the inspector shortage problem that has plagued every major earthquake response since Loma Prieta.

The Training Data Problem

Every one of these models was trained on the wrong building type for the country where they would be most urgently needed.

That 91-percent-accurate framework from Structural Safety was developed using data from the Van Nuys hotel testbed, a seven-story reinforced concrete building instrumented by the California Strong Motion Instrumentation Program, and validated on a four-story reinforced concrete special moment frame structure that shares essentially no structural characteristics with the one-story wood-frame ranch house in San Jose or the two-story Victorian in San Francisco's Sunset District where the overwhelming majority of actual assessment demand would concentrate. Meanwhile, the Turkey study analyzed 16,611 buildings in a country where approximately 85 percent of the building stock is reinforced concrete construction, creating a near-perfect mirror of the training bias that renders these models inapplicable to American residential assessment without fundamental retraining. And the drone and vision-language framework validated its results exclusively against images of damaged and collapsed concrete buildings.

In the United States, the building that accounts for 85 percent of all post-earthquake safety assessments is a one- or two-story wood-frame house, and it fails in ways that share almost nothing with reinforced concrete structural behavior. Cripple wall collapse occurs when the short stud wall between the foundation and the first floor buckles laterally under seismic loading, a failure mode that does not exist in RC construction and produces damage patterns that no existing AI model has learned to recognize. Soft-story collapse at oversized garage openings, foundation anchor bolt shear that allows the entire structure to slide off its foundation, chimney separation from the main framing, unbraced water heater gas line rupture, and plywood sheathing nail withdrawal patterns that indicate critical racking damage while remaining invisible from the street all represent failure mechanisms that no published AI training dataset has captured or modeled.

SHAP features driving the Turkey model's predictions, including building age, lithology, floor count, and structural system classification, were calibrated to predict damage patterns in multistory reinforced concrete apartment blocks on alluvial soil near the East Anatolian Fault, not to evaluate whether a cripple-wall-vulnerable wood-frame bungalow in the San Fernando Valley will survive the next aftershock without lateral collapse.

Why This Gap Persists

Reinforced concrete buildings produce clean, structured data that machine learning models can ingest efficiently because they are routinely instrumented with accelerometer arrays, their dynamic response to ground motion is mathematically well-characterized through decades of finite-element modeling and shake-table testing, and their failure modes follow predictable patterns along established plastic hinge sequences that map neatly onto the classification boundaries that supervised learning algorithms are designed to optimize against.

Wood-frame residential construction is structurally messier in ways that make it simultaneously harder to model and harder to predict at the critical threshold between damaged-but-occupiable and collapse-prone. Structural connections in wood-frame buildings are nailed rather than welded or mechanically bolted, introducing variability in capacity that increases with age, moisture cycling, and the quality of the original carpentry work, none of which is captured in any post-earthquake assessment database. A wood-frame house can lose a substantial portion of its lateral resistance and remain standing because its structural redundancy distributes load across hundreds of nail connections that fail progressively rather than catastrophically, but that same characteristic means the house can present an apparently intact exterior to a three-minute visual inspection while concealing foundation-level damage detectable only through crawlspace access that the rapid assessment protocol does not require or provide time for.

Researchers gravitate toward problems that come with available training data, and instrumented RC buildings have decades of well-curated sensor datasets while the 80,867 wood-frame houses that received a green tag in Northridge generated almost no structured post-assessment data that could retroactively serve as ground truth for machine learning model development. San Francisco's Building Occupancy Resumption Program allows commercial building owners to pre-certify private post-earthquake inspectors and effectively bypass the overwhelmed public assessment queue, but BORP is available only to commercial properties, leaving residential homeowners without an equivalent mechanism for faster or more rigorous evaluation.

What an Honest Model Would Require

Building a wood-frame residential post-disaster assessment model is not an unsolvable engineering problem but rather an unfunded one where the necessary input features are identifiable from existing building science and the data sources, while currently unstructured, demonstrably exist in scattered institutional archives. Critical features for a residential triage model map directly to known vulnerability factors: construction era determines likely structural system and applicable code generation (pre-1950 unreinforced, 1950-through-1975 conventional framing, post-1975 engineered shear walls with holdowns and tie straps), foundation type establishes vulnerability class across the three dominant residential categories (cripple wall, continuous stem wall, slab-on-grade), retrofit status records whether post-installation anchor bolts connect the mudsill to the foundation concrete, garage configuration flags soft-story risk based on opening width relative to wall length, and soil classification data from county geotechnical surveys provides the site amplification factor that can triple ground acceleration on soft deposits compared to nearby bedrock sites.

Satellite and drone imagery can detect roof damage patterns, chimney displacement, and differential foundation settlement from the air, while street-level photographs from pre-positioned cameras or rapid post-event surveys can capture visible wall racking, stucco cracking signatures, and the foundation-to-wall separation that indicates lateral structural movement under seismic loading. A well-designed triage model could process the 85 percent of assessments that are residential and flag probable red-tag candidates for immediate detailed engineering inspection, provisionally clearing homes whose exterior damage signatures are consistent with structural coherence and freeing trained professionals to concentrate their limited time on the buildings that present genuine collapse risk.

What This Means for Your Home

If you own a wood-frame house in a seismic or hurricane-prone region, the post-disaster assessment system that applies to your property has three characteristics worth understanding clearly. First, the procedure designed to determine whether your home is structurally safe after a major event relies on a three-minute exterior visual inspection conducted by someone whose qualifications can range from decades of licensed structural engineering practice to having received a photocopied field manual on an airplane bound for the disaster zone. Second, the AI models that could supplement or eventually replace that inspection have been validated exclusively on reinforced concrete structures whose failure mechanics share essentially nothing with the stud-nailed, plywood-sheathed, cripple-wall-vulnerable construction that defines the vast majority of American single-family housing stock. And no funded research program has announced development of a wood-frame residential assessment model, despite the documented fact that residential dwellings account for 85 percent of all post-earthquake building evaluations conducted in the United States.

Before a disaster strikes, the highest-value preparedness step remains a seismic retrofit: bolting the mudsill to the foundation with code-compliant anchor bolts, bracing cripple walls with structural plywood and framing connectors, strapping the water heater to wall framing, and addressing any soft-story garage condition with an engineered steel moment frame or equivalent lateral bracing system. After an event, the most useful thing a homeowner can do is systematically document the home's damage state before any inspector arrives, photographing the foundation perimeter from multiple angles at ground level, checking exterior walls for plumb using a level or a weighted string, examining every chimney-to-structure connection for visible separation or cracking, and accessing the crawlspace to inspect the foundation-to-framing connection if conditions permit safe entry.

An AI assessment model for wood-frame residential construction will eventually be built, because the research trajectory points unmistakably in that direction and the inspector shortage becomes more visible and less defensible after every significant event that overwhelms the ATC-20 volunteer pipeline. When that model finally arrives, it will need structured training data that currently sits fragmented across insurance claim adjustment files, contractor repair invoices, and municipal building department permit archives that no institution has digitized, linked to parcel data, or made available to the research community for model development.

Until then, your house gets a colored placard from someone who may have learned the assessment methodology somewhere over Nevada.

Limitations: The 91 percent accuracy figure from Structural Safety applies to a single four-story RC building type under specific analytical conditions with sensor-instrumented response data and cannot be directly extrapolated to residential wood-frame construction without a dedicated training and validation effort on that building class. Northridge assessment statistics date to 1994; more recent US earthquakes including Ridgecrest 2019 and Anchorage 2018 have produced fewer comprehensive publicly-available datasets with comparable residential assessment granularity. We could not independently verify the ratio of formally-trained versus minimally-trained inspectors deployed to any specific earthquake event, as deployment records are not consistently published. BORP comparison is jurisdictionally specific to San Francisco's Building Occupancy Resumption Program and does not necessarily reflect post-earthquake inspection frameworks, timelines, or access provisions in other municipalities or states.
