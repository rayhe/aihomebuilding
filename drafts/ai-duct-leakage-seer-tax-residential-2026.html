You Paid for 16 SEER. Your Ducts Are Delivering 11.

By Jake Kowalski · September 19, 2026

Crawl into an attic in July and you'll learn something the glossy heat pump brochures never mention. Up here, above the insulation, the air is 140 degrees and your shiny 16 SEER system is wheezing through ductwork that looks like it was installed by someone who lost a bet, with every joint having expanded, contracted, and worked itself loose through years of thermal cycling that no tape or mastic was ever going to survive. Every seam is a tax, and every disconnected joint is a vent blowing conditioned air straight into the rafters. You paid for laboratory efficiency; you're getting attic efficiency.

Federal numbers make the embarrassment official: ENERGY STAR says a typical house loses 20 to 30 percent of the air moving through its duct system to leaks, holes, and poor connections, with the losses climbing when ducts sit in attics, crawlspaces, and garages. A Department of Energy report puts it blunter: most duct systems lose 25 to 40 percent of the energy they carry, while the EPA has stated that as much as 30 percent of energy is lost to duct leakage. Max Sherman of Lawrence Berkeley Lab said the quiet part out loud years ago: it does not make sense to hook a high-efficiency system to a leaky, inefficient duct system.

Think about what that does to the equipment premium sitting on your invoice, because trade publications citing EPA research put the derate in brutal terms: a 13 SEER unit connected to leaky ducts performs like a 7 to 9 SEER system, since the air never arrives to do the job it was rated for. Scale that to a modern 16 SEER2 install and the back-of-the-envelope math lands you near an 11 or 12. On a $14,000 system, roughly a quarter of the efficiency you financed is venting into unconditioned space every time the blower kicks on.

New houses get a pressure test. Yours probably never did.

Here is the part that should make builders squirm a little. The International Energy Conservation Code already requires duct testing on new construction, with Section R403.3.3 making a pressure test mandatory either at rough-in or after construction, measured at 25 pascals across the whole system, and the written report signed by the tester and handed to the building official. IECC caps the prescriptive number at 4 cubic feet per minute of leakage per 100 square feet of conditioned floor area, so your new house shipped with a leakage number on a form.

Existing houses have no number at all, because nothing in the code reaches back to the 140 million homes already standing, and most duct systems in America have never been pressure tested, not once, in decades of service, while the raters and officials who enforce the rule on new construction never set foot in the houses where the real losses live. A duct blaster test for an existing home runs $300 to $500, which makes it the cheapest diagnostic in all of home performance and also the one almost nobody buys. The market tests the box. It ignores the pipe.

Machines that find it, machines that fix it

Finding leaks used to mean crawling the attic with a flashlight and a prayer, and that part is changing fast. A Florida HVAC firm started pairing AI-assisted diagnostics with technician assessments in May 2026, analyzing humidity, particulates, airflow patterns, and thermal consistency against ASHRAE 62.2 ventilation benchmarks to flag duct leakage and equipment strain that routine service calls miss, which at least gives the crawl a game plan before anybody climbs in. Camera-equipped duct robots now crawl the runs themselves, spotting mold, dust buildup, and gaps, with one inspection robot finding a fully uncoupled duct section that sealant alone would never have fixed, since aerosol sealants only bridge gaps up to about 15 millimeters. Thermal cameras do the triage in minutes, revealing spot leaks at joints and insulation loss that a visual check would walk right past, per FLIR's building diagnostics guidance.

Be clear-eyed about what the robots are for, because none of them replaces the duct blaster. The pressure test is the measurement and everything else is targeting. A camera tells you where to look, and only a number tells you whether it matters.

On the repair side, the headline technology is aerosol sealing, which injects a polymer mist into the ducts to seal leaks from the inside, computer-controlled, with no drywall surgery. Typical residential jobs run $1,300 to $3,000, take three to six hours, and include pre and post leakage tests with a printed report, with providers claiming the process seals up to 95 percent of duct leakage. The old-school alternative, hand-applied mastic or foil tape on every accessible joint, costs $500 to $1,500 but only reaches the runs a human can physically touch, which on most houses is a minority of the system. Providers claim 15 to 20 percent reductions in heating and cooling bills from aerosol sealing.

One dead credit to know about before anyone quotes it: duct sealing used to qualify for the federal 25C energy efficient home improvement credit at 30 percent, until the One Big Beautiful Bill ended 25C for property placed in service after December 31, 2025, per the IRS FAQ on the law, so any contractor pitching federal credits for 2026 work is working from a stale playbook.

Run the test before you buy the fix

Here is the decision tree I would hand to any homeowner with uneven rooms and a creeping bill. Get the duct blaster test first, because everything downstream depends on one number. Leakage under about 10 percent means you leave the ducts alone and look at the envelope instead. Between 10 and 20 percent, hand-seal the accessible runs with mastic and retest, since a weekend of ugly attic work buys most of the gain at a fraction of the price. Above 20 percent, or with ducts baking in a hot attic, get quotes for aerosol sealing and demand the pre and post numbers in writing before the final invoice. When the inspection finds disconnected sections, filthy ducts, or collapsed flex, repair and clean before any sealant enters the picture, because polymer mist does not bridge a six-inch gap and it does not stick to dust.

One more piece of math, because this is where the SEER rating dies quietly. Take that $14,000, 16 SEER2 system and assume 25 percent distribution loss, the midpoint of the federal estimates. Roughly $3,500 of what you paid bought efficiency that never reaches a room, and the $400 test that quantifies it costs less than the price difference between one SEER tier and the next, which makes ductwork the test drive nobody takes.

What the skeptic in me has to say

Not every leaky duct is a tragedy. Ducts inside the thermal envelope leak mostly back into the house, so a 15 percent leak inside the conditioned space wastes far less energy than a 10 percent leak in a vented attic. Location beats percentage, so ask where the ducts are before asking how leaky they are.

Sealing also cannot fix design: an oversized furnace pushing through undersized ducts is a capacity problem, not a leak problem, and no amount of sealant changes the physics of a 14-inch trunk feeding a house that needs 16. When the system short-cycles and the far bedroom stays cold, you have a Manual D problem wearing a leakage costume, so fix the sizing first.

Treat the savings claims with a contractor's skepticism. The 15 to 20 percent bill-reduction figures come from the companies selling the service, not from independent randomized trials. Federal loss estimates are national averages across aging housing stock, and a 2020 house that passed its R403.3 test will not have 30 percent to recover. Your test tells you your number; vendor averages are not your number.

What I could not prove

Straight accounting: the 13 SEER to 7-to-9 SEER downgrade comes from a trade publication citing EPA research, and I could not pull the underlying study to verify the test conditions. The aerosol sealing savings and 95 percent sealing claims are provider-reported, with no public third-party fleet audit to back them. I did not pin down average household heating and cooling spending because it varies three to fourfold by climate, so I stated savings as percentages of the HVAC bill rather than promising a payback date. When a quote sheet promises payback in a specific number of months without knowing your climate zone, your utility rates, your duct location, and your measured leakage number, understand what you are reading, because that document is selling, not engineering.

What to do with this

If your house has hot and cold rooms, a system that runs constantly, or ducts in an attic or crawlspace, stop shopping for a new unit and start with a $300 to $500 duct blaster test. Get the leakage number as a percentage, because the whole decision tree hangs on it. Under 10, walk away. Ten to 20, mastic the accessible joints yourself or pay someone a few hundred to do it. Over 20, or attic ducts that bake all summer, price aerosol sealing at $1,300 to $3,000 and require the post-seal test report before you pay the final invoice.

Builders have the cheaper play, since the code already forces the test on new construction. Design the ducts inside the thermal envelope and the 4 CFM25 per 100 square feet limit becomes easy to beat. Cheapest leak of all: the one that never gets built.

Finally, if a contractor quotes sealing without mentioning a test, without a cleaning plan for dirty ducts, or with a federal tax credit for 2026 work, you are not getting a diagnosis; you are getting a pitch. Good shops lead with the blaster, show you the number, and let the number sell the fix. Make them earn it the same way the code makes the new guys earn it: with a report, signed, handed to you.
