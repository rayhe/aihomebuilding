Your Florida Dream Home Sits on Swiss Cheese. A Free AI Map Shows Where the Holes Are.

By Frank DeLuca · September 20, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

The house in Seffner looked fine on closing day. Three bedrooms, fenced yard, twenty minutes from Tampa. In February 2013 the ground opened under one of those bedrooms in the middle of the night and took the man sleeping in it. The county condemned the house, then condemned the one next door. That is the part of the sinkhole story nobody puts in the listing photos. The ground does not send a warning letter.

Florida sits on limestone, and limestone dissolves. Rainwater turns faintly acidic falling through air and soil, and over thousands of years it eats the rock into voids. When the sand and clay above a void can no longer bridge it, the ground drops. The sudden kind, cover-collapse, is the one that makes the news. The slow kind, cover-subsidence, sags the yard over years and cracks the slab long before any camera crew arrives. Both live in the same counties. Both are now mapped by machines, in public, and almost nobody buying a house has heard of the maps.

Seven numbers that predict a hole

A dissertation out of the University of Central Florida spent years on this problem and came back with something practical. Researcher Yong Je Kim built probabilistic sinkhole hazard models for east central Florida using three methods: frequency ratio, logistic regression, and an artificial neural network. The models take seven hydrogeological factors as inputs. Translated out of geologist: how much the water pressure differs between the shallow and deep aquifers, how fast groundwater recharges, how permeable the soil is, how thick the overburden is, how thick the two aquifer layers are, and how close the site sits to known karst features. Feed in those seven numbers for any patch of ground and the model returns a probability, location and size both, rendered as a regional map in five classes from very low to very high.

A separate study trained a C5.0 decision tree on 1,113 documented sinkholes in the same region, using twelve contributing factors, and hit 83.52 percent prediction accuracy measured by area under the ROC curve. A 2025 paper added explainability on top, using SHAP analysis with random forest and gradient boosting models; the random forest reached 95.5 percent accuracy with 98.8 percent AUC, and the SHAP values named the strongest predictors outright instead of leaving them inside a black box. That last study worked a basin in Turkiye, not Florida, so treat its numbers as a methods demonstration, not a Florida result. The Florida work stands on its own.

One finding from the Florida research deserves a buyer's full attention. The models show the Orlando area carries a higher probability of larger sinkholes than the Ocala area, and the reason is mechanical: thick overburden layers bridge bigger voids before they fail, so when they fail, they fail bigger. Thin soil gives you frequent small sags. Thick soil gives you rare catastrophes. Your risk is not one number. It is a size distribution, and the tail of that distribution is what eats houses.

The 48-hour triage nobody runs

Here is the part that bothers me. The inventory behind these models comes from the Florida Geological Survey's Subsidence Incident Reports, which are public. The susceptibility maps are published in open proceedings. The seven factors are all things a geotechnical engineer measures with a drill rig and a piezometer. Everything a buyer needs to screen a lot exists, costs nothing to look at, and takes about two days to assemble. Almost nobody does it, because the standard Florida inspection covers the roof, the panel, the AC, and the termites, and the ground gets a pass until it does not.

Run the math the way a foreman would. A residential geotechnical investigation with standard penetration borings typically runs $1,500 to $3,000 depending on the market and the number of borings. Remediation of an actual sinkhole averages $10,000 to $15,000 in Florida, and the Citizens bid pricing shows why: compaction grout mobilization alone is $3,000 a project, underpinning runs $1,476 per 24-foot pin plus $2,500 mobilization, and a real stabilization eats a dozen pins before lunch. The boring costs one-tenth to one-fifth of the repair and it happens before you own the problem. That is the cheapest insurance in the entire transaction, and it is not insurance at all. It is information.

The decision rule writes itself. Pull the FGS subsidence incident reports for the parcel's section and check the published susceptibility class for the area. Very low or low: proceed with the standard inspection. Moderate or above: make SPT borings an inspection contingency, same as the roof certification, and get the engineer's letter before the contingency clock runs out. High or very high: borings plus a written foundation recommendation, and price the grouting into your offer or walk. Nobody has published this as a homebuyer rule, which is strange, because every commercial developer in central Florida already runs a version of it.

What the models cannot do

Now the honest half. These are regional models. They classify zones, not lots, and they predict susceptibility, not timing. A very-high zone can sit quiet for thirty years while a moderate zone drops a driveway next spring, because the trigger is often a local event the model never sees: a broken water main saturating the soil, a new well drawing down the aquifer, a season of heavy rain after a drought. The FGS inventory is report-based, which means it undercounts the events nobody bothered to report, and undercounted training data biases every model built on it. Treat the map as a screening tool, not a verdict.

There is also the fraud history, and it matters. Florida's sinkhole claim volume exploded in the Tampa Bay area in the 2000s, much of it real, some of it not, and the legislature responded in 2011 by tightening the claims process and in 2012 Citizens pulled sinkhole coverage out of the base policy entirely. That history is why your insurer will want an inspection before selling you the coverage, and why you should want the borings before you need the coverage.

The insurance fine print

Since 2012, Florida homeowners policies include catastrophic ground cover collapse in the standard policy at no extra charge, and that is not the same thing as sinkhole coverage. Sinkhole loss coverage, the kind that pays for cracked slabs and grouting, is optional, separately rated, and sits outside the rate glidepath. Citizens, the state insurer of last resort, notes on its own materials that a sinkhole inspection may be required to qualify. Under Florida statute 627.7151, private insurers may offer limited sinkhole policies, Citizens may not issue them at all, and policy limits below $50,000 are prohibited unless the replacement cost is lower. Translation: the coverage exists, it costs extra, it requires proof, and the state will not let you buy a token amount and call it protected.

What this analysis does not prove

Several boundaries, stated plainly. The susceptibility numbers cited here come from peer-reviewed regional models for east central Florida; they do not transfer directly to the Panhandle, south Florida, or any other karst region, and the SHAP study's 98.8 percent AUC was achieved in Turkiye on different geology. The repair and grouting figures are Florida-specific and dated 2026; your market will differ. The $1,500 to $3,000 boring range is a typical residential figure, not a quote, and complex sites with deep borings or difficult access cost more. No model discussed here predicts when a specific lot will collapse, and none replaces a licensed professional engineer's site-specific opinion. The Seffner case is cited for the stakes, not as a data point; it predates these models and proves nothing about their accuracy. Finally, insurance statutes change; verify 627.7151's current text before relying on it at the closing table.

Your checklist

If you are buying in Florida, especially the central corridor from Tampa through Orlando to Ocala, search the FGS subsidence incident reports for your parcel's area before you make the offer. It is free and it takes an evening. If the published susceptibility maps or the incident history put the area at moderate or above, write SPT borings into the inspection contingency and hire the geotech before the clock runs out, not after. Get the engineer's foundation letter in writing, with a recommendation, not a verbal "looks fine."

If you already own the house, watch for the slow kind. Doors that stop latching, cracks that step through the block wall, a low spot forming in the yard that was not there last year: that is cover-subsidence announcing itself, and it is cheaper to grout early than to underpin late. Call the geotech before you call the insurer, because the insurer's engineer works for the insurer.

If your agent says sinkhole coverage is included, ask which kind. Catastrophic ground cover collapse is the free one and it pays only when the ground literally swallows the structure. Sinkhole loss coverage is the optional one that pays for the cracked slab, and since 2012 you have to ask for it, qualify for it, and pay for it separately. The ground under a Florida house is a known condition with known maps. Buyers who read them are not paranoid. They are just reading.
