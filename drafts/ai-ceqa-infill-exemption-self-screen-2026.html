On June 30, 2025, the governor of California held his own state budget hostage. Gavin Newsom told the legislature he would not sign the budget bill unless it also passed two trailer bills gutting the state's most litigated environmental law, then signed all of it the same day. Fourteen months later, most small builders in California still have not heard what changed, which is a shame, because the change might be worth more to an infill developer than any construction technology covered on this site.

Assembly Bill 130 and Senate Bill 131 rewrote the California Environmental Quality Act for housing. Together they created ten new CEQA exemptions, and the centerpiece is a complete exemption from environmental review for qualifying urban infill housing projects, codified at Public Resources Code section 21080.66. Complete means complete: no initial study, no mitigated negative declaration, no environmental impact report, for the project and for the permits and public improvements that come with it. AB 130 passed the Assembly unanimously and the Senate 28 to 5, while SB 131 cleared 50 to 3 and 33 to 1, margins that tell you Sacramento had stopped pretending the old regime could be repaired with another tweak and had decided to replace it instead. Those vote counts tell you how thoroughly Sacramento had decided the old regime was finished. Done.

To understand what finished, consider the numbers. Holland & Knight partner Jennifer Hernandez has now published three studies of every CEQA lawsuit filed in California, covering 2010 through 2021, and all three found the same thing: housing is the most frequent target of CEQA litigation, and the most frequent target within housing is housing approved in existing communities, which in plain English means infill Lawsuits filed in 2020 alone targeted 47,999 housing units, roughly half of the 110,784 homes California built per year on average. Litigation including appeals typically runs four to five years, and Hernandez documents the mechanism that makes the lawsuit itself the weapon: lenders, investors, and grantors will not fund a project while a CEQA suit is pending, so opponents do not need to win, a dynamic the study describes as a low-cost, no-risk strategy for anyone with a filing fee and a grievance. Filing is enough. Winning is optional. Roughly two-thirds of these anti-housing suits allege violations of greenhouse gas and vehicle-miles-traveled targets, which means the law originally written to protect the environment had become, in Hernandez's phrase, a population control statute aimed at the housing California's own climate policy says it needs.

Seven Gates to Freedom

An exemption is not a vibe; it is a checklist, and checklists are automatable, which is where this story gets interesting for readers of this publication. Synthesizing the Association of Bay Area Governments summary, the Holland & Knight analysis, and county counsel briefings, a project qualifies for section 21080.66 only if it clears all seven of these gates.

  1. Project type: it must be a "housing development project" under Government Code section 65589.5(h)(2): residential units including single-family homes, mixed-use with at least two-thirds of square footage residential (or 50 percent when other conditions are met), transitional or supportive housing, or farmworker housing.
  2. Location: your site must sit in an incorporated city, charter cities included, or in an unincorporated area the Census Bureau defines as urban.
  3. Infill: one of four alternative tests must be satisfied: previously developed with an urban use; at least 75 percent of its perimeter adjoining urban-developed parcels; at least 75 percent of the area within a quarter-mile radius developed with urban uses; or, for a four-sided site, at least three sides developed with urban uses plus two-thirds of the perimeter adjoining urban parcels.
  4. Size: twenty acres or fewer.
  5. Density: at least half the local "Mullin density." In Santa Barbara County, where the base density is 30 dwelling units per acre, an eligible project must hit 15 units per acre. Your county's number will differ; the half-density rule will not.
  6. Consistency: comply with local zoning, density, and objective planning standards, all of them, with documentation.
  7. Clean ground: the site must not sit on environmentally sensitive or hazardous ground, and in certain circumstances the statute requires a Phase I environmental site assessment, so order it early rather than discovering the requirement mid-entitlement.

Note what is missing from the list. Unlike earlier CEQA exemptions, the new law imposes no below-market-rate affordable housing requirement and no new wage requirements on projects under 85 feet. It sets no cap on unit count and, unlike the old Class 32 categorical infill exemption, requires no proximity to major transit. Sacramento stripped the conditions that had made previous exemptions unusable, then dared opponents to sue over the result, which is either confidence or a bluff the courts have yet to call.

Find the Cheapest Gate

Here is the analytical contribution nobody in the land-use bar has published yet. SB 131 created a second pathway for projects that miss the exemption on exactly one criterion: a streamlined review limited to the environmental effects caused by that single disqualifying condition, with no alternatives analysis and no growth-inducing-impacts analysis required. Lexology's summary calls it the near-miss provision, and its existence turns exemption screening into an optimization problem.

Consider a 40-unit project on 2.8 acres in Santa Barbara County, which pencils out to 14.3 dwelling units per acre against a 15-unit floor. Under the old law, that project faced full CEQA review and the litigation lottery. Under the new law, it qualifies for streamlined single-condition review, and the single condition is density. Adding two units, or roughly one unit per acre, flips the project from streamlined review to a complete exemption. Whichever gate is cheapest to fix, that marginal criterion is now the highest-leverage design variable on an infill site, so design around it. A 21-acre site fails the size gate; trimming the site plan to 20 acres flips it. A project whose perimeter is 70 percent adjoined by urban parcels fails the infill test; acquiring the adjacent lot, or documenting the quarter-mile-radius alternative, flips it.

This is precisely the kind of screening software does well. Every one of these gates is objective, published, and numerical: acreage, density ratios, perimeter percentages, radius areas. An AI diligence tool can already pull parcel geometry, compute the perimeter and radius tests against assessor data, check the Census urban-area designation, and flag which single gate is cheapest to clear. Cleantech Industry Resources launched EPSA, an AI site-diligence platform that produces full due-diligence reports in minutes against a traditional three-to-four-day timeline. Locus Technologies shipped an AI report agent that assembles environmental compliance reports with humans reviewing only what changed. Ecobot has processed more than 60,000 environmental field reports across 6,500 assessment projects. A LightBox survey of more than 300 environmental professionals found AI moving into Phase I environmental site assessments, the exact document AB 130 requires in certain circumstances, with Terracon's national due-diligence manager predicting AI will have "an outsized role" as clients demand near-real-time answers.

Run the litigation math that follows. Hernandez found the most-sued category of California housing was housing approved in existing communities, which is another way of saying the projects section 21080.66 now exempts. If the exemption captures the majority of the historical anti-housing litigation vector, and each avoided lawsuit represents four to five years of delay during which no lender will fund the project, then the economic value of a correct exemption determination dwarfs the cost of the screening. For decades the industry paid environmental consultants to study impacts. Now the game is paying them, or their software, to prove there is nothing left to study.

Your Opponents Have AI Too

Any honest account must admit the weaponization did not end; it relocated. Last year the Los Angeles Times revealed that CiviClick, an AI-powered advocacy platform, had generated more than 20,000 public comments to defeat clean-air standards at the South Coast Air Quality Management District, some submitted in real residents' names without their knowledge or consent. Advocates are calling for investigations. State Senator Christopher Cabaldon introduced SB 1159 to clarify that AI systems and automated agents do not qualify as "persons" under California transparency laws including CEQA, the Brown Act, and the Administrative Procedure Act, specifically to stop AI from flooding agencies with fake public input. SB 1159 is introduced, not enacted. The flood it targets is already here.

Apply that logic to the new exemption regime: opponents can no longer sue over the EIR, because there is no EIR. They can still sue over the exemption determination itself: was the infill finding supported by substantial evidence, was the Phase I adequate, does the project truly meet the density floor. That same screening AI can mass-produce comment letters attacking each gate. The marginal-criterion game cuts both ways, and the other side knows the math too. A project engineered to clear the density floor by half a unit per acre is a project whose density math will be Exhibit A in the challenge. SB 131 narrowed the administrative record in CEQA litigation, which helps, but the near-miss streamlined review has never been tested in an appellate court, and the first published opinion interpreting it will be read very carefully by everyone in this business.

Here is the strongest case against this article's optimism, stated at full strength: Exemptions do not end lawsuits; they relocate them to the exemption determination, where the standard of review and the evidentiary burden are still being written by courts that have spent fifty years expanding CEQA. A builder who treats the seven-gate checklist as self-executing, without building a substantial-evidence record for each gate, is buying the same litigation at a different address. AI screening accelerates the analysis, but it does not sign the findings, and under current law it cannot testify.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you build infill housing in California, run the seven-gate screen on every project in your pipeline before you retain CEQA counsel, because the first question your lawyer should answer is whether you need CEQA review at all. Free screening tools and AI diligence platforms can compute the objective gates in minutes. Spend the legal budget on the judgment calls instead: the density interpretation, the infill evidence, the administrative record that has to survive a writ petition.

For each gate you clear, assemble the substantial evidence contemporaneously. Photograph the urban perimeter. Pull the assessor maps for the radius test. Document the density calculation against your jurisdiction's Mullin number. An exemption defended by a thick record is an exemption; an exemption asserted in a staff report is an invitation.

Order the Phase I environmental site assessment early, because where the statute requires it a late Phase I is a missed gate, and where it does not the assessment remains the cheapest insurance against the hazardous-ground challenge that could otherwise unwind the entire exemption after you have already designed around it.

If your project misses exactly one gate, price the fix before accepting streamlined review, because the delta between one more unit per acre and a full environmental review is the entire economics of the project. One unit per acre, one acre trimmed from the site plan, one adjacent parcel acquired: compare that cost against even a slimmed-down environmental review plus its litigation tail. That marginal criterion is where the money is, so price it before you price anything else.

Use AI diligence tools for what remains, but disclose their use. A live debate runs through the environmental consulting industry over what counts as proper AI use in Phase I work, so disclose it: a disclosed AI-assisted study survives scrutiny, while an undisclosed one, discovered in discovery, does not.

If you are buying rather than building, ask about the stalled project near you. A half-entitled infill apartment building that has sat in CEQA review since 2024 may now be fully exempt, its developer may not have run the screen yet, and the spread between the project's stalled valuation and its exempt valuation is sitting on the table for whoever notices first. Buying into a project before its sponsor realizes the lawsuit threat evaporated carries a discount. That kind of arbitrage does not last.

What We Did Not Prove

Fourteen months after the governor's signature, no public dataset tracks how many projects have actually used the section 21080.66 exemption, so this article cannot tell you the uptake rate or the win rate on exemption challenges. Santa Barbara County's published briefing figures anchor the density example. Your jurisdiction's Mullin density will differ, its reading of the half-density rule will differ, and the Phase I requirement details come from a county counsel briefing summary rather than the statute text itself. Verify both with local counsel before you rely on either. Vendor claims, not independent benchmarks, stand behind the cited AI diligence tools' speed and capability figures. The LightBox survey describes AI adoption in Phase I assessments generally. It says nothing about CEQA exemption screening specifically. SB 1159 had been introduced but not enacted as of this article's reporting. Zero appellate opinions interpret the near-miss streamlined review. Its litigation risk is therefore unpriced, and unpriced risk is the most expensive kind. Nothing here is legal advice; the code does not care about your feelings, and neither does the court of appeal.

A law that swallowed California housing production just lost its teeth for infill projects. The industry's response has been fourteen months of mostly not noticing. Run the checklist, count the gates, then build.
