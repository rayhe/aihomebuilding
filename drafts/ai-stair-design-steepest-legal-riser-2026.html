Your Stairs Are Legal. They Are Also the Steepest Shape the Code Allows.

By Elena Vasquez, September 15, 2026, Architecture & Design

Climb a staircase the way your body climbs it, without thinking, the way you have climbed ten thousand staircases before, and notice how little of the work your conscious mind is doing. By the fourth riser your feet have memorized the rhythm, and everything after that happens on autopilot, which is precisely the problem, because four steps is the entire calibration. A staircase is a contract between your body and a carpenter you will never meet, and your body signs it in about four steps, before you have formed a single conscious opinion about the geometry.

It is a contract Americans break more than a million times a year, which makes the staircase, that quiet piece of millwork between floors, one of the most injurious objects in the American home, outranked only by the floor itself. Read that twice. Researchers analyzing federal emergency-room data counted an average of 1,076,558 stair-related injuries annually between 1990 and 2012, with direct and indirect costs estimated at $92 billion a year (American Journal of Emergency Medicine). A later analysis of the same federal surveillance system found "stairs or steps" caused 11.3 million injuries from 2014 through 2023, the only household product besides flooring to cross ten million (LendingTree analysis of CPSC NEISS data). In 2020, the Consumer Product Safety Commission's combined category of stairs, ramps, landings and floors ranked first among all product groups: 2.66 million emergency department visits (CPSC NEISS highlights).

None of this surprises architects. What should surprise the rest of us is the shape of the stairs we keep building, decade after decade, as if the dimensions were handed down on tablets rather than negotiated between comfort and floor area. Under the International Residential Code, risers may run up to 7-3/4 inches tall and treads as shallow as 10 inches, with no more than 3/8 inch of variation anywhere in a flight (IRC R311.7.5, via the Stairbuilders and Manufacturers Association). Read that as a designer and you see a safety standard, minimums drawn from a century of falls; read it as a production builder and you see a budget line, because every inch of stair run is floor space that could have been a bedroom, so the economics push builders toward the maximum: a 7.75-inch riser on a 10-inch tread, the steepest staircase the law allows in an American home and, more often than not, the default product, because steep is standard.

Model-home staircases photograph beautifully because the ceilings are tall and the light is warm. All that slope does its quiet work where the camera never looks: in your calves, in your knees, in the small unconscious negotiation your ankles perform fourteen times on the way to bed.

The machine that draws stairs

This is the staircase artificial intelligence has now learned to draw, not as a rendering or a concept but as a buildable object with stringers, treads, risers, landings, dimensions, and a cut list. A plain-language description goes in, "U-shaped stairs, 9 foot rise, closed stringers, landing at half height," and a complete three-dimensional staircase comes out in under a minute. RedX, the most direct of these tools, then runs live code checks as you refine the design, flagging risers over 7-3/4 inches and treads under 10 inches, and exports dimensioned PDF blueprints with full cut lists for every board (RedX stair builders). Graphisoft's ARCHICAD has offered an algorithmic stair tool since 2017 that validates designs against ergonomic and safety specifications across thousands of permutations (Engineering.com).

For a homeowner planning a remodel or an architect iterating a scheme, this is genuinely useful, because stair math is fiddly, unforgiving arithmetic where one wrong division becomes a five-figure mistake poured in concrete, and automating it removes a real source of expensive failure. Having the machine do the division is progress, real progress.

But notice what the machine optimizes for: it checks the code, and only the code, treating the legal minimum as the design target rather than the design floor. Think of the code as a floor, not a ceiling, and remember that the danger lives at the floor.

Do the geometry

Take a standard 9-foot floor-to-floor rise, the most ordinary dimension in American residential construction, and work it two ways, because nothing clarifies a design decision like watching the same nine feet of vertical rise resolve into two completely different staircases.

At the code maximum: 14 risers at 7.71 inches each, 13 ten-inch treads, 130 inches of total run, a 37.6-degree pitch, and a twice-the-riser-plus-the-tread of 25.4 inches, just past the top of the 24-to-25-inch band architects still teach as walkable (RedX publishes the same rule).

Now the gentler version: 16 risers at 6.75 inches, 15 eleven-inch treads, 165 inches of run, and the pitch falls to 31.5 degrees. Six degrees may not sound like much until you stand on it, one foot on a 37.6-degree flight and the other, in memory, on a 31.5-degree one, feeling the difference in your knees before your eyes have finished comparing them. It is the difference between a staircase you descend facing forward and one your body starts treating with suspicion, which is the body's honest review of the geometry, delivered without words.

Six degrees of slope costs 35 inches of floor space, nearly three feet of hallway per story. At typical U.S. construction costs of $500 to $1,000 per square foot, those 35 inches, roughly nine square feet at a 3-foot stair width, represent $4,000 to $9,000 in direct construction cost, before counting the market value of the floor area itself. This is the trade the code maximum quietly encodes: thousands of dollars of space in exchange for a steeper contract with your ankles. Thousands of dollars for six degrees.

And here is the detail that should make everyone pause. RedX publishes its default arithmetic openly: riser count comes from dividing the total rise by a 7.5-inch target. Run a 9-foot rise through that default and you get 14 risers at 7.71 inches. In other words, the AI's first draft is the steep stair. A tool that can generate a thousand code-compliant staircases defaults to the least comfortable legal one, because its target was chosen for compactness, not for bodies, which tells you everything about what optimization means when the thing being optimized was never comfort in the first place. Defaults matter.

What the tools will not tell you

There is no independent audit of RedX's code-check engine, no published pricing, no user count. "Run the code checks" is a vendor claim, and the site itself disclaims local amendments, which are common, with the honest advice to verify everything with your building department. An AI that flags a 7-7/8-inch riser in California may or may not know that your county amended the rule. Treat its green checkmarks the way you would treat a spellchecker: useful, fast, and occasionally confident about the wrong thing, which is fine for typos and less fine for load-bearing geometry. Trust, but verify, with a tape.

Your phone is not a substitute inspector either. iPhone LiDAR scanning, the technology behind a wave of measure-and-model apps, is accurate to about plus or minus one inch in practice (AEC Magazine). A controlled comparison against survey-grade laser scanning put phone LiDAR in a 5-to-15-centimeter accuracy class versus 3 millimeters for professional equipment (MDPI Remote Sensing). No code tolerance is tighter than the stair rule, 3/8 of an inch, and that sensor error is larger than the thing it would need to measure, which is the whole story in one comparison. Your ten-dollar tape measure, dragged riser by riser up the flight with your thumb on the numbers, remains the most advanced stair-auditing technology in your house, which says less about the tape than about the gap between what the sensors promise and what the code requires.

What the code demands is not gentle stairs but uniform ones, because the danger was never really the height of any single riser, it was the difference between one riser and the next. Your feet calibrate to the first two risers of a flight; the riser that differs is the one that catches your toe, which is why the most dangerous stair in a house is often not the steepest one but the one renovated by two different carpenters. When you hear about a fall on a staircase that "met code," uniformity is usually where the story lives.

A fair case for steep stairs

The code maximum exists because floor space is the most expensive material in a house, more precious than oak or stone or glass, and every inch the staircase surrenders is an inch the floor plan cannot spend anywhere else. On a small lot, in an ADU, in any home where the stairwell competes with a bedroom for the same rectangle, shaving three feet off the stair run can be the difference between a compliant plan and a failed one. Production builders are not being careless when they build 7.75-by-10 stairs; they are trading a few degrees of slope for thousands of dollars of sellable space and, at the margin, for housing affordability itself. A mandate for gentler stairs would function as a regressive tax on small homes, paid by the buyers who can least afford it.

Then there is the matter of blame, which belongs mostly to circumstance rather than geometry, and deserves its own hearing. Most stair injuries involve behavior and circumstance more than geometry: carrying laundry baskets, skipping the handrail, descending in socks on finished hardwood, poor lighting, aging knees, the dog asleep on the landing. A perfect 6.75-by-11 staircase with no handrail use and a dark landing is more dangerous than a steep one gripped properly in good light. Geometry is necessary but not sufficient, and any article that pretends the riser height explains a million injuries is selling a simpler story than the data supports.

Which is why the honest position is not that steep stairs should be illegal, or that every production builder is cutting corners, but something more uncomfortable for everyone involved: It is that steepness is a budget line item, and buyers should know they are the ones paying it, in risk rather than dollars. This is the quiet work the code maximum does, disclosed to nobody at closing.

What the numbers cannot tell you

A few honest boundaries before the advice. Start with what the injury statistics actually describe: every staircase in America, including century-old ones built before any code existed; the federal data cannot isolate new construction, so the million-injuries figure indicts the housing stock, not your builder specifically. That 2R+T comfort band dates to a 1675 treatise by Francois Blondel. It is convention, not biomechanics. No survey was found on how many production builders actually default to the code maximum versus gentler dimensions; the claim rests on floor-space economics, which is strong logic but not a measurement. And the floor-space cost figures above are illustrative, built from typical per-square-foot construction costs, not from any builder's books.

Measure your stairs this weekend

Five things, in order of importance, that a homeowner or buyer can actually do.

1. Run the 60-second tape test. Measure every riser in each flight of your stairs. If any two differ by more than 3/8 of an inch, that flight has a uniformity defect the code prohibits in new construction, and a genuine trip hazard whatever its age. Fixes range from shimming a tread to rebuilding the flight, but you cannot fix what you have not measured, and measurement is the part nobody does, because stairs are the one part of the house everyone assumes the builder got right.

2. Ask for 7 by 11 on custom plans. If you are building or remodeling, specify 7-inch risers and 11-inch treads, then watch what happens to the floor plan as the stairwell stretches nearly three feet and the hallway starts negotiating with the bedroom for every inch. You are buying 35 inches of run per story. Know the price, pay it with open eyes, and enjoy the gentlest staircase in the neighborhood.

3. Draft it free before you hire. Tools like RedX will generate a code-flagged 3D stair and a cut list from a single rise measurement at no cost, which means the first draft of your staircase no longer requires a designer, a fee, or an appointment. Bring that first draft to your designer or stair builder. You will have a smarter conversation, and you will know immediately whether their proposal is gentler or steeper than the machine's default, which is the entire point of asking the question out loud before the concrete is poured.

4. Mind the handrail and the light. Code requires handrails on at least one side for stairs with four or more risers, plus light switches at both levels for interior stairs with six or more (IRC R311.7.8, R303.7). For older occupants these matter more than an inch of tread. If your stairs have a rail on one side only, adding the second is the cheapest safety upgrade in the house.

5. Do not trust the phone scan. LiDAR apps are wonderful for room layouts and furniture, for seeing whether the sofa fits and the rug works, but the 3/8-inch uniformity check that actually predicts trips lives below their resolution. Use the tape.

Your staircase was probably legal the day it was built. Legal is the floor, nothing more. Worth asking, tape measure in hand: how far above the floor were your particular stairs built, and was the person who chose the height thinking about your feet or their floor plan? Either staircase, the steep one and the gentle one, the code-maximum and the comfortable, can now be drawn by AI in under a minute, priced to the dollar, and set side by side on the same screen. Which one gets built is still a human decision, and for the first time, it can be an informed one.
