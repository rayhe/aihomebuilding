# Research Notes: HUD AI Permitting Grant — Evidence Gap

## Article #695
**Journalist:** Catherine Chen
**Slug:** hud-ai-permitting-grant-evidence-gap
**Kill test:** Does this help someone building or buying a home? YES — tells homeowners and builders what AI permitting actually means for their timeline, what evidence exists, and whether the federal push will change anything at their local building department.

## Angle
The federal government announced two AI homebuilding grant programs in June 2026 totaling $13M+. The permitting grant (up to $3M) explicitly named four vendor platforms in the NOFO. The strongest published evidence for AI-assisted permitting comes from Honolulu, where 19 CivCheck-processed permits showed a 55% review time reduction — from 73 to 32.5 days. That's the entire evidence base. The federal government is scaling a national policy based on a sample size that wouldn't survive peer review.

## Key Data Points

### HUD Grant Programs (announced May/June 2026, deadline July 13)
1. **Automated Permitting Systems Demonstration (PDR-2600-DC-029O)**
   - Up to $3M total, $300K–$1.5M per city
   - For deploying automated building code permitting systems
   - NOFO explicitly names: "PermitFlow, Blitz Permits, CivCheck, Permitify, or similar platforms"
   - 3-year software licensing, staff salaries, and operational expenses covered
   - Source: HUD NOFO, HUD.gov, Construction Dive (June 11, 2026)

2. **Mass Market Robotics/AI Home Construction (PDR-2600-DC-029Q)**
   - Up to $10M per project, minimum $3M
   - For robotics and AI in factory-built/modular housing
   - Expects results by September 2026
   - Source: Homes.com (June 9, 2026)

### Honolulu CivCheck Results (strongest published evidence)
- Launched December 2025
- **Sample size: 19 CivCheck-processed permits vs 17 non-CivCheck** (total: 36)
- Average review time: 73 days → 32.5 days (55% reduction, ~40.5 days saved)
- Plan review cycles: 3.4 → 1.4 (59% reduction)
- ~40 total applications have used the software
- Backlog went from 6-month wait (2023) to 7 days
- CivCheck will become mandatory later in 2026
- Davis Pitner, DPP: "These are the most current performance results available"
- Source: GovTech (July 2026), Honolulu DPP

### Austin
- Using Archistar's eCheck for single-family home permits
- "Formally adopted this technology as a pre-check layer"
- No published outcome data found
- Source: Independent Institute (March 2026)

### Seattle
- Mayor Harrell signed executive order directing AI pilot program (June 2025)
- CivCheck pilot: 70% reduction in review time (60-90 min → under 15 min per application)
- Full public rollout expected 2026
- Source: GeekWire (June 2025)

### Denver
- Also using CivCheck
- No published outcome data
- Source: PYMNTS (June 2026)

### NAHB Survey
- Only 1% of single-family builders use AI in construction
- Source: 2025 NAHB survey, cited by Homes.com

### Cost of Permitting Delays
- Julia Richman (Clariti VP, former CO Deputy CTO) experienced $100K in delay costs during Denver home renovation
- "Every day that delays the opening is money" — Mike Videla, Permit Rockstar
- Source: StateScoop (June 2026)

## The Tension
1. The evidence is real but tiny — 19 permits in one city
2. The federal government named specific vendors in a grant announcement (unusual for NOFOs)
3. Clariti (CivCheck's parent) is actively "helping governments apply for the grant funds" — per StateScoop
4. The sample wouldn't pass basic statistical significance tests
5. BUT the underlying problem is genuine: permitting delays cost real money and the plan-checker workforce is aging out

## Counterargument (at full strength)
The small sample is partly because the technology is new — CivCheck only launched in December 2025. You can't have large-scale evidence for something that's been deployed for 7 months. The HUD grant is designed precisely to generate that larger dataset by deploying across multiple jurisdictions. Waiting for perfect evidence before funding pilots is a policy catch-22. The early signals are directionally strong (55% reduction is large), and the cost of inaction (continued permitting backlogs) is well-documented. The vendor naming in the NOFO uses "such as" language and includes "or similar platforms" — it's a guidance list, not a mandate.

## Limitations
- No access to the actual NOFO text (only summaries from Construction Dive, VML, etc.)
- Don't know how many cities applied before the July 13 deadline
- No independent verification of Honolulu's numbers (self-reported by DPP)
- No cost-per-permit data for the AI tools themselves
- Don't know what "automated code screening" actually means at a technical level for each vendor

## Original Contribution
Cross-referencing the HUD NOFO announcement with the actual published evidence base from deployed cities reveals a sample-size gap: the federal government is scaling a national program on data from fewer permits than most county offices process in a single week. Nobody else has done this comparison explicitly.
