Fifty-One Percent of U.S. Builders Are Operating at a Loss. A Model Spots the Ones Who Will Fold Two Years Early. Nobody Sells It to Homeowners.

By Frank DeLuca · July 27, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

A contractor walks into a bank and asks for a construction loan. The bank runs a financial model. It checks current assets against total liabilities, revenue trends over three years, equity capital trajectory, and a half-dozen other ratios that together produce a probability score. If that score crosses a threshold, the loan gets denied. The entire process takes minutes, and its accuracy, depending on the model, runs between 90 and 97.5 percent.

Now the same contractor walks into your kitchen and asks you to sign a $350,000 contract to build your house. You get his license number, a handshake, and maybe a Yelp rating.

The bank's model exists. Yours does not.

The Profit Illusion

The 2026 State of Residential Construction Industry Report, published by the Association of Professional Builders, surveyed data from 8,462 builders collected since 2020. Its headline finding is startling: 51.4 percent of U.S. residential builders are operating unprofitably once proper accounting is applied.

Only 17.1 percent of those builders reported making a loss. The remaining 34.3 percent thought they were profitable. They were wrong.

12% of U.S. residential builders correctly understand all four critical financial metrics: Work In Progress adjustments, markup versus margin, fixed expense ratios, and net profit margins. (2026 SORCI Report)

The gap between self-reported and actual profitability comes down to accounting errors that compound over time. Nearly 80 percent of builders who claimed they understood Work In Progress calculations could not correctly explain how WIP is calculated. WIP adjustments determine whether revenue on a partially completed project is overstated or understated on the books. Get it wrong, and a builder who thinks he made $40,000 on your project actually lost $15,000. He may not find out until three projects later, when the cash is gone.

This is not incompetence. Most residential builders are small operators who learned to build, not to run a balance sheet. They price jobs from experience, manage cash by instinct, and carry overhead costs in their heads. It works until it doesn't. And when it stops working, the builder who was busy yesterday is bankrupt tomorrow, with your half-finished house as collateral in a proceeding you were never warned about.

What the Models Can See

Academics have spent decades building models to predict exactly this kind of failure, and the models have gotten very good.

A 2021 study from Poland tested three different approaches on 80 construction companies, half solvent, half insolvent. A regression tree model classified them correctly 97.5 percent of the time. The logit regression model hit 95 percent. Both used six financial ratios: current assets to total assets, sales revenue to current liabilities, total assets to equity, net profit to current assets, and two year-over-year equity and revenue change metrics. Six numbers. That is all it took to separate the companies that would survive from the ones that would collapse.

A South Korean research team built ensemble learning models that predicted financial distress two full years before it materialized, with an area under the receiver operating characteristic curve of 0.94. Three years out, the accuracy dipped slightly to 0.91, still high enough to catch most of the failures that matter. These were not toy models tested on clean data. They used real financial statements from real construction firms over a six-year period.

A 2024 scientometric review from the University of Utah catalogued 93 journal articles on predicting construction company failure. The progression is clear: from Altman Z-scores in the 1990s to neural networks and ensemble methods today, each generation of models got more accurate. The review identified four remaining gaps, and not one of them was "the models do not work." They work. The gaps are about making them work with more diverse data, across more types of firms, in more countries.

92–96% of construction insolvency cases each year involve micro- and small-sized firms, exactly the kind that build residential homes. (2025 sector analysis, Buildings journal)

Who Gets the Score

Banks get it. Every construction lender runs some version of a financial viability model before approving a builder's credit line. The Federal Deposit Insurance Corporation requires it.

Surety companies get it. Under the federal Miller Act, any contractor working on a public project over $100,000 must post a performance bond and a payment bond. To issue that bond, the surety company conducts a detailed financial analysis: three years of audited financials, work-in-progress schedules, bank lines of credit, equipment ownership, backlog aging, and a personal financial statement from the owner. A surety underwriter is, functionally, a bankruptcy prediction model with a desk and a calculator.

Insurance carriers are getting it. Construction Executive reported in 2024 that predictive analytics in builders risk insurance now evaluate subcontractor default probability, construction timelines, material sourcing stability, and weather patterns. Companies like Shepherd have built proprietary underwriting platforms that convert software usage data from tools like Procore and Samsara into premium credits, recognizing that builders who manage projects digitally are measurably lower-risk.

Homeowners get none of it.

What You Actually Get

State licensing boards verify that a contractor has a license. In most states, that license confirms the contractor passed an exam, posted a bond of $15,000 or less (California's minimum is $25,000), and has not had the license revoked. It does not confirm the contractor is solvent, profitable, or likely to be in business next year. A builder with $200,000 in unpaid debts and a negative net worth can hold a valid license in every state in the country.

BBB accreditation is voluntary and pay-to-play. A builder who pays starts with an A+ rating. One who does not may have no rating at all, regardless of quality. Complaints are self-selected and unevenly distributed.

Online review platforms carry an estimated 15 to 25 percent fake review rate across all service categories, according to Federal Trade Commission research. A residential builder with 47 five-star reviews may have paid for 10 of them. You cannot tell which 10.

Mechanics lien filings, the single most useful public record for assessing a contractor's financial distress, are scattered across thousands of county recorder offices with no centralized search. A builder who has been liened three times in the past year in the next county over will not show up in your county's records.

Court records, tax liens, and judgment searches require knowing where to look, paying for access, and understanding what you find. Most homeowners do not do this. Most homeowners do not know they should.

Why the Gap Persists

Residential construction is fragmented in a way that makes centralized risk assessment hard to build and harder to sell. Roughly 750,000 residential construction establishments operate in the United States, according to Census Bureau data. Most are small. Most are private. Most do not file audited financial statements with anyone other than the IRS, and those filings are not public.

Academic models use inputs like current assets, total liabilities, and revenue trends, all available for publicly traded builders like D.R. Horton or Lennar. None of it is available for the three-person framing crew that just bid on your remodel. Building a prediction model requires data that small contractors are not required and not incentivized to share.

But proxies exist. Lien filings are public. Court judgments are public. State licensing complaints are public. Permit volumes are public. Insurance lapses, in states that require continuous coverage reporting, are public. Subcontractor payment patterns leave traces in county records. A builder who stops pulling permits, accumulates liens, and lets insurance lapse is exhibiting the same financial distress signals that the academic models detect in balance sheet ratios. Nobody has aggregated those signals into a consumer product.

Think of it as a credit score for contractors, assembled from public records rather than financial statements, updated continuously, and available to the person about to hand over the money. Nothing like it exists in the U.S. residential market.

40%+ of construction businesses in the UK failed within five years, with domestic building construction at a 43.4% failure rate. Cash flow remains the primary pressure point. (PBC Today, January 2026)

What Australia Got Right, Partially

Australia's construction sector has been devastated by builder insolvencies, with 3,490 firms collapsing in a single year, one every two and a half hours. Construction accounts for roughly a quarter of all company failures in the country. In response, several Australian states now require builders to carry domestic building insurance backed by government-regulated schemes, and some jurisdictions have introduced mandatory financial reporting requirements for licensed builders.

It is partial protection. The insurance covers incomplete work and defects, not the full cost of finding a new builder mid-project, which routinely runs 30 to 50 percent above the original contract price. But it means someone, the insurer, is at least running a financial assessment of the builder before underwriting the policy. In the United States, no equivalent requirement exists for most residential projects. The homeowner is the uninsured counterparty.

What Would Change

A contractor financial health score, assembled from public records and delivered to homeowners at the point of decision, would restructure incentives overnight. Builders with clean records and stable finances would gain a competitive advantage they currently cannot demonstrate. Builders in distress would face market pressure to shore up their balance sheets before they lose contracts, which is better for everyone, including the builder.

Every input is available. Every model is proven. Consumer demand is obvious. What is missing is the commercial product that connects them.

If you are signing a residential construction contract this year, five steps offer partial protection until that product arrives.

Check your state contractor licensing board, and understand that a valid license tells you almost nothing about financial health.

Search the county recorder's office for mechanics liens filed against your contractor in the past three years. Any lien means a subcontractor or supplier was not paid. Multiple liens are a five-alarm signal.

Ask whether your contractor is bonded, and by whom. If a surety company has underwritten a performance bond, someone with a financial model has already assessed the builder's ability to finish the job. If the builder is not bonded, ask why.

Request references from projects completed at least two years ago. A builder who is solvent at completion but bankrupt during the warranty period cannot honor callbacks. The two-year reference test catches this.

Structure payments around verified milestones, never ahead of work completed. Progress-based payment schedules limit your exposure if the builder fails mid-project. In an ideal arrangement, you are never more than one phase ahead of the work on the ground.

None of this replaces the tool that should exist: a data-driven, continuously updated score that does for contractor vetting what the credit score did for lending. The models are sitting in academic journals, validated on real data, achieving accuracy rates that would make any lender comfortable. Somebody needs to build the consumer version. Until then, you are the underwriter, and nobody showed you the file.
