**Kill test:** Does this help someone building or buying a home? YES — anyone building a pool or buying a home with one needs to know: (1) the federal safety law doesn't cover backyard pools, (2) the four-sided fence is the only layer with Cochrane-grade evidence (OR 0.17 vs three-sided), (3) what their state requires at permit and at sale, (4) that drain covers carry a stamped expiration date nobody checks, and (5) what AI drowning detection costs and what it cannot do.

## Thesis
Congress wrote a pool safety law in 2007. It covers 309,000 public pools and ignores the 10.4 million residential ones, 97% of the nation's pools. The layers that actually prevent toddler drowning — isolation fencing first — are governed by a state-and-city patchwork, inspected once at permit time (if that), and then never checked again. Meanwhile the drain covers mandated even on new residential pools carry stamped service lives of 3 to 10 years against a pool life of 25 to 30 years, meaning 3 to 8 replacement cycles go unmonitored on a typical backyard pool. AI drowning detection is now for sale to homeowners ($999-$1,499), but unlike the fence it has no peer-reviewed evidence of effectiveness, and every documented save happened in a guarded commercial pool.

## Original contribution: three calculations nobody has put together

**1. The 97-to-3 enforcement gap.** 10.4M residential pools vs 309K public = 96.9% of US pools sit outside the VGB Act's drain-safety enforcement. The federal law that mandates anti-entrapment covers, stamped service lives, and health-department re-inspection applies to 1 in 34 pools. The other 33 get whatever their city requires at permit time — and California is the only state with a two-feature drowning-prevention mandate on new residential pools.

**2. The expiring-protection math.** A VGB-compliant drain cover's stamped life is 3-10 years (commonly 5-7). A pool's structural life is 25-30 years. That means 3 to 8 full replacement cycles on covers that sit underwater where nobody looks, on pools with no re-inspection regime in most states. A replacement cover costs roughly $50-$150; the failure mode isn't price, it's attention. Virginia Graeme Baker died on a ~10-year-old cover that had gone brittle — the exact failure the service-life stamp was created to prevent, and on residential pools nothing enforces the stamp.

**3. The mandate-cost audit.** On a $65,000-$73,500 new pool, California's two-feature requirement costs: cheapest compliant pair = exit alarms on pool-access doors (~$150) + self-closing/self-latching door hardware (~$100-200) ≈ $300-$350, or 0.5% of project cost. The most effective pair = ASTM F2286 isolation fence ($17-$35/linear ft installed; 100 linear ft = $1,700-$3,500, per Life Saver Pool Fence) + any alarm ≈ $2,000-$3,800, or ~3-5% of project cost. The menu structure lets builders pick the two cheapest features — and the AAP's own technical report says the cheap alarm layer has "limited research" behind it. Effectiveness and price run in opposite directions through the compliance menu.

## The AI angle and its limits
- **What exists:** Coral Mylo ($999-$1,499) brings underwater computer vision to residential pools: entry detection plus continuous in-water trajectory monitoring with escalating alarms. Poseidon (commercial only) is the only system with a claimed save record — 19 lives, all in guarded pools where lifeguards were present. SwimEye and similar systems target the same commercial market.
- **What it cannot do:** detect entrapment risk from a degraded drain cover (the thing with the stamped expiration date); substitute for the passive barrier (fences work when nobody is watching; cameras only work when someone is notified and responds); or change the Cochrane evidence base, which is entirely about physical barriers. No independent study validates any residential AI drowning detector's sensitivity or false-alarm rate — the "~1 false alarm per 3 weeks" figure is a vendor claim.
- **Where AI could matter at the construction stage:** plan-review automation for barrier compliance (the same class of tool Honolulu and Hamilton use for permit pre-screening) has no published pool-barrier application yet — a real gap, since barrier geometry (48" height, 4" openings, 54" latch height) is exactly the kind of rule a vision model can check from a site plan. Stated as an opportunity, not a product.

## Limitations
- No US dataset tracks residential barrier compliance rates; the 83% figure is effectiveness, not adoption. We cannot say what share of the 10.4M residential pools have compliant isolation fencing.
- Vendor claims (Mylo false-alarm rate, Poseidon saves) are unattributed to independent studies; Poseidon's save count comes from the vendor via trade press.
- Pool cost figures are 2025-2026 national averages; California builds run higher. Fence pricing is from manufacturer/installer FAQs, not a transaction dataset.
- The California analysis does not generalize: most states have no two-feature rule, and above-ground pools (4.3M of the 10.4M) face different risk geometry (ladders, not gates).
- CPSC fatality counts are reported drownings, a floor on true incidence; nonfatal injury counts are ED estimates.

## Strongest counterargument
Technology layers — AI cameras, pool alarms, door alarms — may make supervision worse, not better. Drowning is silent and takes under two minutes; a camera that pages a parent who is cooking dinner converts a passive protection problem into an active response problem, and nobody has shown parents respond in time. The evidence hierarchy is unambiguous: four-sided isolation fencing has Cochrane-grade evidence (OR 0.17); alarms have "limited research" (AAP's words); AI detection has vendor claims only. Every documented Poseidon save happened in a guarded commercial pool with a trained lifeguard on the response end — the residential buyer gets the camera without the lifeguard. The counterargument's policy upshot: states that let builders satisfy safety mandates with two alarms instead of a fence are regulating the purchase of devices, not the prevention of drownings.
