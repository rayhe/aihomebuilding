By Frank "The Foreman" DeLuca August 1, 2026

Your Builder Waited Seven Days for a $60,000 Draw. The AI That Would Have Released It in Three Minutes Exists. Your Lender Doesn't Use It.

Somewhere in suburban Texas right now, a framing crew is idle because the lumber is stacked and the plans are posted, a permit is taped to the front window, but nobody is swinging a hammer because the builder submitted a draw request nine days ago and the lender hasn't released the funds.

He needs $62,000 to pay the framing subcontractor, who won't mobilize until the check clears, and the lender needs an inspector to drive to the site, confirm the foundation is complete, photograph the work from multiple angles, write a report summarizing what was observed against what was planned, and attach all of that to a package that also includes six invoices, four lien waivers, an updated insurance certificate, and a revised budget reconciliation showing every dollar disbursed so far. Then a loan officer reviews it all, cross-references the original construction budget, confirms the draw amount against the percentage of completion, checks for liens, and signs off.

According to HousingWire's analysis of construction draw timelines, that process takes, on average, seven business days in residential construction lending, and the human review component alone consumes 20 to 40 hours of labor per draw, which means a single reviewer working full-time on nothing else would need nearly a week to process one request from start to finish.

An AI agent released in production earlier this year does the complete, auditable review in three minutes.

What a Draw Actually Involves

If you've never built a custom home, the draw process is invisible until it breaks your schedule. A construction loan doesn't hand the builder a lump sum. It releases money in stages, typically four to six draws over the life of a build, each one tied to a construction milestone: foundation poured, framing complete, rough mechanical installed, drywall hung, finishes done. Before releasing each tranche, the lender verifies that the milestone is actually finished, and this verification step, which sounds simple when described as a single sentence, is where entire projects grind to a halt while subcontractors idle, carrying costs accumulate, and schedules that were already tight begin to collapse under the weight of a funding process that was designed for a world where nobody was in a hurry.

Each draw request is a package, and not a simple one: it includes the builder's invoice, the subcontractor invoices that justify it, signed lien waivers from every sub and supplier who touched the previous draw, proof of current insurance, inspection photos or a third-party inspection report, and a budget-to-actual reconciliation showing how much of the original loan has been disbursed and how much remains. Miss one document and the package bounces back to you. Wrong lien waiver format gets you bounced, insurance certificate expired two days before submission gets you bounced, and a budget line item that doesn't match the original draw schedule gets you bounced again, each time adding three to five business days to the funding timeline while your subcontractor's crew sits in a parking lot waiting to be told the money is coming.

I have watched draws bounce four times before funding, each round adding three to five business days while the builder's phone rings with subcontractors asking when they're getting paid, the homeowner calls asking why nobody has been on site since Tuesday, and the project manager stares at a Gantt chart that stopped meaning anything two weeks ago. On a $500,000 construction loan at 9 percent interest, every calendar day of delay costs the borrower roughly $123 in carrying costs. Across five draws, with an average of five extra calendar days per draw from the manual process, that is $3,075 in interest that buys you exactly nothing: no square footage, no fixtures, no closer to move-in.

What Built Technologies Did

Built Technologies, a Nashville-based construction finance platform, launched something called Draw Agent in late 2025 and rolled it into full production in early 2026. It is an autonomous AI agent that processes construction loan draw requests end to end, from document ingestion to funding recommendation, without requiring a human to open a single file, compare a single line item, or pick up a single phone.

Here are the numbers, reported by Built and its technology partner MightyBot, and they are specific enough to be useful even though they have not been independently audited: 99%+ accuracy in production across thousands of construction loans; a 95% time reduction on draw reviews, with draws completing in as few as three minutes; 30 to 60 percent improvement in overall draw turn time from borrower request to funding; 400% more risks detected compared to human-led reviews; and 100% policy adherence with documented audit trails for every decision the system makes.

That last number deserves a pause, because four hundred percent more risks detected does not mean four hundred percent more false positives: it means missing lien waivers that a tired reviewer skimmed past at 4 PM on a Friday, budget overruns that only become visible when you compare five sequential draws against the original construction budget simultaneously, insurance gaps that a human might catch on draw four but missed on draw two because the certificate looked current at the time and nobody checked the renewal date against the projected completion schedule three months out.

Built structured the rollout in three graduated modes: audit mode is read-only, assist mode handles routine steps while flagging exceptions for human review, and automate mode executes fully when all policies are met, a design choice that exists because most lender staff do not fully trust the technology yet and need to watch it work before handing it the keys, the same way you let a new estimator shadow for a month before giving them their own bid packages.

Inspections: Where Speed Meets Reality

Document review is only half the draw problem. Someone has to verify that the work described in the draw request actually happened on the job site. Traditionally, this means scheduling a third-party inspector, who drives to the site, walks the property, takes photos, writes a report, and submits it to the lender. In rural areas or during busy building seasons, scheduling alone can take a week.

Truepic, a San Diego visual authentication company, announced a partnership with Cotality in June 2026 to bring verified virtual inspections to construction and renovation draws. Instead of sending an inspector, the system sends a secure link to the borrower, builder, or their agent, who uses a mobile phone to complete a guided property inspection where every image is captured through controlled-capture technology that validates geolocation, timestamp, and tamper detection at the moment the shutter clicks, not after somebody has had time to crop out the part of the foundation that cracked.

Median turnaround is 24 hours, and one lender using the system described funding draws in under 30 minutes from the time the inspection request was sent.

But here is where honesty matters more than enthusiasm. A virtual inspection is not an on-site inspection. An inspector walking the job can look behind walls, check structural connections that aren't visible in any photo, and assess workmanship quality with the practiced eye of someone who has watched a thousand foundations cure and knows what wrong looks like before a moisture meter confirms it. Borrowers submitting photos of their own project have an obvious incentive to photograph favorable angles and skip the corner where the rebar spacing is wrong. Virtual inspections solve the speed problem beautifully, but they do not solve the moral hazard problem at all, and for routine draws on standard residential construction, the trade-off probably works fine, but for complex projects, high-risk borrowers, or draws that represent a large percentage of the total loan, a human on site still has value no phone camera can replace.

Why Your Lender Probably Doesn't Have This

Custom home construction lending is a community bank business, and according to FDIC data, institutions with less than $10 billion in assets hold roughly 30 percent of all construction and development loans, despite holding only 12 percent of total banking assets, which means the lenders who fund most custom homes are the ones least equipped to deploy AI tools. IT departments at these banks are measured in single digits, technology budgets prioritize core banking system upgrades that regulators require over AI agent platforms that regulators haven't mentioned yet, and vendor relationships often predate the smartphone era entirely.

Integration alone is a barrier, since Built's platform requires connections to loan origination systems, document management workflows, and inspection scheduling. For a large regional bank with a dedicated construction lending team, that is a technology project with a known return on investment and a clear implementation path. For a community bank that processes 40 construction loans a year, it is a transformation that touches every system in the building, requires training for staff who have done this work the same way for fifteen years, and delivers savings that are real in aggregate but invisible on any single loan.

Builders and homeowner-builders need to understand what this asymmetry means: if your lender is a community bank, your draw process is almost certainly manual, and the seven-day timeline is not a failure but a structural limitation that you should factor into your decision before signing the loan, not discover after the framing crew is waiting for a check.

What This Means If You Are Building

Three things a builder or homeowner-builder should do right now.

First, ask your lender how they process draws before you commit to the loan. What is their average turn time from draw submission to funding? Do they use a digital platform or is the process email-and-spreadsheet? Do they accept virtual inspections or require an on-site visit for every draw? Answers to these questions will tell you whether your project timeline has a hidden seven-day tax baked into every milestone, and if the lender cannot give you a specific number for average turn time, that alone tells you something about how much attention they pay to the metric.

Second, build the draw timeline into your construction schedule. Five draws at seven business days each means 35 business days consumed by funding logistics alone, roughly seven working weeks, while a lender using AI-assisted draw processing could cut that to 15 business days or less, meaning you save a full month of carrying costs, subcontractor holding fees, and schedule compression that would otherwise cascade through every downstream trade waiting for the previous one to finish and get paid. Over a 10-month build, that month matters.

Third, submit clean packages every single time, because missing lien waivers, expired insurance, and invoices that don't match the approved budget line items will bounce your draw in any system, whether human or automated, and an AI is faster but less forgiving: a human reviewer might call and say "Hey, you forgot the plumber's lien waiver, can you send it over?" while an AI flags it, stops, and moves to the next package in the queue.

What We Don't Know

Built Technologies' performance data is self-reported. No independent audit of the 99%+ accuracy claim or the 400% risk detection improvement has been published, and until one is, those numbers carry an asterisk the size of a foundation footer. Built's client base skews toward larger institutions and commercial projects. Whether the same results hold for a community bank processing 40 residential construction loans a year with a part-time construction lending officer is a question nobody has answered yet. Truepic and Cotality announced their partnership in June 2026, which is too new for outcomes data. And the fundamental tension between speed and thoroughness in construction inspections remains unresolved by any technology. Faster is not always better when the thing being inspected is the structural integrity of someone's home.

Technology to automate it exists, is in production, and is delivering measurable results at scale, but it has not reached the lenders who fund most custom homes because those lenders lack the infrastructure to adopt it, which makes this a distribution problem rather than a technology problem, and until that gap closes, the framing crew in suburban Texas will keep waiting for a check that an AI could have approved before the lumber truck left the yard.
