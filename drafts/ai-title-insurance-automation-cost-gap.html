You Paid $2,200 for Title Insurance. The Industry Paid Out Five Cents of Every Dollar in Claims. An AI Just Qualified 80% of Refinances to Skip It.

By Catherine Chen • July 25, 2026

A three-bedroom ranch in suburban Phoenix, listed at $412,000, closed on a Tuesday in April with the kind of mechanical efficiency that makes real estate agents believe the system works. Pre-approval was done, inspection was clean, appraisal came in at value, and the settlement statement arrived with no surprises except one: a line item on page three, wedged between the recording fee and the transfer tax, reading $2,247 for owner's title insurance. When the buyer asked what it covered, the closing agent said it protected against someone else claiming ownership of the property, and when the buyer asked how often that actually happens, the agent said almost never, and the buyer paid it anyway because the lender required it, the realtor recommended it, and nobody at the table could name a single alternative.

That $2,247 joined approximately $27 billion in annual title insurance premiums collected across the United States, an industry that pays out roughly 5% of what it collects in actual claims. Five cents on the dollar. Ninety-five cents covers salaries, commissions, title searches, marketing, lobbying, and profit. For comparison: homeowners insurance pays about 70 cents of every premium dollar in claims, auto pays about 75, and health insurance under the Affordable Care Act is legally required to pay at least 80. Title insurance operates in a category entirely its own, and the reason is not that the risk it covers is uniquely difficult to price.

Analysts at the Urban Institute ran the math and concluded that if title insurance were priced the way other insurance products are priced, with premiums reflecting actual loss exposure rather than the cost of a distribution apparatus that has remained structurally unchanged since the 1970s, a homebuyer's premium would land below 0.05% of the purchase price. On that Phoenix ranch: $206, not $2,247.

Now a company called Doma has built an AI that processes title searches in seconds instead of days, and Fannie Mae ran a pilot program to find out what happens when qualifying transactions skip the lender's title policy altogether. According to reporting from Inman, 80% of refinance candidates qualified for the waiver. Doma's "Upfront Title" product, which embeds instant title decisioning into the loan application flow, saves borrowers 40% to 70% compared to standard rates. In April 2026, Opendoor acquired a portion of Doma and absorbed 85 employees to handle the demand. Blend Labs, the NYSE-listed mortgage platform, deepened its integration the same quarter. Fannie Mae extended the pilot through 2027.

Industry response has been swift, well-funded, and entirely predictable.

Low Claims as a Feature, Not a Bug

In June 2026, First American published a white paper arguing that a low claims ratio is not evidence of overpricing but proof that the system works, because title insurance differs fundamentally from casualty insurance in that it does not merely indemnify against future events but prevents them. Title professionals search records, examine chains of ownership, and resolve defects before a policy is ever issued, and in this framing the 5% claims ratio reflects an industry that eliminates risk upstream rather than paying for it downstream.

Not a frivolous argument, because professionals in this industry do catch real problems: unreleased liens, boundary disputes, forged deeds, missing heirs, undisclosed easements that would have surfaced years later as six-figure lawsuits had someone not pulled the file at the county recorder's office and flagged the discrepancy before ink hit the closing documents. First American estimates the industry mitigates $600 billion to $900 billion in risk exposure annually. Whether that work has value is not the question. Whether it costs $27 billion is.

ALTA, the industry's trade group, has published research showing that more than 40% of refinance losses are linked to fraud and forgery, risks invisible to any automated search because they exist outside public records entirely. Average claim cost exceeds $200,000. Here is the industry's strongest card, played clearly: AI can read public records faster than a human, but it cannot detect a forged signature on a deed recorded three counties away in 1997, and it cannot chase a fraudster who filed an impersonation scheme across state lines before anyone noticed the name was wrong.

What AI Actually Automates

Doma's technology does not claim to eliminate fraud, and understanding this distinction matters more than most coverage of the company acknowledges. What it automates is the 60% to 80% of the title search process that involves reading and cross-referencing public records: deeds, mortgages, liens, judgments, tax records, plat maps, all the meticulous documentary work that a human title examiner performs by pulling files from county recorder offices, many now digitized, and tracing the chain of title back 40 to 60 years to verify that every transfer, every release, every encumbrance was properly recorded and properly resolved. A typical residential search takes three to five business days. Doma compresses it to minutes.

Cost structure tells you why this matters. Roughly 85% of every premium dollar goes to operating expenses rather than claims: agent commissions, examiner salaries, office overhead, technology systems, marketing, and the referral ecosystem that connects lenders and realtors to title companies through relationships that the homebuyer never sees and rarely questions. Commissions alone can exceed 70% of the premium in some states. A 2015 CFPB study found fewer than 20% of borrowers compared title insurance prices; many did not know they had the option.

When an AI compresses a three-day search into a three-minute search and removes the agent commission from the equation, Doma's 40% to 70% savings are not aspirational projections but the mechanical result of stripping the labor and distribution costs that account for most of what the homebuyer pays, while leaving the actual risk being insured, that narrow 5% paid in claims, completely unchanged.

What Fannie Mae Proved

Fannie Mae's title insurance waiver pilot, launched in 2024, allowed certain low-risk refinance transactions to close without a lender's title policy, using Doma's technology to assess algorithmically whether a clean title could be confirmed. Since then, 80% of refinance candidates have qualified, and CEO Max Simkoff told Inman that "the demand was outstripping our ability to close transactions" before Opendoor's acquisition provided the operational capacity to keep pace with volume that nobody, apparently, had anticipated growing this fast.

What makes the pilot significant is not the qualification rate alone but the institutional weight behind it: a government-sponsored enterprise, for the first time, officially sanctioned a path to closing a mortgage without traditional title insurance. Fannie Mae extended the program through 2027, and if the loss data continues to hold, a permanent policy could follow. Implications for purchase transactions, which carry higher risk than refinances because the chain of ownership changes rather than merely being reaffirmed, remain genuinely unclear, but the precedent is in the record.

Where the Industry's Argument Holds

Fraud is real. An AI reading public records cannot detect an impersonation scheme where a fraudster files a forged deed, takes out a mortgage against stolen property, and vanishes with the proceeds. This happens rarely, but when it happens, the homeowner faces a catastrophic loss that only title insurance makes whole. No responsible analysis dismisses this risk, and Doma does not claim to eliminate it.

But if 80% of refinance transactions can be cleared algorithmically, and the remaining 20% carry concentrated risk, then the economically rational structure is to price insurance for the 20% that need it and let the 80% keep their money, which is exactly what Doma's model does and exactly what the title insurance industry is spending millions to prevent, because those low-risk transactions that are easiest to clear are also, by an enormous margin, the most profitable.

An industry-aligned analysis in HousingWire warned in February 2026 that "coverage decisions should never be based solely on automated review of public records," noting that "some of the costliest risks do not appear in public records at all." Both statements are true, and both are also true of every other form of insurance in existence. Homeowners insurance does not prevent arson; auto insurance does not prevent drunk driving. Undetectable risk is not, by itself, an argument for charging $2,200 to insure $110 worth of expected claims.

What This Means for Your Next Closing

If you are buying a home in 2026, you will almost certainly pay for title insurance, because virtually every lender requires it, because most states set premiums by regulation or filed rates that you cannot negotiate, and because your agent and your lender will recommend a title company that you will use unless you actively intervene in a process designed to discourage exactly that kind of intervention. Expect to pay between $1,000 and $3,500 depending on purchase price and state, for a one-time policy protecting you and your lender against ownership defects for as long as you hold the property.

What you can do is ask whether a title insurance waiver or alternative product is available. For refinances, the Fannie Mae pilot has created a genuine option that did not exist two years ago. For purchases, some states allow attorney opinion letters as an alternative, Iowa operates a state-run title guaranty program at a fraction of private-insurer cost, and a handful of lenders are experimenting with reduced-rate products for transactions that pass automated risk screening.

Technology to make title insurance dramatically cheaper already exists and has been validated by the largest mortgage purchaser in the country across hundreds of thousands of transactions, the AI works, the pilot data is in, and what remains is whether the regulatory and distribution infrastructure that keeps a 5%-loss-ratio product priced like a 70%-loss-ratio product will change before the next generation of homebuyers stops paying $2,200 for $110 worth of risk and starts demanding the math add up.

Limitations

This analysis relies on industry-wide loss ratios reported by the Urban Institute and ALTA. Loss ratios vary by state, by transaction type (purchase vs. refinance), and by policy type (owner's vs. lender's). Doma's 80% qualification rate applies specifically to the Fannie Mae refinance waiver pilot and may not be representative of purchase transaction risk profiles. We could not independently verify Doma's savings claims of 40-70%. Fraud and forgery risk cited by ALTA (40%+ of refinance losses) is not broken down by transaction value or geographic distribution in publicly available data.
