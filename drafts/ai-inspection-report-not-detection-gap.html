Your Home Inspector Used AI to Write the Report. The AI Never Looked at Your House.

By Jake Kowalski · July 30, 2026

Efra Rivera crawls under houses for a living. He shines a flashlight at floor joists, photographs discolored drywall, and decides whether the stain above your kitchen means a $200 caulk job or a $12,000 foundation problem. That part of his work hasn't changed, but what changed is that he stopped typing.

Rivera is an early user of Spectora's AI Report Assist, one of three AI tools the Denver-based inspection software company launched into early access in June 2026. He speaks his observations into his phone while snapping photos, and the AI matches what he says to pre-approved comment templates in his report library. "Instead of stopping to search for comments, I can queue up multiple defects using audio, and the AI matches them to the right narratives," Rivera told Spectora. Inspectors in early access report cutting about 25% of their time per inspection, finishing reports on-site instead of at the kitchen table that night, which is a real improvement for a profession where the paperwork has always been the part nobody went into business to do.

But read the press release carefully and you notice something conspicuous in its absence: not one of Spectora's three new AI tools detects a defect the inspector didn't already see. AI Report Assist matches voice notes to templates. An AI Scheduling Agent answers the phone when the inspector is on a roof. A connector tool lets inspectors plug ChatGPT into their business data for revenue forecasting. All three are administrative tools wearing AI's marketing uniform, and every one of them is useful, genuinely useful, in the way that a better filing cabinet is useful to someone who files a lot of paper. None of them looks at your house.

Home inspection is not a documentation problem. It is a detection problem, and that distinction changes what the 25% number actually means for the buyer who pays the bill.

What $343 Actually Buys

A standard residential inspection in the United States costs an average of $343, according to Angi's 2026 data, with a typical range of $296 to $424 and prices climbing past $500 in high-cost markets like San Francisco and Manhattan. An inspection of a 2,000-square-foot home takes two to four hours and covers the structure, roof, electrical, plumbing, HVAC, insulation, and accessible crawlspaces, where the word "accessible" does a lot of heavy lifting because inspectors do not open walls, do not pull up carpet, do not move furniture, and do not test behind installed appliances. A standard inspection is, by design and by industry practice, entirely visual.

Repair Pricer analyzed 50,000 home inspection reports and found that more than one million repairs across those homes would have cost over $11,000 each to fix. Fifty-five percent of homes had doors that needed adjusting, which can indicate foundation settlement, and forty-eight percent lacked GFCI protection in water-prone areas like kitchens and bathrooms, a genuine electrocution risk that costs $50 to $200 per outlet to remedy but can cost a life if it stays unfixed. A Porch study found that 86% of buyers discovered at least one issue requiring repair during their inspection.

Those are the problems inspectors catch. Nobody publishes reliable data on the ones they miss, because the incentives run the wrong direction: an insurer who could prove inspectors systematically missed a category of defect would use that data to adjust policy pricing, not to share it with an academic journal. What we know from trade anecdotes and consumer reporting is directional. Inspectors regularly miss roof damage they don't climb up to see, appliance failures they only test with a power switch, and behind-wall conditions that are, by definition, invisible to a protocol that forbids touching drywall.

Where AI Does Detect Defects

Automated defect detection technology exists, and in commercial settings it works remarkably well, which makes its absence from residential inspection all the more glaring.

At the University of Houston, Vedhus Hoskere published a method in March 2026 that combines ground-penetrating radar with a convolutional neural network called InternImage to detect hidden damage in cold-formed steel framing behind finished walls, flagging the probable location, severity, and type of structural damage without anyone removing a single sheet of drywall. It is an impressive piece of engineering applied to a real problem in nonresidential buildings, where cold-formed steel appears in 30% to 35% of structures. It is also irrelevant to single-family homes, where cold-formed steel framing is rare and the research was never designed to operate.

Drone-based facade inspection is further along and closer to deployment. Multiple academic teams have trained convolutional neural networks on high-rise exterior imagery, achieving defect detection accuracy above 92% for cracks, spalling, staining, and corrosion, with systems that process photographs through transfer-learned classifiers and output bounding boxes with confidence scores directly onto building schematics so that an engineer can pinpoint the problem from a desk rather than a scaffold. Those systems inspect curtain walls on office towers and concrete facades on parking structures, and they have not been adapted for split-level ranches in suburban New Jersey, because nobody has built that product and nobody appears to be building it.

Paraspot, reviewed by Inman in late 2025, uses computer vision and audio narration to automate property condition reports, identifying common issues, categorizing images by room, and generating reports in minutes. But Paraspot's primary market is commercial multifamily, single-family rentals, and student housing, where inspections are repetitive, geometrically uniform, and performed at portfolio scale across buildings that look nearly identical from unit to unit. A first-time buyer walking through a 1962 Cape Cod with a real estate agent is not Paraspot's customer.

Even the June 2026 research from National Taiwan University, published in Automation in Construction, which developed a system to automatically anchor inspection photos to their exact location in a building's digital model, requires one thing residential homes almost never have: a BIM model. Elegant technology, built for buildings that have digital twins, solving a problem that residential construction does not yet know it needs solved.

A Throughput Gap Nobody Talks About

Consider the math, because it reveals the size of the opportunity that current AI inspection tools are leaving on the table. A typical inspector covers a 2,000-square-foot home in roughly three hours, examining structural elements, electrical panels, plumbing fixtures, HVAC systems, windows, doors, roofing when they're willing to climb, insulation, grading, and drainage, which amounts to roughly 3,000 to 5,000 discrete observations at a rate of 17 to 28 per minute, each one depending on training, attention, fatigue level, and willingness to crawl into a tight spot on a hot afternoon in July.

A computer vision system running at 30 frames per second processes 1,800 image frames per minute, and even at a conservative analysis rate where only every tenth frame triggers meaningful classification, that works out to 180 processed observations per minute, each evaluated against a trained model that doesn't get tired, doesn't rush because the next appointment is in 45 minutes, and doesn't skip the attic because the pull-down stairs look rickety. Roughly an order of magnitude separates what a human inspector actually does from what a computer vision system could theoretically do, but that gap remains entirely theoretical for residential buyers, because no product bridges it.

Fair Credit Where It's Due

Spectora's counterargument writes itself, and it is fair. If AI Report Assist saves 25% of total inspection time, and most of that saved time was spent typing and searching through comment templates, then inspectors now have that time back to spend looking at the house instead of looking at a keyboard. More looking means more catching. An inspector who finishes reports on-site and drives home at 4 PM instead of typing until 9 PM is a less fatigued inspector, and fatigue is a real variable in detection quality, one that nobody in the industry quantifies but every inspector who has been through a four-inspection day understands in their joints.

Where this argument breaks down is at the boundary between attention and access. A fresh inspector might notice a hairline crack in a foundation wall that a tired one would miss, and for defects like that, faster report writing genuinely helps. But most of the defects that generate five-figure repair bills and six-figure insurance claims hide behind physical barriers: termite damage inside wall cavities, failing sewer laterals under foundations, roof leaks that only manifest during a rainstorm the inspection didn't happen to coincide with. For those problems, the binding constraint is instrumentation and physical access, not whether the inspector had time to type carefully. Faster typing does not produce X-ray vision.

What a Buyer Should Know

If your inspector mentions AI, ask what it does. As of mid-2026, the honest answer is that it helps write the report faster, and that is worth something, because a report finished on-site is a report you can read the same afternoon, which matters when you have a five-day inspection contingency and every hour of delay compresses your decision window. But faster report writing is not better defect detection, and the marketing conflates them because "AI-powered inspection" sounds like the tool finds more than a human alone would.

ServiceTitan's 2026 Residential State of the Trades Report found that 74% of contractors view AI as an efficiency engine, but only about 25% are actually using it, and the gap between perception and adoption tells a familiar story: the industry knows AI is arriving, but what has arrived so far solves the problems that were easiest to solve. Scheduling, typing, revenue forecasting. Not the hard problems that hide behind drywall and under soil, which require sensors and models that nobody has built for the residential market yet.

When someone does, the home inspection industry will genuinely change. Until then, your $343 buys what it has always bought: a professional with a flashlight, a ladder, and three hours to look at your house. AI just writes it up faster.

Limitations of This Analysis

No published, peer-reviewed data exists on residential inspection defect miss rates, and the insurance industry, which holds the claims data that would enable such analysis, has not released it for independent study. Spectora's 25% time savings figure is self-reported by early access users and has not been independently verified. Computer vision accuracy figures cited above come from controlled academic settings rather than field deployments against the messy, variable conditions of actual residential properties where lighting, angles, surface materials, and obstruction differ from one house to the next in ways that lab environments cannot replicate. Visual inspection involves judgment, context, and spatial reasoning that frame-counting cannot capture, so the throughput comparison between human and machine observation is directional rather than precise.
