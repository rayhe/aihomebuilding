You Fired Your General Contractor. Now the Camera Is Your Superintendent.

By Frank DeLuca · September 19, 2026 · 7 min read

Tuesday, 7:40 in the morning, and your framer was supposed to have four men on site at seven. You are forty minutes away pouring coffee in your kitchen, and the wide-angle feed on your phone shows an empty lot with yesterday's lumber still wrapped in plastic. In twelve seconds you have just done the job you used to pay a superintendent to do.

This is the owner-builder's bargain, and it is older than the power nailer. Skip the general contractor, keep the 10 to 25 percent markup for yourself, and take on the scheduling, the sequencing, the sub management, and the daily site walk that nobody warns you about. On a $400,000 build the savings can run $40,000 to $100,000, which is the number that gets people to sign up. The number that gets them in trouble is the one nobody puts on a spreadsheet: the cost of not having eyes on the job.

Custom homebuilding now accounts for roughly one in five single-family starts, about 183,000 homes over the last four quarters according to the National Association of Home Builders, and it has been the one steady segment while overall single-family construction fell nearly 7 percent in the first seven months of 2026. Much of that custom work is contract building, the industry's polite term for projects where the owner is in the loop on everything, and many owners end up deeper in the loop than they planned.

10–25%
The typical GC markup an owner-builder avoids, and the superintendent's salary they have to replace with something. That something is increasingly a camera with software attached.

The camera learns to write the daily report

Jobsite cameras are not new. OxBlue has been selling them since 2001, and every commercial GC above a certain size has a camera on a pole somewhere. What is new is the software layer sitting on top of the video. This month a Campbell, California company called Alpha Vision announced that its platform will generate construction progress reports from timelapse video across connected cameras, and that AI agents will automatically produce PPE non-compliance case reports every 24 hours, flagging which workers were not wearing hard hats or vests, where, and when. The company is demonstrating the system at Blueprint Vegas, the built-world tech conference running September 22 through 24.

Read that carefully, because the pitch has shifted. Where the old pitch was security, catching the after-hours lumber thief, the new pitch is supervision: the camera watches the schedule, watches the crew, and writes the report you would have read over coffee if you had hired someone to stand on your lot all day. For an owner-builder who cannot be on site, that is the single most valuable document in the project, because the draw inspection, the sub dispute, and the schedule argument all come down to the same question, which is what actually happened on Tuesday.

What the kit costs, honestly

The commercial camera vendors do not publish price lists, but they publish enough to do the math. TrueLook puts its hardware between $1,000 and $9,000 depending on capability, with monthly subscriptions from $99 to $600. OxBlue puts monthly service between $200 and $450 at the low end and $500 to $900 at the high end, with cameras that run on 120-volt power or solar panels sized from 45 watts up and a five-day battery backup for the stretches when the temp power is not in yet. TrueLook suggests a financing trick that owner-builders and small builders should steal outright: buy the camera once, roughly $2,500 for a decent unit, and bill it to each project as a reimbursable line item, say $400 a month in equipment rental plus $300 a month in monitoring, the way you would bill a dumpster or a site trailer. On a twelve-month build that is $8,400 in reimbursed costs against a $2,500 purchase.

For a single owner-build, the honest budget is $2,500 to $5,000 in hardware and $200 to $500 a month in service over a nine to twelve month schedule. Against a $60,000 markup savings that is noise, and against a schedule slip it might have prevented, it is the cheapest insurance on the project.

What the camera cannot see

Now the part where the foreman in me ruins the sales pitch: a camera records pixels, not quality. It confirms the framer was on site at 7:40, but it does not confirm the nailing pattern, the flashing detail behind the siding, or the fact that the plumber ran the drain line through a load-bearing stud. Progress is not correctness, and an AI progress report inherits that blindness with total confidence. I have watched twenty years of projects go sideways, and almost none of them went sideways in a way a wide-angle lens would have caught. They went sideways inside walls.

There is also the awkward question of whether the savings you are protecting actually exist, and a contrarian analysis from the design-build world lays it out brutally: contractors mark up sub fees around 20 percent, but they also extract roughly 10 percent in trade discounts you will never see, and your own time has a price. Twenty hours a week for eighteen months at professional billing rates is a six-figure number, and every month the project runs long is another month of rent or temporary housing. The camera does not fix that arithmetic; it just documents it in high definition.

The legal traps are real, and one of them is a crime

Before you mount anything, understand that filming workers is regulated, and the rules change at state lines. Federal law tolerates visible workplace cameras for legitimate business reasons, but several states require written notice to everyone being recorded. Connecticut and Delaware demand prior written notice of any electronic monitoring. California courts have advised employers to disclose surveillance in writing and get a signed receipt. New York requires written notice at hire, an acknowledgment, a posted notice, and an annual repeat.

Audio is the trap that ruins people, because video-only surveillance is one legal regime and the moment your camera records sound you are in wiretapping law. In all-party-consent states like California and Florida, recording a conversation without everyone's consent is a violation per conversation. Turn the microphone off, then check the app settings, because many cameras ship with audio enabled by default.

Two more things a superintendent would have told you. Cameras cannot go anywhere workers have a reasonable expectation of privacy, which on a residential site means the portable toilet area and any changing space, and surveillance that chills union organizing can run afoul of federal labor law. So the written notice you give your subs should state a business purpose, progress documentation and security, and nothing about evaluating individual performance. Good subcontractors are in short supply, the ones worth hiring will ask about the cameras, and the answer you want ready is that the footage protects them too, because it proves they were there and proves what they did.

The owner-builder camera playbook

  1. Two cameras, two jobs. One wide-angle unit on a pole or high mount covering the whole lot for progress documentation and lender draw evidence, plus one pan-tilt-zoom aimed at the active work zone for detail. Solar kits with cellular uplink mean no temp power and no Wi-Fi dependency. Buy the hardware once and bill it per project the way you would bill a dumpster.
  2. Put the cameras in the sub agreements, in writing: visible cameras, video only, no audio, business purpose stated. In Connecticut and Delaware this is legally required; in California get a signed acknowledgment; in New York post the notice.
  3. Audio stays off, because in all-party-consent states recorded audio is a wiretapping violation per conversation. Check the default settings twice.
  4. Use the footage for what it is good for: verifying who was on site and when, documenting conditions for draw inspections and disputes, catching after-hours theft. Do not use it as a substitute for inspections, and do not judge trade quality from your couch. The camera is your superintendent's eyes, not your inspector's hands.
  5. Save the timelapse. A full-project timelapse costs a few hundred dollars from most vendors and is the single best document you will own at resale, at warranty time, and in any dispute. Future you will thank present you.

Every owner-builder makes the same gamble: trade money for control, and hope the control is worth more than the money. For the first time, the control part has a product you can buy off a shelf, mount on a pole, and expense for less than one percent of the build. It will not frame your walls straight. It will tell you, every morning, whether anyone showed up to try, and in this business that is half the job. It is the half that used to require a truck, a thermos, and a man with a name like mine.
