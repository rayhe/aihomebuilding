# Research: Who Owns AI-Generated House Plans? (Article #762)

**Journalist:** Catherine "Code" Chen (policy & regulation beat)
**Slug:** `ai-generated-house-plans-copyright-nobody-owns-2026`
**Date:** September 3, 2026
**Kill test:** Does this help someone building or buying a home? YES. Anyone paying an architect or designer for plans — custom ($72K–$135K in design fees on a typical $900K custom build) or AI-generated stock plans — needs to know whether those plans can actually be protected from copying. If the answer is "thin to none," that changes what the fee buys, what contract language to demand, and whether a copycat builder down the street can legally reuse the design.

## Thesis

Generative design tools (Midjourney massing studies, TestFit feasibility layouts, Autodesk Forma/AI massing, ARCHITEChTURES, Maket) are now inside residential design workflows. Federal copyright law, as clarified by the Copyright Office (Jan 2025) and the D.C. Circuit (Mar 2025), protects only human-authored expression. Prompts don't count. So a growing share of residential plan sets sits in a legal gray zone: the more AI did the expressive work, the less of the plan set the "owner" can actually stop someone else from copying. Standard AIA contracts assume a human author and don't address this. The practical consequence runs in a perverse direction: the client who paid full custom fees may hold weaker rights than the client whose architect drew by hand.

## Primary sources (6)

### 1. U.S. Copyright Office, "Copyright and Artificial Intelligence, Part 2: Copyrightability" (Jan 29, 2025) — PRIMARY (government report)
- Outputs of generative AI are protected **only where a human author has determined sufficient expressive elements**.
- **Prompts alone do not constitute authorship**, even detailed, effortful prompt engineering: "Prompts do not appear to adequately determine the expressive elements produced, or control how the system translates them into an output."
- Human-authored work perceptible in AI output, or creative arrangement/modification of AI output, CAN be protected — but only the human contributions.
- AI as a tool (ideation, editing) does not taint the whole work; case-by-case, fact-specific analysis.
- Office found "the case has not been made" for legislative change to protect AI outputs.
- Register Shira Perlmutter: "Where that creativity is expressed through the use of AI systems, it continues to enjoy protection. Extending protection to material whose expressive elements are determined by a machine, however, would undermine rather than further the constitutional goals of copyright."
- Source URLs: https://newsroom.loc.gov/news/copyright-office-releases-part-2-of-artificial-intelligence-report/s/f3959c36-d616-498d-b8f9-67641fd18fab ; https://blogs.loc.gov/copyright/2025/02/inside-the-copyright-offices-report-copyright-and-artificial-intelligence-part-2-copyrightability ; Skadden summary https://www.skadden.com/insights/publications/2025/02/copyright-office-publishes-report

### 2. Thaler v. Perlmutter, 130 F.4th 1039 (D.C. Cir. Mar 18, 2025) — PRIMARY (federal appellate decision)
- Unanimous 3-judge panel (opinion by Judge Millett): Copyright Act requires works "be authored in the first instance by a human being" — as a matter of statutory law.
- Court identified 6+ statutory references to "author" intelligible only for humans: capacity to own property, lifespan-based term (life+70), widow/widower and surviving children inheritance, signature requirements for transfers, nationality/domicile, author's intent.
- The Act refers to machines only as "tools."
- Work-made-for-hire argument rejected: a non-human can't be an author, so there's nothing to own via that doctrine.
- Case: Stephen Thaler listed his "Creativity Machine" as sole author of "A Recent Entrance to Paradise"; Copyright Office denied registration; D.D.C. affirmed (2023); D.C. Cir. affirmed.
- Note: court did NOT reach the waived argument that Thaler himself was the author by virtue of creating/using the machine — that door is technically still open but untested.
- Sources: https://fairuse.stanford.edu/case/thaler-v-perlmutter/ ; https://constitutioncenter.org/amp/blog/federal-court-rules-artificial-intelligence-machines-cant-claim-copyright-authorship ; https://www.loeb.com/en/insights/publications/2025/03/thaler-v-perlmutter

### 3. Copyright Office Review Board, "Zarya of the Dawn" (Kristina Kashtanova, Feb 2023) — PRIMARY (agency decision, via Mondaq summary)
- Comic book text (human-written) and the selection/coordination/arrangement of images: copyrightable. The Midjourney-generated images themselves: NOT copyrightable.
- Template for the plan-set analysis: in a set of construction documents, the architect's hand-drawn parti, manual CAD drafting, and curated arrangement may be protected; raw AI-generated elevations/massing images likely are not.
- Source: https://www.mondaq.com/unitedstates/copyright/1588504/copyrightability-of-ai-outputs-us-copyright-office-analyzes-human-authorship-requirement

### 4. AIA Contract Documents, "Who Owns Building Design in the Age of AI?" (May 22, 2024) — PRIMARY (industry body)
- AIA's own contracts publication acknowledges the ownership question is unresolved and jurisdiction-dependent.
- Flags the derivative-work risk: if AI output is arguably derived from training data (other architects' designs), existing copyright law may leave ownership with the training-data creators — meaning your "custom" AI-generated plan could theoretically infringe someone else's design.
- Source: https://learn.aiacontracts.com/articles/the-ownership-dilemma-who-owns-building-design-in-the-age-of-ai/

### 5. Frantz Ward LLP, "AI Is Already on Your Project. Your Contract Doesn't Know It." (JDSupra) — PRIMARY (law firm analysis)
- 5 specific gaps in AIA contracts re: AI: (1) standard of care, (2) design document responsibility, (3) IP/ownership of AI-generated content, (4) third-party technology risk allocation, (5) insurance alignment.
- "AIA contracts do not clearly allocate responsibility between the project team and the software provider if those tools fail"; ownership/licensing questions remain open "especially when third-party AI platforms are involved."
- Recommended fix: address in supplementary conditions whether AI is used, human oversight expectations, responsibility allocation, **ownership and permitted use of AI-generated content**, insurance coordination.
- Source: https://www.jdsupra.com/legalnews/ai-is-already-on-your-project-your-4185383/

### 6. AIS Designs Co. v. J.J.S. Custom Built Homes, Inc., No. 4:24-cv-837 (S.D. Tex., jury verdict) — PRIMARY (case outcome via OBWB report)
- Jury found home-design copyright infringement; court entered judgment: J.J.S. Custom Built Homes to pay **$278,605.95**; Valleri Salazar **$570,407.19**; Jose de Jesus Salazar **$6,984** — roughly **$856,000 total**, plus permanent injunction.
- This is what enforceable plan copyright is WORTH when the plans are human-authored and registered. The article's contrast: none of this enforcement machinery works for expression a machine authored.
- Statutory backdrop: 17 U.S.C. § 504(c) allows up to $150,000 per work for willful infringement — but only for registered, copyrightable works.
- Source: https://www.obwb.com/ais-designs-co-prevails-in-home-design-copyright-litigation

## Original contribution: the plan-set copyrightability audit
No one has mapped the Copyright Office's human-authorship framework onto a residential plan set. The article will do a worked example — a hypothetical $900K custom home, $96K design fee (midpoint of the 8–15% custom-residential fee range) — and classify each deliverable:
- **Likely protected (human-authored):** hand-sketched parti and massing concepts; architect's manual CAD/BIM drafting and detailing; the curated *selection and arrangement* of AI-generated options into a coherent scheme (Zarya logic); site-specific problem-solving (grading, structural coordination).
- **Likely NOT protected (machine-determined expression):** raw Midjourney/DALL-E elevation renderings passed through unmodified; unmodified TestFit-generated unit/floor layouts where the architect only picked from outputs; AI-written specification boilerplate reproduced verbatim.
- **The dollars:** a registered human-authored plan set unlocks statutory damages up to $150K/work for willful infringement (the AIS Designs verdict shows ~$856K real-world recovery). The AI-generated portions unlock $0 and can't even be registered. So the client paying $96K may be buying a plan set whose most visually distinctive elements — the AI-generated elevations the neighbors will actually copy — are the least protectable.
- **Methodology note:** this is a legal-reasoning exercise applying published Office guidance to a hypothetical workflow, not a legal opinion; outcomes are fact-specific per the Office itself.

## Strongest counterargument (at full strength)
The counterargument is genuinely strong: (a) In practice, almost no residential plan set is "purely" AI-generated — architects modify, detail, stamp, and take professional responsibility, and that human overlay may be enough to protect the set as a whole under the Office's "AI as tool" doctrine. (b) Copyright registration is rarely the actual enforcement mechanism in residential plan piracy; contracts, licenses, and practical obscurity do most of the work, so the copyrightability gap may matter less than this article suggests. (c) The D.C. Circuit explicitly left open whether a human who creates and directs the AI system can claim authorship — Thaler waived that argument, so the strongest pro-protection theory has never been tested. The article must concede all three, then answer: the gap matters at the margin where it matters most — high-value distinctive designs worth copying, where the infringer's defense ("your honor, the expressive elements were machine-determined") is now citable federal guidance.

## Limitations (to state in article)
- Not a legal opinion; no attorney-client relationship; fact-specific inquiry per the Copyright Office.
- The "waived argument" in Thaler (human as author via creating/using the AI) is untested — a future case could narrow the gap.
- Fee range (8–15%) is industry rule-of-thumb, varies by market and scope; the $96K figure is illustrative.
- No data exists on how many residential plan sets are AI-generated or have faced authorship challenges at the Copyright Office — the registration-denial data isn't broken out this way.
- AIA has not issued AI-specific contract amendments as of this writing; the Frantz Ward recommendations are proposals, not standard language.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
1. If commissioning custom plans: add a supplementary condition to B101 requiring the architect to disclose which deliverables were AI-generated and to warrant which portions they consider human-authored.
2. Ask who owns the *selection*: get the architect's optioneering record (rejected AI schemes) — it evidences human selection/arrangement, which is the protectable part.
3. Register the human-authored portions with the Copyright Office promptly (within 3 months of publication preserves statutory damages eligibility, 17 U.S.C. § 412).
4. If buying AI-generated stock plans: assume thin protection; compete on execution and service, not on the un-copyability of the design.
5. Never upload proprietary or client-confidential plans into public AI tools without checking the platform's training-data terms (data disclosure risk per Sonis/Frantz Ward).

## Skepticism
- The 6% AIA regular-use figure cuts against urgency: most residential architects still draw by hand/CAD. The article must not oversell how AI-saturated residential practice is today. The honest frame: the law is now settled BEFORE the wave arrives — which is rare and worth acting on during the window.
- Did not verify whether any residential architect has actually been denied a plan registration on AI-authorship grounds. None found in research.
- The justoborn.com "86,000 homes / ICC AC547" result encountered during research appears to be fabricated AI slop (unverifiable Reuters/WSJ citations, unknown ICC standard); deliberately excluded.
