# Research: AI Rebuilds Lost Victorian Details — The "False History" Line (2026)

**Slug:** ai-rebuilds-lost-victorian-details-false-history-2026
**Journalist:** Elena Vasquez (Architecture & Design)
**Date:** September 14, 2026

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? YES. Anyone restoring a historic home (or buying in a historic district) faces the exact problem this story solves: how to replicate lost architectural details, what the federal preservation code actually allows, and where AI-generated detail crosses the line into illegal "false history." Actionable: photograph everything now (your future replication depends on it), restore windows instead of replacing them (240-year payback on replacement), and use the three-question test before letting an architect mill an AI's guess into your facade.

## Primary sources (4)

### 1. Palo Alto Online — "How AI helped rebuild a Victorian tower lost in Palo Alto during the 1906 quake" (May 28, 2026)
https://www.paloaltoonline.com/palo-alto/2026/05/28/how-ai-helped-rebuild-a-victorian-tower-lost-in-palo-alto-during-the-1906-quake/

- A.G. Herzinger House, 1023 Forest Ave, Palo Alto (Crescent Park neighborhood, ~2 blocks south of University Ave). Built 1896 for ~$6,500. 4,516 sq ft. Category 2 historic resource on Palo Alto's historic inventory. Commissioned by Agnes G. Herzinger; architect C.H. Barrett; contractor M.P. Madison.
- Great San Francisco Earthquake of 1906: under 45 seconds of shaking toppled the chimney and tore away the round lookout tower (belvedere). Tower never replaced for ~120 years.
- 3-year restoration by architect Lee I. Lippert (Lippert & Lippert Design); GC Jim Parden (Parden Construction). Seismic retrofit first (shear walls, steel beams hidden in walls), then architectural restoration.
- House predates detailed permits/building codes: almost no paper records. "Architectural forensics" — paint-layer archaeology, old photos, vintage design catalogues. Lippert: "I haven't slept in three years."
- Generative AI analyzed the single pre-1906 photograph to approximate spatial relationships and proportions of the missing belvedere.
- Tapered rope columns + Scamozzi capitals matched pieces still intact on the second-story porch. Team stripped paint from best-preserved originals, laser-scanned them into 3-D models, milled replacements from engineered wood. New pieces conceal modern steel supports.
- Vitruvian acanthus leaf frieze: generative AI analyzed the photo to interpret the missing pattern and generate a scaled CAD drawing. Artists at the American College of the Building Arts (Charleston, SC) hand-sculpted molds and cast 25 decorative panels ringing the structure.
- Belvedere roof: team deliberately chose copper, a material very different from the original cedar shingle, for fire safety AND so it would not be mistaken for original. Roof shape inspired by a bell-shaped gazebo at Olde Mystic Village, Connecticut.
- Finial: scaled from the surviving finial on the home's conical turret; original belvedere finial ~25% larger with a more elongated profile. Fabricated in copper, mounted on a steel support tube; doubles as a lightning rod (electrically isolated, grounded at foundation).
- Chimneys: rebuilt with lighter modern construction under historically accurate finishes. Recessed terra cotta tile inlays reproduced from a Gladding, McBean & Company pre-1896 catalogue pattern found online; company still operating but tiles no longer manufactured, so a Southern California artisan recreated them by hand. Arches: vitrified clay pipe section hidden beneath thin brick cladding on a steel frame.
- The line: "If we can't replicate something accurately, we'd rather do something clearly different than create confusion about what's original." — Lippert. Preservation standards discourage "false history."

### 2. Engineering News-Record — "AI-Driven Facade Inspection System Deployed for Historic Building Preservation" (2023)
https://www.enr.com/articles/56644-ai-driven-facade-inspection-system-deployed-for-historic-building-preservation

- T2D2: algorithmic computer vision for facade damage detection, spun out of structural engineer Thornton Tomasetti. Founder/CEO Badri Hiriyur (2020 ENR Newsmaker), COO Jonathan Ehrlich. AI trained on Thornton Tomasetti's past project data.
- Used by Building Conservation Associates Inc. (BCA); COO Beth Romizer-Bailly: "great potential" for cyclical surveys of large historic buildings; drone inspections allow data-gathering visits over years to build deterioration timelines. "Right now we're doing that with binoculars and walking around."
- Learning curve: AI needs different training for ornate geometry (cathedral vs rectilinear tower). Early failure mode: software could not distinguish shadows from prior crack repairs vs actual open cracks.

### 3. NPS — The Secretary of the Interior's Standards for Rehabilitation (36 CFR 67)
https://www.nps.gov/crps/tps/rehab-guidelines/stand.htm

- Standard 3: "Each property shall be recognized as a physical record of its time, place, and use. Changes that create a false sense of historical development, such as adding conjectural features or architectural elements from other buildings, shall not be undertaken."
- Standard 6: "Deteriorated historic features shall be repaired rather than replaced. Where the severity of deterioration requires replacement of a distinctive feature, the new feature shall match the old in design, color, texture, and other visual qualities and, where possible, materials. Replacement of missing features shall be substantiated by documentary, physical, or pictorial evidence."

### 4. National Trust for Historic Preservation window economics + ASC 2014 LCA
- NTHP "Saving Windows, Saving Money": an old wood window combined with a storm window is about 15% more energy efficient than a new replacement window; retrofitted historic windows can meet or exceed replacement efficiency.
- Old House Journal figure (via San Antonio historic window workshop): it can take 240 years to recoup the cost of replacing a single-pane/storm-window combination with a low-e double-pane replacement window. Historic wood window can last 100+ years; a new window may not last 25.
- ASC 50th Annual International Conference (2014), "Life Cycle Assessment of Residential Windows: Saving Energy with Window Restoration" — compares restored wood windows (with/without storm) vs new PVC and aluminum-clad replacements across manufacture, use, maintenance, disposal.

## Original contribution (novel analysis)
Standard 6 permits replacement of missing features only when "substantiated by documentary, physical, or pictorial evidence." Generative AI now lets architects *extrapolate* proportions and patterns from a single photograph — Lippert's team did exactly this. That creates a new category the Standards never contemplated: AI-interpolated evidence, sitting between "substantiated by pictorial evidence" and Standard 3's banned "conjectural features." This article proposes the practical test: (1) surviving physical reference exists (porch columns) → extrapolation is substantiated; (2) photograph shows it but no physical reference (frieze pattern) → substantiated but with stated uncertainty; (3) neither exists and the AI is inventing → Standard 3 bars it; the honest move is Lippert's copper-roof rule, make the new work visibly new. Original arithmetic: the entire belvedere reconstruction rested on ONE pre-1906 photograph; had it never surfaced, the Standards would have barred the reconstruction entirely. 120 years missing, 3 years of forensics, one photo.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Generative image models hallucinate with total confidence. Ask one to "complete" a damaged historic photo and it will invent plausible-looking detail that no 1896 craftsman ever carved. Milled into a real facade, that guess commits precisely the "false history" Standard 3 was written to prevent. The copper roof on the Herzinger belvedere is the case in point: its bell shape came from a Connecticut gazebo Lippert saw on a family visit, not from any Palo Alto evidence, so the team refused to fake the original cedar shingle. If AI replication gets cheap, historic districts could fill with convincing-but-wrong details that future researchers will mistake for the record. Review boards mostly lack the expertise to tell the difference.

## Limitations (honest accounting)
- Based primarily on one project (the Herzinger House). The Lippert team did not publish cost figures, so no cost comparison between AI-assisted replication and traditional millwork research is possible.
- No independent verification of the AI's proportion-extrapolation accuracy exists; the results were validated by matching surviving physical references (porch columns, turret finial), not by third-party measurement.
- The three-question test is the author's framework, not NPS guidance. NPS has issued no opinion on AI-generated detail.
- Window economics figures are from NTHP/OHJ studies of various vintages; actual payback varies with climate, labor rates, and window condition.
