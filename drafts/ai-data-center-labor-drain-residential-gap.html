Marcus Washington August 16, 2026

The AI Industry Needs 499,000 Workers to Build Data Centers. It Found Them on Your Job Site.

Scotty Wristen owns WE Electric in Abilene, Texas. He pays his electricians about $20 an hour. Down the road, OpenAI, Crusoe, and Oracle are building a four-million-square-foot data center called Stargate. Those jobs pay $35 an hour plus overtime and per diem. Wristen cannot compete, and he knows it.

Gene Lantrip has been building homes in Abilene for decades. Since the data center projects started, his builds take two months longer. "That's the downside," he told the Texas Tribune. He said it the way a man says something he has stopped being angry about because being angry did not bring his electricians back.

They are not coming back.

How big the gap is

According to iRecruit, the data center construction industry faces an anticipated shortfall of 499,000 workers for projects that typically take 18 to 30 months to complete. Associated Builders and Contractors puts the broader construction industry shortfall at 349,000 net new workers needed in 2026. More than 80 percent of firms report difficulty finding qualified workers, according to the AGC/Sage 2026 Construction Hiring and Business Outlook Survey, the highest level in three years.

These are not competing numbers. They stack. Data center demand creates a vacuum that sucks workers out of every other construction sector, and residential gets hit the hardest because residential pays the least. IBEW estimates that 45 to 70 percent of an entire data center construction budget goes to electrical subcontractors. When that budget runs into the billions, the electrician who used to wire your kitchen becomes a rounding error the moment he leaves.

HBI calculated the damage: the skilled labor shortage costs homebuilders $10.8 billion a year and prevents the construction of roughly 19,000 homes annually. That was before the current wave of data center spending, which is projected to surpass $52 billion in 2026 alone, according to McKinsey and the Birmingham Group. McKinsey projects nearly $7 trillion flowing into data center construction over the next five years.

Seven trillion. With a T.

Where the workers went

Texas has added more than 2.6 million residents since 2020. It has 300 data centers in operation and roughly 100 more in the pipeline. Texas does not have enough electricians for both priorities, and the priorities are not competing on equal terms. In November 2025, the Wall Street Journal reported that workers in skilled trades tied to data center construction see pay increases of 25 to 30 percent, with some earning more than $225,000 a year. A residential electrician making $41,000 does not need a career counselor to do that math.

An Uptime Institute survey found that 52 percent of construction firms reported staffing shortages causing disruptions, up from 43 percent the year before. Contractors working on data centers report an average backlog of nearly 11 months. Other contractors average about eight months. Lightcast's workforce analysis found that global job postings for construction roles mentioning data centers rose 23 percent in the most recent six-month period and have roughly doubled over the past two years.

Edward Brady, CEO of the Home Builders Institute, a nonprofit that trains people in the building trades, described the dynamic plainly: "If they don't have the [residential work], they're going to leave the residential industry and go to the commercial. Then, residential construction suffers. Those commercial jobs are more stable and, in many cases, better paying." He warned that when housing demand returns, the residential industry will struggle to ramp back up without a larger and more reliable workforce.

In Arizona, homeowners are already seeing labor costs rise 12 to 18 percent. In Abilene, duplex rents jumped $1,000 and average home prices climbed more than 10 percent annually as temporary data center workers flooded the local housing market. Workers building AI infrastructure need somewhere to live, and their presence makes it harder for everyone else to afford housing that is already taking longer to build.

$265 million to train workers who will never wire your house

Meta announced a $115 million program called America's Workforce Academy in June 2026. Five weeks of free training in electrical, welding, plumbing, and fiber-optic installation. Meta covers tuition, airfare, lodging, and a daily stipend. Every graduate gets a guaranteed job.

At a Meta data center construction site.

Not at your house. Not wiring your kitchen or running conduit for your EV charger. At Meta's data center in Richland Parish, Louisiana, which the company has said would be "so large that it would cover a significant part of Manhattan." Or at one of its facilities in Ohio, Indiana, or Texas. Meta called the program "the largest private-sector commitment to the skilled trades with a job guarantee in American history." A previous Meta fiber-technician program received 35,000 applications for 1,000 slots.

Google is spending $50 million over three years to expand apprenticeship programs for electricians and other skilled trades, aiming to increase annual intake from 19,500 to 30,000. BlackRock invested $100 million in Texas for the same purpose. Combined, three entities have committed $265 million to training construction workers.

Every single one of those training dollars is designed to produce workers for data centers. Not one of the three programs trains an electrician and sends her to a residential job site. All three exist because the companies creating the labor shortage need to build their own supply chain for workers the same way they build supply chains for GPUs. Access to skilled electricians, eWeek reported, is "becoming as strategically important as access to Nvidia GPUs."

Here is the part that stings. Meta launched America's Workforce Academy in June 2026. In May 2026, it laid off approximately 8,000 white-collar employees. Meta cut desk workers and hired wrench workers in the same quarter. Its data center in Lebanon, Indiana will create 4,000 construction jobs. When construction finishes, 300 permanent jobs will remain.

What homeowners actually pay

A residential electrician in Texas earns about $20 an hour. A data center electrician on the same stretch of highway earns $35, plus overtime that pushes annual compensation past $80,000 or more. No residential contractor can match that wage because the margin on a $400,000 home does not allow it. Electrical work on a house is a $15,000 line item. Electrical work on a data center is 45 to 70 percent of a multi-billion-dollar budget. Those are different economic universes, and a worker standing between them will walk toward the larger number every time.

Homeowners pay for this gap in three ways. Longer timelines: two extra months in Abilene and climbing elsewhere. Higher costs: subcontractor bids rising 12 to 18 percent in markets near data center construction. Lower quality: when the experienced electricians leave, the ones who remain are stretched thinner and less likely to catch the junction box someone forgot to ground.

Nobody has built the product that addresses this. No AI tool optimizes residential subcontractor scheduling around the gravitational pull of nearby mega-projects. No predictive model forecasts which trades will be hardest to source in which zip codes based on the data center pipeline. No workforce-sharing platform matches idle data center electricians between project phases with residential contractors who need three days of panel work. An industry sophisticated enough to plan $7 trillion in infrastructure spending and model GPU supply chains across continents has built nothing for the residential homeowner competing for the same electrician.

Limitations

Wage gap data comes primarily from Texas, which has the densest concentration of data center construction. Markets without nearby data center projects may not experience the same labor drain, though the national shortage numbers suggest the effect is broadly distributed. iRecruit's 499,000 shortfall figure is a projection, not a count of unfilled positions. HBI's homebuilder cost figure ($10.8 billion annually) predates the current acceleration of data center spending, meaning the actual cost today is likely higher. We could not find independent verification of Meta's claim that its program is "the largest private-sector commitment to the skilled trades with a job guarantee in American history."
