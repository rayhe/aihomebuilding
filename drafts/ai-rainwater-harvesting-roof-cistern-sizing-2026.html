Your Roof Catches 30,000 Gallons a Year. Your Tank Holds 500. That Is the Entire Problem.

By Priya Greenwood · September 17, 2026

Stand outside during the first real storm of the season and watch your gutters. Twenty minutes of hard rain, and a 2,000-square-foot roof sheds more water in that time than most families use in a week, with nearly all of it going straight to the storm drain. According to the Texas Water Development Board's design guide, the collection arithmetic is almost insultingly simple: gallons equals rainfall in inches times roof area in square feet times 0.623, which means one inch of rain on 1,000 square feet of roof is 623 gallons. A 2,000-square-foot roof in San Antonio produces roughly 30,000 gallons of runoff a year, a figure that sounds like a solution until you learn when the water arrives and when the yard wants it. Your roof is already a water utility, and you just let the product leave.

Meanwhile the demand side is enormous, and EPA's WaterSense program reports that the average American family uses more than 300 gallons of water a day at home, with about 30 percent going outdoors, mostly to lawns and landscaping, and rising to 60 percent in arid regions. Their estimate, built on DeOreo et al.'s 2016 end-use study, puts the average home's outdoor use at 50,500 gallons a year. Half of irrigation water is lost to wind, evaporation, and runoff from inefficient systems, which means the country's lawns are drinking from a hose with a hole in it, and nobody has to fix the hose to sell you a tank. So the rough comparison writes itself: thirty thousand gallons in, fifty thousand five hundred out. A well-sized cistern could theoretically cover three-fifths of a home's outdoor water demand, theoretically, although the word is doing a great deal of work here.

Rain Arrives in Storms, but Lawns Want Water in August

Collection is trivial. Storage is the entire engineering problem. Almost nobody sizes it correctly.

Rain arrives in violent, concentrated bursts and then vanishes for months. In San Antonio, May averages about four inches of rain, which means a single wet month dumps roughly 5,000 gallons off that 2,000-square-foot roof, and a single one-inch storm delivers about 1,246 gallons in an afternoon. A typical rain-barrel setup holds 100 to 500 gallons. It fills before the storm ends, spends the rest of the event overflowing, and then watches the lawn die in August while the tank sits there, full of May, because it cannot hold enough May to matter. May arrives anyway. The binding constraint was never collection but timing.

This is where sizing tools earn their keep, and where the industry's gap is frankly embarrassing, because the physics of collection is settled science while the practice of sizing remains guesswork. Among the tools that work is the Department of Energy's free Rainwater Harvesting Calculator, which takes historic precipitation data, roof area, and monthly demand and produces collection curves a homeowner can actually plan from. Toronto's Sustainable Technologies Evaluation Program built an Excel-based design and costing tool verified against real monitoring data, with sizing criteria that include holding a specified number of rainless days in reserve. Demand-matched calculation is the right method. Take the collection curve for your roof, month by month, set it against the irrigation demand curve for your planting, month by month, and size the tank to bridge the worst deficit. What most installers do instead is a rule of thumb that amounts to buying the biggest tank that fits in the side yard, and the tank that fits is almost never the tank the demand curve asks for. One is engineering; the other is hope in a polyethylene cylinder.

Colorado Lets You Keep 110 Gallons, yet One Storm Delivers Eleven Times That

Before you size anything, check whether your state even lets you keep the water, because that legal patchwork is wilder than most homeowners expect, and it changes what is buildable.

Colorado was, until 2016, the only state in the country with an outright ban on residential rainwater collection, a prohibition rooted in prior appropriation doctrine, the first-come-first-served water-rights system where farmers and ranchers hold senior claims. House Bill 16-1005, the compromise that finally passed and is summarized by Colorado State University, allows two 55-gallon barrels per household, 110 gallons total, outdoor use only. Do the math against the collection formula: one inch of rain on a 2,000-square-foot roof yields about 1,246 gallons, which means Colorado's legal allowance covers less than a single afternoon's cloudburst. Colorado's legal limit captures less than one-tenth of a single moderate storm. Adding irony, a Colorado State University Stormwater Center study found rain barrels have no quantifiable effect on the water cycle at all, since the water would have infiltrated or evaporated within a day or two anyway. So the fiercest water-law fight in the West was over a rounding error.

California went the other direction entirely. The Rainwater Capture Act of 2012 (AB-1750) legalized residential collection outright, the California Plumbing Code's Chapter 16 governs nonpotable catchment systems for uses like toilets and irrigation, and SB-558 in 2018 went so far as to exclude rainwater capture systems from new-construction property tax reassessment. Arizona offers a state income tax credit for harvesting systems. DOE's state regulation summary shows most states have no restrictions at all, which makes Colorado's 110 gallons look even more like what it is: a political artifact, not a hydrological one.

The Payback Table Nobody Shows You

Now the honest numbers, because this is where the sales pitches get quiet, and where the spreadsheet every installer should hand you but none will finally gets its hearing.

Take that San Antonio roof: 30,000 gallons a year collected, minus a realistic 15 percent for first-flush diversion, filter losses, and overflow, leaves about 25,500 usable gallons. A gallon's value depends entirely on your water utility's tariff. So here is the sensitivity table instead of a promise. At $6 per CCF, a common Western municipal rate (one CCF is 748 gallons, so roughly eight-tenths of a cent per gallon), those 25,500 gallons offset about $205 a year. At $10 per CCF, about $340 a year. A simple gravity-fed barrel system, the kind the City of Bellingham documented at roughly $2,250 installed with seven 275-gallon tanks, pays back in seven to eleven years on water savings alone at those rates. Respectable, though not exciting.

Scale up, though, and the economics fall apart: a 9,500-gallon corrugated steel tank lists around $9,200 before pump, filtration, trenching, and permits, and a realistic installed system lands in the $12,000 to $15,000 range. The math is brutal: at $340 a year in avoided water bills, that is a thirty-five to forty-four year payback, which is longer than the tank's warranty. Municipal water is absurdly cheap relative to storage. A 5,000-gallon tank costs thousands of dollars and holds about forty to seventy dollars' worth of water, which means you are spending four figures to store what your utility sells you for the price of a pizza. Any installer who leads with bill savings and skips this paragraph is selling you a feeling, not an investment, and you should ask what the number on the tank's price tag buys you in gallons before you sign anything. Ask for the gallon math.

So when does a cistern actually make sense? When the payoff is not the water bill. During drought restrictions, when the city bans lawn watering but your tank is full of May, the value is a living landscape and a resale story, which is a return no utility tariff can price and no payback table captures. Where cities levy stormwater fees, on-site detention can cut the charge. At new construction, when the trenching is already open and the gutters are already going up, the marginal cost of plumbing for collection is a fraction of a retrofit. And for households on wells, where Colorado's own law quietly admits the ban made no sense by allowing unlimited collection, a cistern is backup supply with a real insurance value. Judge a cistern the way you judge a generator: not on payback, but on what it is worth to you when the system it backs up fails, because nobody buys a generator for its return on investment either.

What We Did Not Prove

The 15 percent collection loss is my assumption, applied transparently; real losses range from 10 to 25 percent depending on first-flush design and storm intensity, and no national dataset pins it down. Note that the $6 to $10 per CCF price band is an illustrative range for Western municipal utilities, not any specific utility's tariff, so the payback math is a sensitivity table and should be rerun against your actual bill. Tank prices are equipment list prices from a 2026 vendor catalog, where installed costs vary wildly by site and pump, filtration, trenching, and permits can double the equipment number, while San Antonio's rainfall figures are long-term averages and any given year's collection will differ, sometimes wildly, which is exactly why the tank exists. And no third-party audit of residential cistern payback claims exists anywhere, which is why this article shows the arithmetic instead of citing a vendor's brochure.

The cistern industry will not tell you this: for most homes, the greenest water intervention is not a tank. It is replacing turf with native and regionally appropriate plantings, which the EPA notes need little water beyond normal rainfall once established, plus a WaterSense-labeled irrigation controller, which the agency says can cut a home's irrigation use by up to 30 percent, around 15,000 gallons a year, with no tank, no mosquitoes, no first-flush diverter to unclog, and no freeze protection to maintain. Tanks are large, they are not beautiful, and stagnant water breeds mosquitoes while screens tear and diverters clog, which is the maintenance reality the glossy brochure photographs leave out. In a humid climate with cheap water and no drought risk, rainwater harvesting is a hobby, not an investment, and a native garden beats a cistern on every metric that matters, including cost. Buy the plants first, and then, if you still want the tank, size it against your demand curve like an engineer rather than your side yard like a shopper.
