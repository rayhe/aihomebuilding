A 1906 Earthquake Stole This Victorian's Tower. AI Rebuilt It From a Single Photograph. The Law Decides Which Details Are Real.

By Elena Vasquez · September 14, 2026

There is exactly one photograph of the Herzinger House as it looked before 1906. In it, a round tower climbs the top corner of the three-story Queen Anne on Forest Avenue in Palo Alto, crowned by a bell-shaped roof, a finial, and a weathervane catching the afternoon light, the kind of silhouette that listings call storybook and preservationists call a character-defining feature, which is why its absence went unremarked for so long and its return matters so much. For a hundred and twenty years, that tower existed only inside that image. This spring, it stands on the roofline again.

A belvedere, the tower is called, and the 1906 San Francisco earthquake took it in under a minute, along with the chimney. Owners rebuilt the chimney and left the tower missing through eleven decades, four owners, and a stint as a Menlo College dormitory. Then the current owners, East Coast transplants who grew up around old houses, bought the 1896 home during the pandemic and commissioned the rarest thing in residential construction: a full seismic rehabilitation and a full restoration at the same time. Architect Lee I. Lippert of Lippert & Lippert Design, who had spent his career in the exacting world of Bay Area historic restoration, took the building all the way back to its first form, treating the missing century as a solvable problem rather than a settled loss.

Getting there meant detective work, because permits did not exist when the house went up, so no drawings survive at the Palo Alto Historical Association or the city’s building department. Lippert spent three years on what he calls architectural forensics, peeling paint down to the first coat, pulling wainscoting off a staircase to find green floral wallpaper underneath, uncovering a fan-shaped stained-glass window sealed inside a wall by the chimney, and combing old trade catalogues online late at night. “I haven’t slept in three years,” he told Palo Alto Online.

Rebuilding the belvedere ran through a chain of machines and hands that the 1896 crew would not have recognized. With the single pre-1906 photograph as their guide, the team used generative AI tools to approximate spatial relationships and proportions from the flat image, coaxing three-dimensional measurements out of a two-dimensional record that had been sitting in an archive drawer for over a century. That reading revealed something useful. The tower’s tapered rope columns and Scamozzi capitals matched pieces still intact on the second-story porch. From there the work turned physical: the team stripped paint from the best-preserved originals, laser-scanned them into three-dimensional models, and milled replacements from engineered wood, with the new columns doubling as concealment for modern steel supports.

Friezes do not scan as easily as columns. A ring of curling foliage called the Vitruvian acanthus leaf band had once wrapped the tower’s entablature, and it existed only in the photograph. So the team had the AI generate a scaled CAD drawing of the missing pattern and sent it to artists at the American College of the Building Arts in Charleston, South Carolina, who hand-sculpted molds and cast twenty-five panels, each one a small negotiation between what the photograph showed, what the AI inferred, and what a human hand could actually carve. For the finial, they scaled the surviving finial on the house’s conical turret and found the original had run roughly twenty-five percent larger, with a more elongated profile. Cast in copper and mounted on a steel tube, the new finial doubles as a lightning rod, electrically isolated and grounded at the foundation.

The law enters through the copper roof

Copper was not a period choice, and that is the point. Lippert chose copper partly for fire safety, and partly so the new roof could never be mistaken for the original, which had been cedar shingle. He found the bell shape not in any Palo Alto record but in a gazebo at Olde Mystic Village in Connecticut, spotted during a family visit. Because the shape came from somewhere else, faking the original material would have been, in preservation terms, a lie. “If we can’t replicate something accurately, we’d rather do something clearly different than create confusion about what’s original,” he said.

Translate that sentence into regulation and you get the Secretary of the Interior’s Standards for Rehabilitation, the 36 CFR 67 rules that govern every tax-credit historic project in the country. Standard 3 forbids “conjectural features or architectural elements from other buildings,” anything that creates a false sense of historical development. Standard 6 requires that a replacement match the old in design, color, texture, and where possible materials, and adds the sentence that now matters most: “Replacement of missing features shall be substantiated by documentary, physical, or pictorial evidence.”

Generative AI lives in the crack between those two standards. Lippert’s team had pictorial evidence, exactly one photograph, and used the AI to extrapolate proportions from it, which a reasonable reviewer can read as substantiation. But consider the next project, where no photograph exists and the AI fills the gap with its best guess. That guess is the textbook definition of a conjectural feature. The Standards were written before anyone imagined a machine that could invent a convincing acanthus frieze from nothing, and they have not been revised to say so. What Lippert practiced is the honest framework, and it runs three questions: does a surviving physical reference exist; does a photograph show the detail; if the answer to both is no, make the new work visibly new, and his copper roof is the precedent.

The forgery problem

Now the strongest case against all of this, stated at full strength. Generative image models hallucinate with total confidence. Ask one to complete a damaged historic photo and it will invent detail no 1896 craftsman ever carved, rendered with photographic authority. Milled into a real facade, that invention becomes precisely the false history Standard 3 was written to prevent, and the tooling that makes the forgery possible is getting cheaper every year. Within a few years, historic districts could fill with plausible-but-wrong millwork, cast panels, and friezes that future researchers will photograph, cite, and mistake for the record. Most local review boards have neither the staff nor the expertise to distinguish a laser-scanned original from an AI’s confident fiction. In careless hands, the same technology that rescued the Herzinger belvedere becomes a forgery machine with a user manual.

None of this argues for leaving old houses alone, and the economics punish replacement anyway. The National Trust’s “Saving Windows, Saving Money” analysis found that a historic wood window paired with a storm window performs about fifteen percent better than a new replacement unit, while Old House Journal’s widely cited figure puts the payback period on replacement windows at two hundred and forty years. A maintained wood window can last a century; its replacement may not see twenty-five. For the watching-and-waiting part of preservation, the AI vision system T2D2, spun out of the engineering firm Thornton Tomasetti, now flies drones over facades and compiles years of imagery into deterioration timelines for firms like Building Conservation Associates, replacing the binoculars-and-walkaround survey, a shift its users describe as moving from anecdote to evidence, though the system is still learning to read the difference between a shadow and a crack. Its early failure mode deserves remembering: the software could not tell shadows from old crack repairs apart from open cracks. Machines read surfaces. People read buildings.

What this analysis does not prove

This rests on one project. The Herzinger House team published no cost figures, so no honest comparison between AI-assisted replication and traditional millwork research is possible here. No independent party has verified the AI’s proportion extrapolations; they were checked against surviving physical references, not third-party measurement. Note that the three-question framework is this article’s proposal, not National Park Service guidance, and NPS has issued no opinion on AI-generated detail. Window economics figures come from studies of various vintages, and real payback varies with climate, labor rates, and the condition of the sash.

What to do Monday morning

If you own a historic home, photograph everything now, in high resolution, inside and out, because Standard 6 makes your future replication rights depend on pictorial evidence you possess today. When a window contractor tells you replacement pays for itself, ask for the math; the 240-year figure is the number to beat. If your architect proposes an AI-reconstructed detail, run the three questions: is there a surviving physical reference, is there a photograph, and if neither exists, will the new work be clearly differentiated? For cyclical surveys of a large or hard-to-access facade, price a drone-based AI inspection before the next hands-on survey; catching deterioration in imagery is cheaper than catching it in scaffolding. And remember what the copper roof proved: the most honest thing a new material can do on an old building is admit it is new.
