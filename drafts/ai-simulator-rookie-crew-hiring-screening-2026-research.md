# Research: AI Simulators vs. the Rookie Ratio — Screening Trades Hires Before They Touch a Wire

**Article #773 | Journalist: Marcus Washington | Date: 2026-09-04**

## Working headline
"One Third of the Crew Building Your House Has Been There Less Than a Year. A 20-Minute Simulator Test Is the Only Interview That Works."

Alt: "Your Builder Hires 119 Workers to Keep 100. The Simulator Grades Them in 20 Minutes."

## Thesis
Construction's labor crisis isn't just a hiring problem, it's a first-year problem. Bridgit's 2026 benchmark: 36.4% of a project team has less than one year of tenure (56% on teams of 51+). Travelers: first-year construction workers account for 47% of injuries and 51% of comp costs. AI/VR skills simulators (Interplay Learning, SkillCat lineage) are becoming the cheapest pre-hire screen in the industry — a 20-minute assessment replacing the 90-day probation as the place where bad hires get caught.

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes. For GCs and subs: the math on bad-hire cost vs. simulator pre-screening is an operational decision they can act on this month. For homeowners: ask your GC how they vet new crew members — crew tenure mix is now a legitimate quality question, same as insurance certificates.

## Original contribution (nobody calculated this)

**The rookie injury-risk multiplier:** If first-year workers are 36.4% of the team (Bridgit rookie ratio) but drive 47% of construction injuries (Travelers), each rookie's per-capita injury risk is 0.47/0.364 = 1.29x vs. 0.53/0.636 = 0.83x for veterans. Ratio: rookies are ~1.55x as likely to be injured, per worker, as everyone else. Nobody has put these two datasets side by side.

**The hiring-treadmill cost for a 20-person residential crew:** median attrition 18.7% means 3.7 turnovers/year on a 20-person crew. Replacing a low-wage hourly worker costs ~16% of annual pay (Center for American Progress); at $20/hr ($41,600/yr) that's $6,656 per churned worker, or ~$24,600/year just to refill the same seats — before rework, callbacks, or injury exposure. For a skilled tradesperson at $30/hr, it's $9,984 each.

**The 90-day probation vs. 20-minute screen:** a bad hire discovered after 90 days costs ~90 days × 8 hrs × $22/hr ≈ $15,840 in wages alone, plus supervised rework and the comp exposure of an unvetted rookie. A simulator assessment takes 20 minutes and a per-seat training subscription (~$100-300/worker/year). The economic case for screening at the door is a 60-to-1 wage ratio before you count injuries.

## Actionable takeaways (required)
- **GCs/subs:** add a 20-minute simulator assessment to onboarding before a new hire touches tools unsupervised; write apprentice training/assessment records into subcontractor prequalification packets (same drawer as insurance certs).
- **Hiring managers:** use Bridgit's rookie ratio as a bid-review metric — ask subs what their rookie ratio is, not just their headcount.
- **Homeowners:** ask your GC who trained the crew and how. A GC who can answer is managing rookie risk; one who can't is hoping.
- **Workers:** simulator scores are portable credentials — they travel better than a reference letter.

## The skepticism (Marcus voice — strongest counterargument at full strength)
- Interplay's numbers are vendor-published, not independently audited. "7 in 10 ramp faster" is the company's own survey of its own customers.
- A simulator score is a gatekeeper, and gatekeepers discriminate. A 20-year hand who tests badly on a screen still wires a house better than a kid who tests well. Hiring by score risks locking out the exact experienced-but-digitally-awkward workers the industry is losing to retirement.
- The rookie ratio is a tenure metric, not a skill metric — simulator pre-screening doesn't fix why people quit in years 1-4 (commute, manager fit, pay), which Bridgit flags as the actual retention drivers.
- DEWALT's own study undercuts the hype: only 8% use AI daily. Most of this is interest, not practice.
- Union apprenticeship programs already assess and test through standardized curricula; a simulator adds marginal value where training is already structured.

## Limitations
- The 1.55x rookie injury multiplier combines two different datasets (Bridgit's GC-leaning benchmark, Travelers' claims data) — same-year but different samples; treat as an estimate, not a measured rate.
- Turnover replacement cost (CAP 16%) is all-industry for low-wage hourly workers, not construction-specific; actual construction costs run higher when rework and callbacks are included.
- Interplay per-seat pricing is not public; the ~$100-300 range is an estimate from public training-product pricing, not a quoted figure.
- No independent study links simulator scores to field rework rates on residential projects specifically.
