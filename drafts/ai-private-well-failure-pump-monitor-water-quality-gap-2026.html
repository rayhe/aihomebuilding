Your Well Pump Dies Without Warning. A $212 Controller Reads Its Pulse.

By Jake Kowalski · September 22, 2026

The call comes on a Friday night. Water pressure fades halfway through a shower, then nothing, just air hissing out of the tap. Your well guy cannot get there until Monday, and you learn, standing in a cold bathroom, that your house has exactly one moving part that matters and it just quit without so much as a goodbye note.

That moving part lives a couple hundred feet underground where you will never see it, never hear it, and never think about it until the day it strands you. A submersible well pump runs eight to fifteen years, then dies. No warning light, no dashboard, no gentle decline you can plan around. One day it pumps, the next day you own a very expensive hole in the ground.

City people cannot relate to this, because their water shows up courtesy of a utility with engineers and SCADA systems and redundancy keeping it showing up. Your water shows up because of one sealed motor the size of a fence post, hanging on a wire in the dark, and nobody is watching it. That is about to change, and the fix is cheaper than the service call you are going to make anyway.

Nobody is in charge of your well

Start with the scale of the blind spot. Forty-three million Americans drink groundwater from private wells, according to the U.S. Geological Survey (USGS Circular 1360). Federal drinking-water rules under the Safe Drinking Water Act do not apply to any of them, not loosely and not with exceptions. The USGS states it plainly on its domestic-wells page: the federal regulations for public supplies are "not directly applicable to the regulation of domestic wells" (USGS). You are the water utility, so congratulations on the promotion nobody told you about.

Your water is not automatically fine, either. USGS scientists sampled about 2,100 domestic wells across 48 states and found roughly one in five contained at least one contaminant above a human-health benchmark. Mostly natural stuff: arsenic, radon, uranium, manganese leaching out of the rock itself. Nitrate from fertilizer and septic systems crossed the federal drinking-water standard in about four percent of wells. Bacteria showed up in roughly a third of the wells tested for microbes. A later USGS round found at least one PFAS compound, the forever chemicals, in twenty percent of private wells sampled across sixteen eastern states (EcoWatch / Environmental Science & Technology).

Here is the part that should embarrass everybody: almost nobody checks. Researchers at Iowa State, Cornell, and UMass found that roughly 23 million U.S. households depend on private wells, that at best half test with any frequency, and that very few test yearly the way public-health officials recommend. Even Iowa, which hands out free testing money, watches the funds go unspent (phys.org). The EPA is still leaning on a fifteen-year-old study for its national picture. Your well water is a regulated utility's problem exactly nowhere.

~3.4 million households plausibly drinking water above a health benchmark without knowing it. Run the math: 23M well households x 20% over benchmark x ~75% not testing yearly. Back-of-envelope, assumptions stated, but the inputs are all sourced.

Multiply it out yourself and the number lands around three and a half million households. That assumes three-quarters of well owners skip annual testing, which is conservative against "very few test once or more yearly." This is not a peer-reviewed estimate. It is arithmetic with honest inputs, and the honest inputs are damning enough.

The pump has a heartbeat, and it is electrical

Now the machinery, because this is where it gets fun. A submersible pump cannot talk, but it has a pulse: its current draw. Bearings wearing out pull more amps, a waterlogged pressure tank makes the pump short-cycle, and a dropping water level forces a dry run that destroys the motor fast. Every one of these failure modes shows up in the electrical signature before it shows up in your shower.

Smart well controllers read that signature every second. Take the DROP Pump Controller, a real product shipping today: it monitors pump current continuously, carries electronic overload protection, detects run-dry conditions from pressure plus motor current, and sends a short-cycle notification that usually means your pressure tank is failing. It logs water usage, watches for leaks, and pushes alerts to your phone (DROP cut sheet). The short-cycle alert is the sleeper feature, because a dead pressure tank is a three-hundred-dollar part that murders a two-thousand-dollar pump, and the controller catches the murder in progress.

This is not exotic hardware anymore, either: Wi-Fi smart well-pump control boxes list around two hundred dollars, and a storage-tank controller with pump protection runs about five hundred (eBay: SWC Well Master Pro, $211.99; eBay: well storage tank controller, $500). Compare that against the failure it prevents: pump replacement averages about $1,897, with installation labor running $900 to $2,500 on top, and constant-pressure systems reaching $2,000 to $5,000 for the hardware alone (PlumbingSupplyAndMore). Add the emergency-weekend premium and two days of bottled water and hotel showers, and the controller pays for itself the first time it turns a surprise into a scheduled appointment.

The math a rural buyer should do before closing

Buyers obsess over the roof and the foundation and treat the well as a checkbox. Flip that. Ask for the driller's log: depth, yield in gallons per minute, date drilled. That single page tells you whether you are buying a ten-year-old system with life left or a thirty-year-old system on borrowed time. No paperwork on a twenty-year-old house means you budget for a pump replacement starting now, because statistically you are already inside the failure window.

Notice the asymmetry: a full lab panel costs a few hundred dollars, while a new well costs as much as a used car. Yet the standard rural purchase contract treats the well like a light switch: flip it, water comes out, good enough. A buyer's inspection contingency should name the well explicitly, with a lab panel and a documented flow test, the same way it names the roof. Two hundred dollars of testing protects a fifteen-thousand-dollar asset, and almost nobody does this. Everybody should.

Why a controller is not enough

Time for the cold water, and I mean that literally. A pump monitor watches the machinery, not the water. No two-hundred-dollar controller detects arsenic, nitrate, or PFAS; only a lab does that. There is a real danger here that the gadget gives you a green checkmark in an app while the actual one-in-five problem, the chemistry, goes unmeasured for another decade. A monitored pump delivering contaminated water is a well-instrumented failure.

Connectivity is the second hole: app-based alerts need internet, and wells live precisely where broadband does not. A push notification about your dying pump is useless if the pump house has one bar of signal and the alert arrives after the motor has already cooked itself. Some controllers handle this with local shutoff logic that does not need the cloud, which is the right architecture, but plenty of the cheap ones are cloud-dependent toys.

Third, no device negotiates with geology, as USGS documents declining water levels in stressed basins across the West, and a well going dry from aquifer depletion is not a pump problem. It is a dig-deeper-or-drill-new problem, at thirty to sixty-five dollars a foot. The controller will faithfully report the run-dry condition while your aquifer keeps dropping, which is useful information but not a solution.

So buy the controller for what it is: a way to convert a $1,897 emergency into a scheduled $1,897 appointment, plus early warning on the pressure tank that kills pumps. Do not buy it as a substitute for testing the water. The pump is the machine and the water is the product: monitor the machine, lab-test the product, and do not confuse the two.

What to do on Monday

If you are buying rural, make the well an inspection contingency in writing. Demand the driller's log and order a full lab panel: bacteria, nitrate, arsenic, lead, and PFAS if you are in a known area, for one hundred to six hundred fifty dollars. Non-negotiable.

If you already own a well, put a monitoring controller on the pump. When the short-cycle alert fires, check the pressure tank first. That three-hundred-dollar tank is the fuse; the two-thousand-dollar pump is what the fuse protects.

Find your pump's birthday: check the well log, the installer's sticker on the pressure tank, or the control box. No paperwork means you start budgeting for replacement today, not the day the shower goes cold.

Test the water yearly for bacteria and nitrate, and run the full panel every three to five years. Retest after floods, nearby construction, or any change in taste, odor, or color. The USGS found contamination follows geology and land use, which means your neighbor's clean test says nothing about your well.

And if you are building on a rural lot, spec the monitoring controller during construction, when the electrician is already there. It is the cheapest insurance on the most failure-critical appliance in the house, and it costs less than the service call you will otherwise make on a Friday night.

What this analysis cannot tell you

The one-in-five contamination figure comes from USGS sampling between 1991 and 2004, published as DeSimone 2009. It is the best national picture available, which is itself an indictment, but it is not current. The PFAS finding covered sixteen eastern states and five aquifer systems, not the country.

Pump lifespan and replacement-cost figures come from industry aggregators and plumbing-cost sites, not a national survey of well owners. Your geology dominates everything: drilling through granite can double or triple the per-foot cost versus sand, and the national averages smooth over that completely.

The DROP controller's features are manufacturer-published, and I found no independent test of its failure-prediction accuracy. Treat vendor claims as a starting point for your own due diligence, the same way you would treat a pump curve.

The 3.4-million-household estimate is back-of-envelope arithmetic, not research. It multiplies a sourced testing-behavior finding by a sourced contamination rate with an assumed non-testing share. Every assumption is stated so you can argue with them. Argue away.
