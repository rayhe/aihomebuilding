Your Lighting Plan Measures Everything Except the Light Your Body Reads

By Elena Vasquez · October 7, 2026

The lighting plan arrives as a beautiful PDF. Forty-seven recessed cans, a pendant schedule, dimmer zones named in a font that costs money. Foot-candles on the countertop, color temperature on every fixture, a reflected ceiling plan drawn with genuine care. Not one number on it measures the light your body actually reads.

That number exists, with a name, an international standard, and published targets, yet your lighting designer has never once put it on a plan. I ran the math for a typical new-build home this week, and the result is a double failure so clean it looks intentional: too little of the right light by day, too much by night, both failures baked into the design before a single can was cut into the ceiling.

The Metric on Nobody's Plan

Your eye has five kinds of photoreceptors, and only three of them are for seeing. Two more, the intrinsically photosensitive retinal ganglion cells, exist for one job: telling your brain what time it is. They are most sensitive to short-wavelength light around 480 nanometers, the blue edge of daylight, and the International Commission on Illumination gave this sensitivity a formal quantity in CIE S 026: melanopic equivalent daylight illuminance, or mEDI.

Think of mEDI as lux for your circadian system: photopic lux measures the light you see by, while melanopic EDI measures the light your biology runs on, and no residential lighting plan in the country specifies the second quantity.

Also wrong is the plane: lighting plans measure horizontal illuminance on the work surface, the countertop and the desk, while mEDI is defined on the vertical plane at eye level, roughly 1.2 meters when seated, because that is where light actually enters the eye. A downlight blasting 400 lux onto your cutting board may deliver barely a third of that to your retina. Plan and biology are not even measuring the same geometry.

Three Numbers, Published and Ignored

In 2022, a workshop of lighting scientists, photometrists, and sleep researchers published the closest thing the field has to a consensus. Brown and colleagues, in PLOS Biology, recommended a minimum of 250 lux melanopic EDI at the eye during the daytime, a maximum of 10 melanopic EDI in the three hours before bed, and 1 lux maximum in the sleep environment.

Two hundred fifty, ten, one: clean numbers, published and ignored. WELL adopted them into its L03 Circadian Lighting Design feature, awarding points at 150 and 275 equivalent melanopic lux. Feasibility guidance exists too: the Illuminating Engineering Society has published its office-focused study of circadian lighting trade-offs. And in residential architecture, where people spend their evenings and raise their children, the targets are known to everyone and specified by no one.

The Worksheet: Your House, Failing Twice

Here is the original arithmetic, the kind a lighting designer could run in ninety seconds and apparently never does: melanopic EDI equals vertical-plane photopic lux multiplied by the lamp's melanopic daylight efficacy ratio, the MDER. MDER is roughly 0.44 for a 2700K warm LED, 0.59 at 3000K, 0.82 at 4000K, and 0.97 at 5000K, using Signify's published BioUp spectral data as industry-typical values. For a normal recessed-downlight layout, vertical illuminance at the eye runs about one third of the horizontal illuminance on the work plane. That is the whole model.

Apply it to a representative new-build, with no daylight contribution, which is the honest case for interior rooms and winter evenings:

Read that again, starting with the daytime kitchen, the room where the lighting design is most generous, which delivers barely two fifths of the daytime biological minimum. Move to the evening living room and the failure flips: more than twice the evening maximum. Both directions fail at once, and neither failure appears anywhere in the documentation, because the documentation measures lux on countertops.

The arithmetic turns punishing when you ask what fixing the daytime side would actually take, because the warm spectrum of residential light works against the target at every step. At 2700K, reaching 250 mEDI at the eye requires roughly 570 lux of vertical illuminance, an absurd number for downlights in a living room. At 5000K it takes about 258 lux vertical, which is reachable but means living under light the color of a parking garage. Without daylight or spectrally boosted fixtures, the daytime target is effectively unreachable in a normal living room. That failure is not in the execution. It is in the physics of the specification.

Measured Reality Agrees

This is not just a model. Cain and colleagues put wearable spectrophotometers on people in real homes (Scientific Reports, 2020) and found median melanopic illuminance of 11.0 lux at 10 pm, already above the 10-lux recommendation, with a range up to 49.6. Nearly half of the homes were bright enough to suppress melatonin by at least 50 percent. My worksheet's evening estimate of 22 lands squarely inside their measured range, which is the kind of agreement between model and field data that makes me trust the worksheet's daytime number too.

Their other finding should unsettle anyone who specifies lighting for a living, because individual sensitivity to evening light varied more than fiftyfold across the study's participants, which turns every fixed number on a plan into a population average that describes a room's actual occupants only approximately. One living room can suppress a sleeper's melatonin by 80 percent while doing nothing measurable to the person reading beside them. A single number on a plan pretends at a precision the biology refuses to support, and any honest circadian design has to admit that the target is a population guideline, not a personal prescription.

There is also a cheap partial fix the data supports. A 2025 Scientific Reports analysis of 52 modern lamps found that fixed warm-CCT LEDs cut the melatonin suppression value 3.4-fold compared with cool LEDs, and that tunable lamps shifting warm in the evening matched or beat fixed-warm lamps. At least the evening problem has a product answer that does not require redesigning the house, and it is almost embarrassingly simple next to the daytime physics: change the spectrum, dim the output, and let the existing fixtures do work they were already doing.

The Tools That Could Do This Before Rough-In

Here is the part that makes the omission inexcusable. Computing mEDI across a floor plan is a simulation problem, and lighting simulation is a solved industry with decades of validated ray-tracing behind it, which is precisely why the omission of circadian metrics from residential plans reads as professional habit rather than technical impossibility. Radiance has done spectral ray-tracing for decades. Solemma's Alfa and Lark plugins bring circadian and spectral lighting simulation directly into Rhino and Grasshopper, which means an architect can model melanopic illuminance across a floor plan before the electrician's bid is finalized. A free toolchain exists for exactly this computation, and the profession simply does not ask the question. Nobody asks.

On the product side, the algorithmic layer is arriving. BrainLit's BioCentric system builds its light schedules from a chronobiological simulation model rather than a wall switch, with luminaires tuned to deliver DER above 1.0, more circadian punch per lumen than daylight itself, though that is a vendor figure and should be read as one. Lutron's Ketra fixtures, at roughly $1,000 per room with bulbs north of $100, re-measure their own output hundreds of times per second to hold a programmed solar cycle. Handheld meters like the In. Licht Pro now measure EML and mEDI in the field and export WELL-aligned reports, so a designer's model can be checked against the built room in an afternoon.

None of this is exotic; it is the normal trajectory of every building system, which moves from measurement to model to specification to verification as the underlying science matures. Lighting is just running twenty years behind HVAC, because nobody ever sued over melatonin.

Why the Skeptics Have a Point

The counterargument deserves to be stated at full strength, because it is genuinely strong and the thesis does not survive contact with it unless every objection gets an honest answer. The Brown et al. targets are a workshop consensus, not a standard. No ANSI, ASHRAE, or IECC residential provision references mEDI, and designing a $40,000 lighting package to an unadopted metric is a defensible way to spend someone else's money only if you say so up front.

Office-focused IES feasibility work found the constraints often cannot be satisfied at the same time: push vertical melanopic illuminance up and you collide with glare limits and energy codes, while spectral tuning trades against luminous efficacy. In a home, the glare math is friendlier but the energy code math is not, and Title 24 does not care about your circadian rhythm.

Then there is daylight, which is the entire argument for not overthinking the electric system, and the honest version of this article admits that a well-placed window makes every fixture schedule in the house look like a rounding error. Brown et al. explicitly say to use daylight first. A well-fenestrated room on a bright day delivers thousands of lux at the eye, and no electric lighting plan competes with a window. My worksheet's daytime failure is a worst case: interior rooms, north-facing lots, Seattle in January. For the daylit half of the house, the fix is glass, not fixtures.

And the evening fix is, bluntly, behavioral. Dimming to 10 mEDI is a thirty-dollar smart-bulb schedule, not a construction story. Warm, dim, and fewer fixtures after dinner. Here architecture matters less than habit, and any article that launders a controls problem into a $40,000 lighting package is selling something.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The worksheet uses the one-third rule of thumb for vertical from horizontal illuminance, not a ray-traced simulation, and real rooms will vary by half in either direction with layout, surface reflectance, and furniture. MDER values are typical for phosphor-converted LEDs and differ by manufacturer and product line. Daylight is excluded from the model, which means the daytime failure is overstated for daylit rooms and accurate for interior ones. No measurements were taken in a real home for this article; the evening figures are cross-checked against Cain et al.'s field data, while the daytime figures are modeled only. Vendor performance figures from BrainLit, Ketra, and Lucibel are vendor figures, and no independent residential field trial of an algorithmic circadian lighting system was found. A fiftyfold individual sensitivity range means the targets describe populations, not people, and the article's numbers should be read as design guidance, not medical advice.

What to Do Monday Morning

If you are building or renovating, put one new line in the lighting designer's scope: vertical-plane mEDI per room, day and evening scenes, computed before rough-in. Radiance or the Alfa/Lark plugins are the free path, and any lighting designer under forty already has the tools installed; what they lack is not software but the instruction, which amounts to a single added line in the scope of work. Ask for the daytime number against 250 and the evening number against 10, and watch how fast the conversation changes when the plan has to answer to biology.

Verify after installation. A handheld EML meter turns a design claim into a measurement in one site visit, and the rooms that fail are usually fixable without new fixtures: raise daytime levels near the eye with wall-washing instead of downlights, which buys vertical lux without glare, and cut evening levels with dimming schedules that actually run.

For the evening, do the cheap thing first. Warm lamps, dimmed hard, fewer of them on. Tunable fixtures that shift warm at night are the productized version of the same idea, and the 2025 lamp data says they work. Nobody needs a four-figure-per-room system to get under 10 mEDI; they need the discipline to dim, which is the least glamorous sentence in this article and also the most useful one.

Architecture is the shaping of light. We have just been shaping the wrong quantity, in the wrong plane, for the wrong century of science. Both the numbers and the simulation are already public. Put them on the plan.
