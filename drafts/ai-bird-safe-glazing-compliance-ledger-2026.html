46% of Bird-Window Deaths Happen at Houses. The Fix Costs $24 and Fits in an Envelope.

By Frank DeLuca • October 6, 2026

A homeowner handed me a dead bird exactly once. It was a varied thrush, beautiful even in the unglamorous way of dead things, and she had found it under the corner window of the house we had finished six weeks earlier. That window was the centerpiece of the whole project: two walls of glass meeting at a mitered corner, no post, trees on the other side doing exactly what trees do, which is reflect themselves convincingly in large panes of glass. She held the bird out the way you hold something you expect a professional to have an answer for. I did not have an answer. I had a schedule, a punch list with eleven items on it, and a warranty binder. None of them covered this.

That was years ago. What I did not know then, and what the industry is only now being forced to learn, is that her house was not an edge case. It was the median. According to the American Bird Conservancy, more than a billion birds die on American glass every year, and its own guide puts 46 percent of those deaths at homes. Not skyscrapers. Houses. Your slider, your corner window, your glass railing. Towers get the press conferences; houses do nearly half the killing, quietly, one thrush at a time, mostly during migration, mostly unwitnessed.

And here is the part that should interest anyone running a job: the rulebook has been filling in while we were looking the other way. San Francisco started in 2011. New York made it real law in 2021. Madison, Wisconsin survived a court challenge. Washington, DC turned its law on in October 2024. A New York City bill would extend the requirements to existing multifamily buildings by 2030. This is no longer a wildlife issue that lives in someone else's department. It is a glazing spec, a plan-check comment, and a line item, and like every line item I have ever met, it is cheapest when you plan for it and most expensive when you discover it.

What the bird sees

Understanding the requirement starts with understanding the failure, and the failure is optical. A bird does not see glass. It sees two things that look like safe passage: the reflection of sky and vegetation on the surface, or the actual sky and vegetation visible straight through two parallel panes, the corner window, the glass railing with the yard beyond it. It flies toward open space at full speed. Glass is invisible at exactly the moment visibility matters most.

Twenty years of tunnel testing, most of it associated with the researcher Daniel Klem, established the geometry of the fix. Birds will not attempt to fly through a gap they perceive as too small, and the threshold that emerged is disarmingly specific: pattern elements spaced no more than 2 inches apart horizontally and 4 inches apart vertically. This is the 2x4 rule, and it is not a suggestion. It is written into the San Francisco standards, the California state building rulemaking, and the New York guidance documents. Vertical elements at least a quarter inch wide, horizontals at least an eighth, and the pattern has to sit on the outside surface of the glass, because reflections happen on the outside and a marker on the inside is a marker behind a mirror.

San Francisco, first in the country with this in 2011, aimed its rule with surprising precision at residential work: in residential districts, a building under 45 feet tall with a facade more than half glass must treat 95 percent of its large glazed segments, meaning panes 24 square feet and up. Read that twice. That is not a downtown tower ordinance. That is the modern farmhouse with the glass gable, the hillside house with the view wall, the ADU with the full-light slider facing the garden. New York's Local Law 15 went broader and blunter: bird-friendly materials on 90 percent of facades up to 75 feet, and every glass railing at any height, for new construction and major glass replacements. Your client's glass deck railing, the one the architect drew because it disappears, is a named hazard in the code now.

The products, priced honestly

Once you accept that the pattern is the product, the market sorts itself into four tiers, and I have now priced all of them the way I price everything: per square foot, installed, with the gotchas attached.

Tier one is fritted glass: ceramic dots or lines baked onto the surface at the factory, the same frit you already see as the black band around a windshield, just patterned across the whole lite. It is the cheapest compliant glass you can buy, it meets the 2x4 rule by construction, and architects hate it, because clients who paid for a view wall do not want to look at it through a dot matrix. Expect it to survive value engineering exactly as long as the first budget meeting.

Tier two is the UV glass, and this is where the technology gets clever. Birds see ultraviolet light; humans mostly do not. Arnold Glas, a German manufacturer, bakes a UV-reflective pattern into the glass that reads to a bird as a spiderweb of lines and to a homeowner as essentially clear glass. Price has always been the catch: the company's own marketing puts Ornilux at two to two and a half times the cost of standard low-E insulated glass per square foot, though only a few dollars more than fritted. This year Guardian Glass launched its Bird1st UV with a Threat Factor of 25, which matters because New York's law effectively demands 25 or better, and it ships in jumbo and super-jumbo sheets specifically so fabricators get better cutting yields and the installed cost comes down. Direction of travel is clear: the invisible option is getting cheaper.

Tier three is film, the retrofit answer. Perforated or patterned films applied to the exterior surface run two to three dollars a square foot, and the established products claim collision reductions in the 60 to 70 percent range. This is the product category that matters most for the existing building stock, because nobody is reglazing a whole apartment building for the birds, but a film crew can wrap one.

Tier four is the envelope on your desk. Feather Friendly sells a do-it-yourself marker tape, quarter-inch dots on the 2x4 spacing, at about twenty-four Canadian dollars for a roll that covers sixteen square feet, with an eight-year rated life. WindowAlert sells UV-reflective decals for eight to twelve dollars a pack. In the average house, the two deadliest windows, the patio slider and the corner lite, can be treated for less than sixty dollars on a Saturday morning. I have managed change orders for thousands of dollars that bought less risk reduction than that roll of tape.

Compliance is a rounding error at design stage and a change order at plan check. The pattern costs almost nothing when it is drawn in. It costs real money when it arrives as a correction notice.

The ledger

Here is the arithmetic I wish someone had shown me before that homeowner handed me the thrush. Take a 2,400 square foot house with roughly 380 square feet of glazing, a normal number for a house with ambitions. Spec the bird-safe pattern into the glass at design development, and the premium over standard low-E runs on the order of fifteen hundred to thirty-five hundred dollars, depending on whether you choose frit or UV and how your glazier buys. That is a line item. It fits in a budget. It does not delay anything, because the glass was being ordered anyway.

Discover the requirement at plan check, in a jurisdiction that has one, and the same glass becomes a change order: resubmittal, revised shop drawings, a glazing package repriced under duress, and two to four weeks you did not have. I have watched smaller corrections eat bigger holes in a schedule. The glass does not get cheaper when you are embarrassed about it.

And then there is the cliff. New York's Int 1073, introduced in 2024, would require existing commercial and multifamily residential buildings to meet the bird-friendly standard by January 1, 2030, and it tightens the renovation trigger from replacing all exterior glazing to replacing any of it. Detached one- and two-family homes are exempt, but every multifamily owner in the five boroughs should be reading this the way I read a weather report: it was filed at end of session and needs reintroduction to advance, so it is not law, but the direction is unmistakable, and 2030 is one capital planning cycle away. For a multifamily retrofit, the film math is the maintenance-budget answer at two to three dollars a square foot, and full glass replacement is the capital-plan answer at an order of magnitude more. Price both now, while the choice is still yours.

Where the machines come in

Twenty years into this problem, the pattern design itself is becoming algorithmic, which is the part of the story that belongs in this publication. A published patent application describes a computer-implemented method for generating bird-deterrent facade patterns: pseudo-random, non-repeating arrangements of UV-reflective elements, with the position, rotation, and size of each element set by design rules executed in software, then applied to the glass in manufacturing. Read that plainly and it says the frit pattern of the future is not drawn by a designer placing dots. It is computed.

In Zurich, researchers at ETH and CSEM have prototyped BirdGuard, a solar-powered window sticker that runs bird detection and trajectory forecasting on a tiny machine-learning chip, no battery, no wiring, and triggers a deterrent when it predicts an imminent collision. It is a student-scale research project, not a product, and I would not spec it on a job tomorrow. But the shape of it is right: detection, prediction, deterrence, computed at the glass.

Research literature points one step further out. Recent reviews of the North American work describe predictive collision models that integrate weather-surveillance radar data, the same radar networks that track migration intensity, with urban landscape data to forecast where strikes will concentrate. A builder who knew, before breaking ground, that a particular facade orientation on a particular lot sat under a heavy spring flyway would spec the glass differently. That forecast does not exist as a product yet. But every input to it already exists as data.

What does not exist, and what should, is the boring middle: a facade collision-risk score inside the design tools. We have energy models that score a wall for heat loss in seconds. We have daylight models that score a room for glare before the slab is poured. Nobody's software looks at a rendering of a glass corner facing a stand of oaks and says: this will kill birds, here is the Threat Factor of the specified glass, here is the 2x4 check, here is the jurisdiction's rule. Science is settled, products are rated, codes are written. That check is a spreadsheet. Somebody will build it, and the builders who run it at design development will never meet the version of the correction notice that the rest of us get at plan check.

The skeptic's corner, fully stocked

Now the other side, because I have been doing this long enough to distrust any story that only has one side. Enforcement is thin. Four or five jurisdictions with real teeth, a federal bill that goes nowhere, and the New York existing-buildings bill stalled at end of session. Most of the country can still glaze a glass box facing a migratory flyway and answer to nobody. UV glass still costs double, and I have sat in the meetings where the frit gets value-engineered out; the aesthetic tax is real even when the dollar tax is small, because nobody frames the view they paid for through a dot pattern without resenting it.

Mostly, the AI angle is promise. BirdGuard is a prototype. A pattern patent is intellectual property, not a product on a truck. No shipping design tool computes a facade risk score, and the radar-driven forecasts live in papers, not in permit sets. And the scale context deserves honesty: domestic cats kill an estimated 2.4 billion birds a year in the United States, more than double the window toll. A builder who specs bird-safe glass while the client's three outdoor cats patrol the yard is, strictly speaking, optimizing the smaller problem. I still think you spec the glass. I just think you should know the size of the thing you are solving.

Finally, the grimmest number in this whole story: of the birds that are found after a strike and taken to rehabilitators, only about 40 percent survive. Injuries are internal, the skull is fractured, the bird flies off and dies somewhere you will never see it. Rescue is not the strategy. Glass is the strategy, because the glass is the only part of this you can decide in advance, on paper, with a pencil, before anything is flying.

What this calculation does not prove

The fifteen-hundred-to-thirty-five-hundred-dollar premium is my arithmetic from industry price quotes applied to a typical glazing area, not a bid tab; real numbers move with region, fabricator, and insulated-unit spec. Collision-reduction percentages attached to the brand-name products are manufacturer claims, and while the underlying pattern science is well tested, not every marketing number has an independent tunnel test behind it. That 2030 New York deadline is contingent on a bill that must be reintroduced. That 46-percent figure is the Conservancy's modeled estimate, not a census. Treat the ledger as an order of magnitude with a clear sign, the way you would treat any early estimate: directionally right, precisely wrong, and useful anyway.

The questions worth asking

If you are building in San Francisco, New York, Madison, or DC, get the bird-safe glazing into the design-development set, not the plan-check response. Ask your glazing sub for the ABC Threat Factor of the proposed glass: 30 or under is the Conservancy's definition of bird-friendly, 25 or under is what New York wants. If the sub cannot answer, they have not priced compliance, and you are about to.

If you own a home, walk outside and look at your glass the way a bird does. Walk outside and look at your glass the way a bird does. The patio slider that reflects the garden and the corner window with the view straight through are the two panes that do the killing. One roll of marker tape, sixteen square feet of coverage, exterior surface, Saturday morning. Sixty dollars and the odds change.

If you own multifamily in New York, price the film retrofit and the glass replacement now, while 2030 is a planning horizon and not a deadline. And if you draw buildings for a living, design the pattern in. No compliance path is cheaper than the 2x4 rule: two inches, four inches, drawn once, done forever. Birds cannot see your glass. Make sure they can see your dots.
