Modular Homes Cost 25% Less to Build. Your Construction Loan Was Built to Stop You From Using One.

By Catherine Chen · September 11, 2026

The invoice arrives before the house does. That is the whole problem, stated in one sentence.

Order a modular home and the factory will ask for most of its money up front: materials, labor, the assembly line time, all paid before your house travels down the interstate shrink-wrapped on a flatbed. Your construction loan, meanwhile, was designed around a different century's workflow. It releases money in draws, each one tied to an inspector confirming that work got done on your lot. Foundation poured, draw one. Framing up, draw two. The bank does not disburse for a house that exists in a factory two states away, because the bank's paperwork has no box for that.

So here is the arithmetic nobody puts in the brochure. Factory-built modular housing cuts build timelines by 20 to 50 percent and hard costs by up to 25 percent, according to Terner Center research presented to the California legislature in January. And yet off-site construction's national market share is stuck at roughly 3 percent of completions, the Wall Street Journal reported in August. A technology that demonstrably builds faster and cheaper has spent decades losing. Financing plumbing is a large part of why.

What Modular Actually Is (and Is Not)

Precision matters here, because the words get sloppy in marketing copy. A manufactured home is built to a federal HUD code, the one that until this year required a permanent steel chassis. A modular home is built in a factory to the same state and local building codes as a site-built house. Different legal animal. When the walls are set on your foundation, a modular home is, in the eyes of the code official, indistinguishable from the house the framing crew spent four months assembling on your lot.

Financing has not caught up to that legal equivalence. Traditional construction loans disburse in inspection-gated draws as work is completed on the property. The factory needs a large share of the contract value before production begins, because it buys materials and reserves line time months ahead. As one modular developer told the Journal, banks structure payouts for a house rising stick by stick on your dirt, not for a house arriving 90 percent complete on a truck. Cautious lenders, unfamiliar with the product, price the unfamiliar as risky and charge accordingly.

Then the appraisal problem layers on top. Appraisers live and die by comparable sales. In most markets there are few modular comps, so the finished home gets valued against stick-built sales, which sounds fair until you realize the comp set the appraiser can find rarely captures the factory home's actual quality tier. Undervalued appraisal, and the buyer either brings more cash to closing or walks away.

What Congress Did (Read the Fine Print)

The 21st Century ROAD to Housing Act, enacted July 11, 2026, contains two provisions aimed at this mismatch. Per the Bipartisan Policy Center's implementation tracker, the Modular Housing Production Act (Section 302) directs HUD to review FHA construction financing programs, identify barriers facing modular developers, and initiate rulemaking to address them within 120 days of publishing its review. HUD owes its review by July 11, 2027. Section 303 separately orders HUD to study the cost-effectiveness of off-site construction and report to Congress by the same date.

Note the verbs. Directs. Orders. Studies. None of them is "requires lenders to change draw schedules," because that authority does not live in this statute. Michael Tillman, CEO of developer PTM Partners, put it plainly to the Journal: the financing provision is "a study and rule-making directive. It's not a policy change." His summary: "signal, not substance."

He is right, and the timeline makes it worse. A review due in July 2027, then up to 120 days for rulemaking to begin, then rulemaking itself, which in the housing-finance world routinely takes years. Anyone whose project breaks ground in 2026 or 2027 is financing under today's rules. The law's practical effect on your construction loan is zero until the rulemaking lands, and the rulemaking's arrival date is best described as eventually.

The Other Half of the Stall: Code Fragmentation

Money is not the only plumbing problem. Tillman's sharper point was about codes: "Every city, every municipality has a different code. That fragmentation prevents the scale economics from taking off." He contrasted the United States with Germany, Japan, and Sweden, where national codes let a factory run one design at volume because what gets approved in Kyoto is approved in Osaka.

This is the economic argument the industry buries under its sustainability copy. A modular factory is a fixed-cost machine. Its unit economics depend on repeating the same product hundreds of times. Every local code variation, every plan-review reinterpretation, every jurisdiction that treats the modules as a novelty to be inspected from scratch, shaves volume off the run and pushes the factory toward bespoke work. Bespoke work is precisely what the factory was supposed to replace.

The ROAD Act gestures at this too, authorizing HUD to fund a study of a standardized modular code. A study of a code. Lobbyists for the factories will be waiting a while.

Where It Actually Works Today

The honest case for modular does not need Congress. Greystar, the country's largest apartment operator, is developing modular projects in Pennsylvania and Massachusetts. The San Francisco Tahanan project, built with factory methods, came in 30 percent faster and 25 percent cheaper than a conventional build, per the Terner Center. And the most instructive data point comes from the nonprofit world: Fauquier Habitat for Humanity's first modular project in Warrenton, Virginia, put four homes where one stood on Haiti Street, no road widening, no parking expansion, first family moved in March 2026, thirteen families total expected.

What Habitat learned is the lesson the brochures skip: the set day is the whole job. A narrow one-way street, a crane angling modules up a steep grade, pallets disintegrating underfoot. The specialized set crew does an enormous share of the real work on delivery day, and everything depends on site conditions no factory controls. Buyers who budget for the factory price and forget transport, crane, set crew, utility hookups, and site prep are the ones who discover the savings evaporated somewhere between the plant and the property line.

Factory-built housing turned out to be a doorway, not a destination.

The Skepticism Ledger

This industry has a graveyard. Katerra burned through roughly $2 billion. Veev took $647 million with it. Every few years a factory announces it will revolutionize housing the way the assembly line revolutionized cars, and every few years the math of transporting buildings down highways reasserts itself. Treat cost-savings claims accordingly: the Terner Center's 20 to 50 percent figures are real research, but independent per-project verification remains thin, and transport plus crane plus set crew can erase the spread on any individual job.

There is also a selection effect in the success stories. Disaster victims, from California fire zones to hurricane corridors, have turned to alternative construction partly because insurance payouts fall short of traditional rebuild costs. That is genuine demand, but it is demand born of desperation, which flatters the economics. A product that wins because the alternative is unaffordable has not yet proven it wins on the merits.

None of this means modular is a mirage. It means the pitch deserves the same scrutiny as the draw schedule: read the contract, price the set day, and discount any savings figure that does not itemize the last mile.

What This Means If You Are Building or Buying

First, reverse the order of operations. Get the factory's payment schedule in writing before you choose a lender, not after. That deposit structure determines which loan products can physically work, and a conventional construction loan sized for stick-built draws will seize up at the first factory invoice.

Second, use a lender that has actually closed modular deals. Ask for the number. A loan officer who has never funded a factory deposit will learn on your timeline, which is to say, slowly and at your expense. Construction-to-permanent products exist that accommodate off-site production; the trick is finding the officer who knows which drawer they are in.

Third, budget the hidden on-site stack separately from the factory price: transportation, crane time, the set crew, site prep, utility connections, and the inevitable weather day. Habitat's experience in Warrenton is the template. Price these before you sign, because this is where quoted savings go to die.

Fourth, start the appraisal conversation early. Confirm the appraiser will have modular comparable sales or understands the product category. If your market has no modular comps, your lender's valuation may come in light, and you will need cash or a different lender. Discovering this at closing is the expensive version.

Fifth, calibrate to the actual law. HUD's FHA barrier review is due July 11, 2027, with rulemaking to follow on an unspecified schedule. If your project is 2028 or later, the financing landscape may genuinely improve. If you are building now, plan for today's plumbing and treat any future reform as a bonus.

Sixth, for disaster rebuilds specifically: factory-built can close the gap between an insurance payout and a real rebuild, which is why it is gaining traction in fire and hurricane zones. But the loan has to fund the factory deposit, not just the on-site work. Verify that before the factory starts cutting steel or printing walls, because by the time the modules arrive, the leverage is gone.
