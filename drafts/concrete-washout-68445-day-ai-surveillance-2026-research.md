**Headline:** "Your Concrete Truck Washed Out on the Curb. That's a $68,445-a-Day Violation."

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes, twice over. For the builder: concrete washout is one of the most common construction stormwater violations, carries a statutory $68,445/day exposure, and EPA treats it as categorically serious. For the buyer: the driver washing out on your lot leaves caustic pH-12 slurry that can kill the lawn, contaminate soil, and become your cleanup problem after close.

## Thesis
Every residential concrete pour ends with a washout. Most residential washouts are illegal — chute water is a pH-12 slurry with toxic metals that may not be dumped on the ground or into drains under EPA CGP Part 2.3.4 or state/local equivalents. Compliance costs ~$100. The exposure is $68,445 per day per violation. And the detection model is changing: AI cameras on municipal trucks, drone stormwater surveys, and builders' own AI site cameras mean the washout on the curb is now a machine-readable event. The fix is a $91 pan. The reason nobody fixes it is nobody has done the math.

## Original contributions
1. **The 752:1 ratio.** $68,445 (one day of statutory exposure) divided by $91 (an Outpak washout pan good for 10 uses) = ~752:1. One week of uncorrected violation = $479,115 theoretical exposure vs $91 of cardboard-and-liner compliance.
2. **The ESA exclusion as enforcement signal.** EPA's own expedited settlement program for construction stormwater explicitly carves out concrete washout — this is the strongest available evidence of how seriously EPA treats it relative to other BMP failures. Nobody in the trade press has framed it this way.
3. **Per-pour violation math.** A typical custom home has 4-6 concrete events (footings, foundation walls, slab, driveway, sidewalk/pad). Each washout is a separate violation event; each uncorrected day is a separate daily violation.
4. **Detection-surface analysis.** Old model: inspector drives by (low probability). New model: neighbor phone, AI camera on a city truck (3rd Eye), drone stormwater survey, builder's own AI camera — converging to near-continuous monitoring of visible violations.

## Strongest counterargument
- Detection is not prevention. An AI camera films the driver hosing the chute; it doesn't stop him. The only thing that stops it is the pan being on site before the truck arrives and someone telling the driver where it is.
- The federal CGP covers sites disturbing 1+ acres; most single-family infill lots are smaller. Those sites answer to state/local stormwater ordinances, which also prohibit washout but rarely carry five-figure fines — local citations are typically hundreds to a few thousand per day. The $68,445 figure is the federal statutory maximum, not a typical settlement (Massachusetts residential stormwater settlements: $4,200-$8,700).
- AI cameras cut both ways: the builder's own AI camera footage is a discoverable record of every violation it captured and nobody acted on.
- Washout pans fail: plastic-lined pits tear, undersized units overflow in rain (the permit requires sizing for precipitation), and an installed-but-overflowing container is still a violation.

## Limitations
- No marketed "AI washout detector" exists as of Sept 2026. The article extrapolates from adjacent deployed products (Sitemetric hazard detection, 3rd Eye municipal detection, DOT drone stormwater surveys). That gap must be stated.
- The $68,445/day figure is the statutory maximum for judicial CWA penalties; real residential settlements are orders of magnitude lower. Present both.
- Penalty amounts cited are the Jan 8, 2025 inflation adjustment; a 2026 adjustment may be pending.
- The ESA pilot document is a 2019 revision; the exclusion policy's current status should be noted as of the archived doc.
- Washout-pH sourcing is EPA/Connecticut DEEP BMP guidance (~12), not a measured residential sample.

## Actionable takeaways (for article)
- Order the $91-$189 washout pan BEFORE the first pour; make the washout location a stop on the pre-pour checklist, and tell the driver where it is (mixer drivers default to wherever is convenient).
- Photo-document the station on every pour day (time-stamped; protects you in ESA/inspection disputes and satisfies SWPPP inspection records).
- Size for rain: the permit requires the container to not overflow due to precipitation — put the rain fly on, don't let it fill with stormwater.
- If you're a buyer: walk the lot after the foundation pour. Chute residue on the lawn or curb = evidence the builder's SWPPP compliance is decorative. Ask where the washout station is.
