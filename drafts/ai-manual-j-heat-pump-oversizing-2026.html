Your Heating System Is 2.75 Times Too Big. Your Thermostat Knew First.

By Priya Greenwood · September 21, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

In 2022, a homeowner shopping for a replacement heat pump collected three contractor quotes. All three said five tons. Three and a half tons, said the load calculation. That number did not come from a fourth contractor with a cheaper price. It came from a Manual J load calculation, the industry's own engineering standard, run after the quotes were already on the table. They bought the smaller system, paid less upfront, and got a house that holds temperature without the clammy cycling the old unit was famous for. Every other quote was a guess wearing a uniform.

That guess is the norm, not the exception. This September, researchers published a study of 74 detached homes across five American climate zones, and the headline number should embarrass the trade: existing heating equipment averaged 2.75 times the capacity that a year of each home's own thermostat data said it needed. Not 2.75 percent. Two and three-quarter times. Individual homes ranged from 1.34 to 6.52, which means some houses are heating with equipment six times larger than their walls require.

2.75× Average oversizing of residential heating equipment versus what smart-thermostat data says each home needs. 74 homes, five climate zones. (Lee, Pergantis & Kircher, September 2026)

Here is the part that should worry you more than the equipment number. The researchers also bought real Manual J calculations from working practitioners and ran them against the same data-driven estimates. The contractors' Manual J results came in 2.26 times higher than the thermostat data supported. Read that again: not the rule of thumb, the actual engineering calculation, done by professionals, still overshot by more than double. Manual J itself is sound. The inputs are where the padding lives. A conservative assumption about infiltration here, a safety factor on the windows there, a design temperature picked for the coldest night of the decade, and each fudge multiplies the next until the "engineered" answer is barely related to the house.

Built for a blizzard that lasts two hours

A companion finding from the National Renewable Energy Laboratory explains the mechanism for heat pumps specifically. Conventional sizing designs for the 99th-percentile temperature, the coldest hours of the year. But most of a cold-climate home's heating load lands in the 20 to 40 degree band, mild by January standards, where the house spends nearly the whole season. A heat pump sized for the design extreme spends its working life wildly oversized for the weather it actually sees. It satisfies the thermostat fast, shuts off at high startup power, and cycles, which drags its effective efficiency down. NREL's recommendation is blunt: pick a smaller heat pump than the Manual J heating capacity suggests and let backup heat cover the handful of hours that actually need it.

That advice runs against every instinct in the trade. Contractors oversize because oversizing is never punished. A house that stays warm on the coldest night generates no callback. A house with a right-sized system that a homeowner second-guesses during a cold snap generates a truck roll and a bad review. The Rutgers green building manual puts the industry's habit in one line: engineers size for overloads that occur one to two and a half percent of the time. You are buying a system for the other 97.5 percent and paying for the rest.

What the extra ton costs you

Start with the purchase. Equipment-only pricing puts a 3-ton heat pump around $4,250 to $7,850 and a 4-ton at $5,450 to $9,750, so each ton of capacity you do not need costs roughly $1,200 to $1,900 before the installer's markup, the pad, the line set, and the labor. Installed, the gap is bigger.

Then the bills. Heating and cooling eat about 48 percent of a typical American home's energy, the single largest line on the utility statement. An oversized air conditioner or heat pump cools the air so fast it never runs long enough to wring out the humidity, which is why oversized houses feel clammy at 72 degrees and their owners keep nudging the thermostat down, spending money to be uncomfortable. Short cycling also beats up the equipment. Compressors die from starts, not from hours.

Cooling has a hard ceiling in the standards, which tells you how routine the violation is. The Department of Energy's sizing guideline says cooling capacity should not exceed the total load by more than 15 percent, and furnaces should stay within 140 percent of peak heating load. ENERGY STAR's quality-installation criteria put the band at 95 to 115 percent of the calculated cooling load, and its verifiers are told to pull the file on any installation under 450 square feet per ton, because that ratio is the fingerprint of rule-of-thumb sizing. Those rules exist because the industry needed rules against its own habits.

Your thermostat is the load calculation

This is where the story turns. The September study's real contribution is not the 2.75 number, striking as it is. It is the method: the researchers estimated each home's design heating load two ways, from smart-thermostat time series and from monthly utility bills, and both methods agreed with each other while disagreeing with the installed equipment and the contractor math. Your house has been running a load experiment on itself every winter. All of that data was sitting in the cloud, and nobody thought to ask it what size equipment the house actually needs.

The authors are explicit about the endgame: run these data-driven methods at scale and write them into the standards. For a homeowner replacing equipment this year, the practical version already exists. ACCA-approved software like CoolCalc and Wrightsoft's Right-J does the Manual J properly when the inputs are honest. Free estimators let you sanity-check a quote from your kitchen table before anyone's van is in your driveway. Field platforms like ServiceTitan and Housecall Pro are folding the load-calc report into the quoting workflow, which means the documentation increasingly exists whether the contractor volunteers it or not. Run the numbers yourself first. If every estimate you produce lands near three tons and the quote says five, that gap is a conversation worth having before you sign.

The honest objections

A few, stated at full strength, because the 2.75 number is so lurid it invites overcorrection.

First, data-driven sizing needs data. A full heating season of thermostat history plus utility bills is the price of admission, which means it works for replacements in lived-in houses and not at all for new construction or a home whose thermostat was installed last month. No history, no estimate.

Second, the 2.26 figure is an indictment of padding, not of the method. Conservative assumptions compound: infiltration guessed high, windows assumed worse than they are, internal gains rounded down, design temperature chosen for drama. A clean Manual J with measured inputs remains the right answer. The problem was never the math. It was the thumbs on the scale.

Third, oversizing is sometimes rational, and not only as callback insurance. A planned addition, a future ADU tying into the same system, a climate where design temperatures keep getting rewritten: these are legitimate reasons to buy headroom. The sin is not the extra capacity. It is the extra capacity nobody disclosed or justified.

Fourth, the equipment matters. The worst oversizing penalties belong to single-stage machines that are either on or off. Modern variable-speed, inverter-driven heat pumps modulate down to a fraction of their rated capacity, which blunts the comfort and efficiency cost of a size-up considerably. If a contractor talks you into the bigger unit, make them sell you the inverter to go with it, and never accept a size-up on a single-stage box without a load calculation in writing.

Finally, NREL's downsize advice has a load-bearing condition: the backup heat has to exist. Sizing the heat pump below the design load and letting resistance or gas backup cover the peaks only works if the backup is actually sized for those peaks. A smaller heat pump with no backup plan is not efficiency. It is a cold house in January.

If you are buying equipment this year

  1. Demand the Manual J in writing before you sign. You want the calculated heating and cooling loads in BTU/hr, the design conditions used, and the selected equipment's rated capacity at those conditions. A contractor who did the work hands it over without hesitation.
  2. Know the bands. 95 to 115 percent of calculated cooling load (ENERGY STAR); cooling no more than 15 percent over total load and furnaces within 140 percent of peak heating load (DOE / Manual S). Anything beyond that needs a written justification, not a shrug.
  3. Watch for the 450 sq ft/ton tell. A quote sizing your home under 450 square feet per ton with no Manual J on file is rule-of-thumb sizing. Get a second quote.
  4. If you are replacing: a year of smart-thermostat history plus twelve months of utility bills is enough for data-driven estimates to check any quote. Run one before the contractor arrives.
  5. If you are building: a tighter envelope shrinks the load. Money spent on air sealing and insulation beats money spent on a bigger heat pump, and code already requires the Manual J, so make the builder show it.
  6. If a contractor insists on sizing up: insist on variable-speed, inverter-driven equipment, which modulates and softens the oversizing penalty.

Manual J has been the standard for decades, required by the residential code and the energy code alike. The scandal is not that the standard is wrong. It is that the most common way to size a $15,000 system remains a glance at the square footage and a number that felt safe, and that your thermostat has known the real answer all along.
