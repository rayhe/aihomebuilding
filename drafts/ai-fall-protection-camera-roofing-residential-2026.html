Fall Protection Topped OSHA's Violation List for the 15th Straight Year. A Camera Cut Violations 41.7% in 90 Days.

By Marcus Washington, September 15, 2026, Workforce & Labor

The harness was in the truck. I know this because I walked the site that morning, a tear-off in the Central Valley with a 7:12 pitch and six men moving fast, and I asked one of the roofers where his tie-off was. He laughed, not unkindly, and pointed down the driveway at the company pickup. "Boss says wear it when the inspector comes," he said. Two hours later the foreman told me the same crew had passed a "safety briefing" that week. The briefing, it turned out, was a ten-minute talk in the parking lot while the materials were being loaded.

Nobody on that roof was unusual. They were the industry, statistically speaking.

Fifteen years at the top

Every September, OSHA unveils its preliminary list of the ten most-cited standards at the National Safety Council's Safety Congress & Expo. Every September, the same violation sits at number one. For the fifteenth consecutive year, Fall Protection, General Requirements (29 CFR 1926.501), took the top spot in fiscal year 2025, with 5,914 violations (Occupational Health & Safety). The year before, FY2024 brought 6,307 violations and fourteen straight years (OHS). FY2023 ran higher still: 7,270 (Bloomberg Law).

Read the rest of the list and a pattern hardens. Fall Protection Training Requirements (1926.503) sits at number six. Ladders at number three. Scaffolding at number seven. Four of the top ten are the same hazard wearing different clothes: people working at height with nothing between them and the ground.

OSHA's regional administrator Eric Harbin, who presented the 2025 list, said roofing, framing, and siding contractors accounted for most of the citations, and roofing was the single most-cited industry. His diagnosis was charitable: small businesses, he said, have the ability and the desire to comply, they just need the knowledge. That is the official line, and I have some sympathy for it. I also have the numbers. Falls are the leading cause of death in construction, according to the National Roofing Contractors Association, and the standard that is supposed to prevent them has been the most violated in America since the first iPhone.

Fifteen years is not a knowledge gap. It is a business model.

The camera that never blinks

Here is the new part. AI vision systems now watch job-site camera feeds and flag safety violations the way a security guard never could: continuously, without fatigue, without taking a lunch break, without being friends with the foreman.

The clearest published result comes from viAct, an AI video analytics company, deployed at Alliad, an engineering and construction firm. Instead of installing new hardware, viAct ran its software on the cameras Alliad already had, training the models to watch for PPE non-compliance, unauthorized entry into hazardous areas, and fire and smoke. In three months, PPE violations fell 41.7 percent and unauthorized access to danger zones dropped 46.3 percent, as workers and supervisors adjusted to being watched (Workplace Material Handling & Safety). Mounir Ziani, Alliad's head of QHSE, credited the system's real-time alerts with letting safety teams "identify potential compliance issues sooner."

The vendors are multiplying. Sitemetric launched a purpose-built construction AI camera this year that detects hard hat and vest compliance, automatically identifies falls, and ties every alert to a specific worker's badge ID, employer, and work zone (PR Newswire). viAct is expanding into Australia explicitly targeting "work-at-height risks" on construction sites, selling its system as a way to catch the hazards that develop between scheduled inspections (IT Brief). Voxel's CEO describes the pitch in a sentence that should make every safety director and every union steward sit up at the same time: an "omnipresent, ever diligent safety manager" (Bloomberg Law).

Who is actually being watched

Now the uncomfortable part, which is the part that matters.

The Alliad pilot happened at a large engineering and construction firm with a QHSE department and a head of QHSE. Sitemetric sells to general contractors with site infrastructure and badge systems. The AI camera revolution, such as it is, is arriving at the organizations that already had safety programs, compliance budgets, and somebody whose job title includes the word safety.

Back to Harbin's numbers: roofing, framing, and siding contractors take most of the citations. On residential work, that overwhelmingly means small subcontractors running five-to-fifteen-person crews, many of them immigrant, many paid by the square, supervised by a foreman who also carries a nail gun. These are the crews where the harness lives in the truck. They do not have QHSE departments. They do not have badge systems. Many of them do not have written fall protection plans for the specific roof they are standing on, despite the rule requiring protection at six feet.

So here is the arithmetic of the thing. The technology works. It cut violations by more than two-fifths in ninety days without a single new camera. And the population it is currently protecting is largely the population that was already safest, because the firms buying AI safety monitoring are the firms that already bought everything else.

That is not an argument against the cameras. It is an argument about who gets to be safe in American residential construction, and the answer, fifteen years running, is: the workers on the biggest projects, watched by the most software, hired by the most capitalized firms. The rest climb a 7:12 pitch and hope.

What the camera cannot see

Honesty requires the rest, and there is plenty of it.

The Alliad numbers are a vendor case study on a large commercial firm. Nobody has published equivalent results for a five-person residential roofing crew, and residential crews are where the citations and the deaths concentrate. An AI can flag a worker without a harness. It cannot verify the anchor the harness is clipped to, and a tied-off lanyard attached to an unrated vent pipe is a decoration, not protection. The models are trained on the visible: hard hats, vests, bodies at height. They are not trained on the rot under the decking, the 30-year-old truss someone decided to reuse, or the foreman who signed the plan and never climbed the ladder.

Then there is the surveillance itself. The same camera that logs an untied-off worker logs everything else: who arrived late, who took a long lunch, who was talking to whom near the material pile. Bloomberg Law's reporting on AI safety monitoring is explicit that employers who deploy these systems without careful legal evaluation can "get themselves in trouble," with privacy, labor-bargaining, and data-retention exposure (Bloomberg Law). Workers notice. Some adjust, like Alliad's crews did, and compliance improves. Some resent it, and resentment on a crew has its own safety cost. My grandfather spent forty years in a union hall, and he had a saying about every new rule: the company enforces it against the workers, never against itself.

Fifteen years of citations also carries its own verdict. Fines did not fix this. Inspections did not fix this. If a camera were enough, we would not need to ask why the same standard has led the list since 2011. A camera escalates. It does not convince. Somebody still has to decide that the harness comes out of the truck, and that somebody is almost never the software.

The Rescue Plan Test

If you are building a home or hiring a roofer, you do not need AI software. You need three questions, asked before anyone climbs your ladder.

First: show me the written fall protection plan for this roof. Not a template. The plan for this pitch, these edges, these anchor points. If the contractor cannot produce a document specific to your roof, there is no plan. OSHA has been citing the absence of one for fifteen years.

Second: who is the competent person on site today, and what is the rescue plan? This is the question that ends conversations, and it should. Federal rules require employers to provide for prompt rescue of a worker who falls and is caught by a fall arrest system (29 CFR 1926.502(d)(20)). A worker hanging in a harness after a fall has roughly fifteen to thirty minutes before suspension trauma turns a saved life into a lost one. Ask your roofer what happens in those thirty minutes. Almost no residential contractor has an answer. The ones that do are the ones you hire.

Third: what monitoring do you run to verify tie-off? You are not asking for a brand name. You are asking whether the contractor has thought about the gap between the safety meeting and the roof. AI camera monitoring is now cheap enough to run on existing job-site cameras, and a contractor who uses it is a contractor who has decided compliance is a process, not a parking-lot speech. If the answer is "we trust our guys," you have learned something useful.

That morning in the Central Valley, the harness stayed in the truck all day. The crew finished the tear-off. Nobody fell. That is the sentence that keeps the industry going: nobody fell, this time. Fifteen years of OSHA's top ten says the luck runs out on schedule, and the camera, for all its limits, at least refuses to look away.
