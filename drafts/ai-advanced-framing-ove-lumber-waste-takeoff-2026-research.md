## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? **Yes.** A production builder framing 20 homes/year leaves roughly $15K–$25K of lumber on the table annually by framing to 1970s conventions instead of optimum value engineering (OVE). A buyer gets a wall with 60% deeper insulation cavities and a lower heating bill. A framing crew spends a third of its stud-setting motions on members the structure doesn't need — in a labor market where BLS projects 35,000 more carpenters needed by 2035 and veterans are retiring out (ADP: median carpenter age fell ~5 years since Jan 2020 as older workers left).

## Thesis
Your house is framed with roughly 30% more pieces of lumber than it structurally needs, and the people who know it best are the framers. Advanced framing (optimum value engineering) has been code-legal and lab-proven since the 1960s–90s. What's new: AI takeoff tools can now read a plan set in minutes and produce the two material lists — as-drawn vs. OVE-redesigned — that turn a 60-year-old argument into a line-item decision.

## Original contribution — the math (representative 2,400 sq ft two-story, 40×30 footprint)
Inputs: 280 LF exterior wall (140/floor × 2) + ~200 LF interior partitions. Lumber at Madison's Sept 2026 index: $506.76/MBF. Home Innovation Research Labs: average new single-family home uses ~15,000 BF of framing lumber.
- **Stud count:** exterior at 16" OC = 210 studs; at 24" OC = 140. Interior 200 LF: 150 vs 100. Total ≈ 360 vs 240 → **~120 fewer studs (~33%)**, consistent with Lstiburek's "30% fewer pieces" and the Toolbase 40-ft wall chart (35→21 studs).
- **Material dollars:** a 2x6x8 stud ≈ 8 BF ≈ $4.05 at index pricing → 120 studs ≈ **$490**. Single top plate (vs double) saves ~280 LF of plate ≈ 420 BF ≈ **$210**. Right-sized headers + eliminated cripples/jacks ≈ **$150–$300** (estimate, flagged as such). **Total ≈ $850–$1,000/home in lumber.** Cross-check: 15,000 BF × 5–10% BF reduction (Lstiburek) = 750–1,500 BF × $0.507/BF = $380–$760. The two methods bracket **roughly $400–$1,000/home**. DOE's $500/1,200 sq ft scales to ~$1,000 for 2,400 sq ft — same neighborhood.
- **Labor:** 30% fewer pieces to cut, carry, and nail. DOE cites up to 5% framing-labor reduction *assuming crew familiarity* — first-time OVE crews may see zero or negative savings (flagged).
- **Energy (modeled, not metered):** framing factor 25% → 15%; DOE-modeled ~5% heating/cooling reduction. On an ~$1,800/yr HVAC bill ≈ $90/yr ≈ $2,700 over 30 years undiscounted. Modest. Don't oversell.
- **Waste:** NAHB Research Center's old line — for every unneeded piece, the builder pays three times: purchase, handling, disposal. 30% fewer pieces ≈ proportionally less cut-off waste.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
OVE's savings are real on paper and fragile in the field. The single top plate only works with **stacked framing** — studs, joists, and rafters aligned within about an inch — which means the framer, the truss plant, and the plan set must agree before the first cut; one trade working off an old detail breaks the load path and the inspector red-tags it. Crews paid by the square foot don't get paid extra for thinking harder, and a production framer who has set 16" OC walls for twenty years will frame 24" OC *slower* the first three houses — eating the labor savings DOE promised. Drywall at 24" OC needs 5/8" board (or sag-resistant 1/2") on ceilings in many jurisdictions, which hands money back to the drywall contractor. In high-wind and seismic zones the double top plate and tight nailing schedules aren't optional, and most plan sets never get drawn for OVE in the first place because the architect's E&O insurer flinches at single top plates. Value-engineering OVE in after permit costs more than it saves. The technique is sixty years old and still rare for a reason: the savings accrue to whoever pays for lumber, while the risk accrues to whoever swings the hammer and stamps the plans — and those are different people.

## Limitations (dedicated accounting)
- Dollar math uses the national Madison's/Random Lengths composite ($507/MBF, Sept 2026); species, grade, and regional pricing vary ±20% or more.
- 15,000 BF/home is a Home Innovation national average; the representative plan is illustrative, not a specific takeoff.
- Labor savings (DOE "up to 5%") assume crew familiarity; first-time OVE crews likely see none.
- Energy savings are DOE-modeled, not metered per-home.
- NAHB's $0.24–$1.20/sq ft savings figures are from 1990s studies, in then-year dollars — cited for direction, not precision.
- AI takeoff vendor claims (LLAIE "under 5 minutes," Bolster "84% faster") are unverified marketing; I did not independently test any tool.
- Code citations reference IRC 2018/2021 tables; local amendments (notably CA, FL, high-seismic/wind jurisdictions) may restrict OVE details.

## Actionable takeaways (for the article)
- **Production GC (10+ homes/yr):** run one plan through an AI takeoff twice — as-drawn and OVE-redesigned by a designer who knows the details. At ~$850–$1,000/home in lumber alone, 20 homes = $17K–$20K/yr. The redesign must happen *before* permit, not after.
- **Custom builder:** cherry-pick the low-risk OVE details even if you skip 24" OC — 2-stud corners with drywall clips, ladder blocking at intersections, right-sized headers. No engineering, no inspector fights, still cuts pieces ~15%.
- **Framing contractor:** if you're paid lump-sum, OVE is margin — fewer pieces, same bid. If you're paid by the piece or square foot, negotiate: the thinking is the work now.
- **Buyer:** ask your builder for the framing factor of your walls. 25% (conventional) vs 15% (OVE) is the difference between a wall that's one-quarter solid wood and one that's mostly insulation. It's also a proxy for whether your builder is still framing like it's 1975.
