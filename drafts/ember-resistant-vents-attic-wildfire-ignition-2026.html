Your Attic Vent Is a Backdoor for Wildfire Embers. A $50 Baffle Vent Slams It Shut.

By Jake Kowalski, September 14, 2026, Construction Technology

After the Witch and Guejito fires tore through Rancho Bernardo in 2007, two NIST researchers did something unusual, tracking down every fire captain who worked the fire and reconstructing exactly how 74 destroyed homes ignited, until they could show that two out of every three were lit by embers, directly or indirectly, including one ember that started a home burning nine hours after the fire front had passed. The fire was over, the crews had moved on, and an ember was still finishing the job.

Your attic vents exist to breathe, which is the entire design: they pull outside air in so the attic does not cook your shingles or grow mold in your sheathing, and during a wildfire they keep right on breathing, inhaling burning debris and exhaling it into a room full of dry insulation. IBHS puts the share of wildfire home ignitions caused by embers as high as 90%. Not flame, not radiant heat, but embers the size of a grain of rice riding a pressure differential straight through the mesh.

Here is the part that should bother you. California's Chapter 7A building code has required vent protection in wildfire zones since 2007, and the requirement is a prescriptive floor of metal mesh, 1/8-inch or finer, that sounds specific but was never tested as an assembly against an actual ember shower, because no such test existed. The mesh size was a guess with a code stamp on it, and millions of code-compliant homes have been built on top of that guess.

Then they built the ember test

ASTM finally filled the gap with test method E2886, which does something beautifully brutal: a machine drops burning wood brands into an ember generator, a fan pulls the embers down through a horizontally mounted vent, and researchers watch whether anything ignites on the other side. Vents that pass get listed. Vents that do not keep getting sold with code-minimum mesh and a hopeful shrug.

Passing vents fall into three camps, starting with baffle-style vents like Brandguard that run airflow through an internal maze of overlapping metal plates, so embers lose momentum and die before reaching the attic, all while maintaining normal net free area, which matters more than most people realize and we will get to why. Multi-layer mesh vents like Vulcan stack screens with air gaps between the layers, so embers that clear the outer screen cool and break apart before reaching the inner one. Intumescent vents go a step further, carrying heat-activated strips that swell shut at around 200°F and seal the opening entirely when fire arrives.

Prices are refreshingly un-tech-bro. A standard 1/8-inch mesh vent cover runs $5 to $15. Brandguard baffle vents run $25 to $50 per vent, with Home Depot listing the 14-by-6 foundation model at $53.45 right now. Vulcan multi-layer vents run $20 to $45. Intumescent units cost $40 to $80, with the catch that they are single-use, meaning after they swell shut in a fire you replace them at full price again. Brandguard carries CAL FIRE's BML approval and a one-hour fire rating per ASTM E119, straight from the spec sheet.

Do the math nobody else did

A typical single-family home has 10 to 14 vents, counting soffit vents along the eaves, gable vents at the peaks, a ridge or roof vent up top, and foundation vents down low. Take the middle and twelve vents at $40 each is $480 in materials, and adding a handyman for a day to swap soffit and gable vents puts the whole job in the $700 to $1,200 band on a single-story home.

Now rank that against every other hardening line item in the Headwaters Economics retrofit report, where a wildfire-rated roof upgrade adds roughly $5,860 to a typical roof, IBHS Plus-standard eaves run about $5,200, a basic ember-defense retrofit runs $10,000 to $15,000, and full exterior protection runs $23,000 to $40,000. The vent swap is the cheapest per-dollar ember-path closure in the entire code book, closing the pathway NIST found responsible for two-thirds of ignitions in its most detailed case study, for the price of a nice dinner per vent.

The UC Berkeley machine-learning study published in Nature Communications in 2025 backs up the prioritization, with researchers training an XGBoost model on CAL FIRE damage data from five major fires, Tubbs, Thomas, Camp, Kincade, and Glass, then using SHAP analysis to rank what actually predicts whether a home survives. Hardening features including vents, roof, eaves, and windows rank alongside structure spacing and defensible space, and hardening plus defensible space together predict a hypothetical 52% reduction in losses, at 82% prediction accuracy on real burn outcomes, which is not a vendor brochure.

There is also a straight insurance play. California's "Safer from Wildfires" program requires every insurer to offer discounts across ten categories, and ember-resistant vents are one of them, with State Farm crediting 2 to 5% and Mercury up to 18%. On California's roughly $2,843 average premium, a 5% discount is about $142 a year, which means a $600 vent retrofit pays itself back in about four years on insurance alone, before counting the house it might save.

The airflow trap

Do not just buy the finest mesh and call it done, because there is a real engineering tradeoff here and it is why the code stopped at 1/8-inch.

Fine 1/16-inch mesh cuts net free area by 40 to 60% compared to standard vents, according to the US Made Supply vent guide, and a starved attic in a hot climate cooks your shingles, shortens roof life, and breeds moisture problems through the winter, which is how you trade the ember door for a condensation door. The baffle and intumescent route exists precisely because it delivers E2886-level ember protection without giving up airflow, and that distinction is worth the $25 to $50 per vent premium over plain mesh.

Installation is the other gotcha. Soffit vents under a tight enclosed eave on a two-story home are a miserable access job, and labor can double your materials budget, so budget the full $1,200 for a two-story before budgeting $700 for a ranch.

The strongest counterargument

Vents are one ember path and embers are creative, slipping through gaps between roof tiles, under decks, and through open windows, according to IBHS research. Worse, the Berkeley model ranks structure spacing as the single strongest predictor of survival, which means if your neighbor's house sits eight feet away, your vents are not your biggest problem. Your neighbor's vents are not your biggest problem. Their house is.

A vent-only strategy on a home with a cedar shake roof is theater, and hardening works as a system, the way the code authors intended when they wrote Chapter 7A as a component-by-component package rather than a menu you pick one item from.

What this analysis does not prove

The cost math uses September 2026 retail pricing and a handyman install on a single-story home. Two-story homes with enclosed soffits, Bay Area labor rates, or custom copper vent profiles will run higher, sometimes much higher, and the CDI discount math assumes your carrier credits the vent category independently, which is not guaranteed because some carriers bundle categories or effectively require most of the ten.

NIST's two-thirds ignition figure comes from one exceptionally well-documented case study, the 2007 Trails fire, and the IBHS up-to-90% figure aggregates many events without publishing the underlying event list in the release. The Berkeley model's SHAP rankings are reported for hardening features as a group in the release materials, so treat "vents rank among the top predictors" as a reading of the published analysis, not a standalone published number.

The takeaway

If you own a home in a wildfire zone and you do one hardening upgrade this year, do the vents: walk the eaves, check the gables, count the foundation vents, then price baffle or multi-layer E2886-listed vents at $25 to $50 each instead of plain mesh. Keep your attic's net free area intact, and file the "Safer from Wildfires" paperwork with your carrier after the work is done.

One ember, nine hours after the front passes. That is the threat you are buying against, and $50 a vent is a stupidly good price for closing that door.
