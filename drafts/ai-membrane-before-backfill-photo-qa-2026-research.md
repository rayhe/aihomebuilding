## Primary sources

1. **IRC Section R406 (International Residential Code)** — dampproofing (R406.1) vs waterproofing (R406.2). Dampproofing is the code minimum for most homes; true waterproofing is only required where a high water table or "severe soil-water conditions are known to exist." Six/seven approved waterproofing methods (40-mil polymer-modified asphalt, 60-mil flexible polymer cement, 60-mil solvent-free liquid-applied synthetic rubber, etc.). "All joints in membrane waterproofing shall be lapped and sealed." Sources: InterNACHI summary (https://www.nachi.org/foundation-dampproofing-waterproofing.htm), ICC code text (https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/INRC2020P1/chapter-4-foundations), Lubbock TX ecode360 adoption.

2. **ASTM D8231-19 / D7877 (Electronic Leak Detection)** — low-voltage ELD scanning locates breaches in waterproofing membranes. D8231 mandates testing BEFORE overburden (backfill) is placed; once backfill is on, testing is compromised. Low-voltage method: wet membrane surface + conductive substrate (concrete); receiver detects leakage current at breaches. Semi-conductive membranes (EPDM, carbon-black fluid-applied) testable since the 2019 practice. Sources: Detec Systems IntegriScan summary PDF (https://detecsystems.com/wp-content/uploads/2026/03/ASTM-Standard-D8231-Summary.pdf), IIBEC Interface Aug 2023 (https://iibec.org/publication-post/risk-mitigation-and-loss-control-using-electronic-leak-detection/), Building Enclosure (https://www.buildingenclosureonline.com/articles/89432-recent-advances-in-electronic-leak-detection).

3. **Carlisle CCW EPDM sheet waterproofing spec (Section 071353)** — commercial-grade QA protocol: applicator must be "specifically approved in writing by the membrane manufacturer"; pre-application conference; owner/contractor inspection upon completion; flood test with 1-2 inches of water for 24 hours BEFORE protection board; manufacturer warranty 1-20 years on materials. Residential jobs almost never do any of this. Source: https://www.carlisleccw.com/download.aspx?id=2558&fileID=420

4. **Angi basement waterproofing warranty guide (2022, crawled 2026)** — waterproofing warranties never cover the cost of water damage itself; manufacturer warranties cover materials only (not labor, not damage); many warranties void if not installed by licensed/certified installer; limited warranties 5-30 years. Source: https://www.angi.com/articles/know-your-basement-waterproofing-warranty.htm?entry_point_id=33796995

5. **OpenSpace / Buildots 360 photo documentation** — hardhat-mounted 360 cameras create street-view-like, plan-mapped visual records; Buildots AI overlays imagery on 3D model and flags deviations/defects automatically. Used on commercial jobs to exonerate subs, verify rough-in before concealment. Residential waterproofing trench is the perfect analog: the most expensive thing to uncover is the least documented. Sources: https://www.openspace.ai/resources/use-cases/insurance-costs/, https://bdcmagazine.com/2020/06/buildots-revolutionising-construction-management-through-the-use-of-artificial-intelligence/, AEC Magazine OpenSpace Field (2026): inspections from 3-4 hours to 30 minutes with photo-located issues.

6. **Cost data** — Prevention $3,000-$10,000 (exterior waterproofing systems); reactive foundation repair average $4,511 (range $2,318-$6,750, HomeAdvisor-style data via contractor sources); extensive structural work $20,000+; mold remediation $1,200-$6,000 typical, up to $30,000; prevention-vs-repair gap framing: $8K now vs $25-50K later. Builder-side delta between dampproofing and waterproofing is a few hundred dollars in materials (contractor framing, treat as directional not gospel).

## The workforce angle (Marcus beat)
- The membrane goes on in a muddy 7-foot trench, often applied by the lowest-paid sub or day labor on the site. The work is physically brutal, the skill requirement (lap joints, terminations, penetrations) is real, and the feedback loop is ~5 years: you don't learn you did it wrong until the basement floods.
- Manufacturer-certified applicator requirement exists on commercial specs (Carlisle: written manufacturer approval) but is absent from residential practice. Nobody inspects coverage before backfill on a typical tract home.
- AI angle: the tech doesn't replace the applicator; it gives the applicator and the homeowner a verifiable record. 360 capture in the trench + AI coverage/lap-seal checks before backfill approval. EFVM as the commercial QC standard that residential never gets.
- Uncomfortable conclusion for the beat: the industry's labor model for waterproofing (cheap sub, no inspection, buried evidence) is precisely calibrated to make failures untraceable. AI documentation breaks that.

## Skepticism to include
- ELD/EFVM gear costs real money and needs a trained operator; overkill for a $400K tract home; but photo documentation costs nearly nothing.
- AI defect detection on membrane images is largely unproven at residential scale; claims should be framed as coverage verification (was every square foot coated?) not defect prediction.
- Dampproofing is code-legal and adequate in many soils; the article should not claim every home needs full waterproofing.
- Cost numbers are contractor-marketing-adjacent; cite as ranges with "reported" framing.

## Actionable takeaways (for the draft)
1. Before your builder backfills: walk the trench. Photograph every wall, every joint, every penetration. Takes 20 minutes.
2. Ask who applied the membrane and whether the manufacturer certifies them. Get the answer in writing.
3. Ask for dampproofing vs waterproofing by name. If you have a high water table and got dampproofing, you got the wrong product.
4. Add a contract line: "waterproofing membrane shall be inspected and photographed prior to backfill; backfill shall not proceed without owner/builder sign-off." Zero cost, total leverage.
5. If you're buying new: ask the builder for the membrane warranty registration and read what it excludes (it excludes the water damage).
