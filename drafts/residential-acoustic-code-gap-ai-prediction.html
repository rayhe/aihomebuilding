Fifty-Three Percent of Buyers Want a Quiet Home. The Code Requires It for the Apartment Next Door. Not for the Room Next Door.

By Elena Vasquez · July 2026

Stand in the hallway of a new-construction home sometime. Not during the open house, when the listing agent has Norah Jones drifting from a Bluetooth speaker and the neighbors are at work. Stand there on a Tuesday evening when the family upstairs is doing what families do: running water, closing drawers, walking in socks across engineered hardwood. Listen to the wall between the master bedroom and the child's room, or the partition separating the home office from the kitchen, and notice how little stands between you and every sound on the other side.

That wall is almost certainly a standard 2x4 wood stud partition with a single layer of half-inch drywall on each side, no cavity insulation, and a Sound Transmission Class rating of approximately 33. At STC 33, normal speech is clearly audible through the wall. Not muffled, not distant. Audible. You can follow a phone conversation. You can tell whether the person on the other side is watching a cooking show or the news.

Before you moved in, that wall passed structural inspection, electrical inspection, plumbing inspection, energy code compliance, fire separation review, and egress calculations. Nobody checked whether you could hear your daughter crying through the bedroom wall at two in the morning. Nobody had to.

What the code doesn't say

Open the International Residential Code and look for acoustic requirements. You will find Appendix K, titled "Sound Transmission," which sets minimum STC and IIC ratings for walls and floor-ceiling assemblies separating dwelling units. STC 45 for airborne sound, IIC 45 for impact sound. Read the first sentence of that appendix carefully. It says: "The provisions contained in this appendix are not mandatory unless specifically referenced in the adopting ordinance."

Not mandatory.

Most jurisdictions do not adopt Appendix K for single-family construction. And even where they do, the requirements apply only to walls separating one dwelling unit from another, such as townhouse party walls. Interior partitions within a single-family home have no acoustic performance requirement anywhere in the IRC.

IBC Section 1206 is more assertive. Its 2021 edition requires an STC of no less than 50 for walls, partitions, and floor-ceiling assemblies separating dwelling units in multifamily construction. That means the wall between your apartment and your neighbor's apartment in a new building must reduce loud sounds to the point where they are faintly heard. But the wall between your bedroom and your child's bedroom in a $750,000 single-family home? It can transmit normal conversation at full intelligibility, and it is perfectly code-compliant.

In practice, the STC gap is enormous: A standard interior wall in a single-family home (2x4 stud, single drywall, no insulation) achieves STC 33-35. At STC 33, normal speech is understood. Code-minimum apartment separations (STC 50) reduce loud sounds to faintly heard. Between what new-home buyers live with and what apartment tenants are guaranteed by law sits a 15-17 point STC gulf, roughly a fourfold difference in perceived loudness.

In a technical FAQ, the American Wood Council confirmed this reading: code provisions on sound transmission "apply to wall and floor/ceiling assemblies separating dwelling units from other dwelling units, or from adjacent public areas, within a building." Interior partitions within a home are unregulated. Exterior walls are unregulated. Silence in the code is comprehensive.

What you actually hear

The subjective experience of STC ratings has been documented extensively, most notably in ASTM research that produced a correlation table used across the industry. At STC 25, normal speech is understood with ease. At STC 35, loud speech is audible but not intelligible. At STC 50, loud sounds are faintly heard. At STC 60 and above, most sounds do not disturb neighboring residents.

Your new home's wall sits at STC 33, squarely in the range where loud speech is audible and normal speech is partially understood. For context, a hotel room wall targeting luxury-tier guest satisfaction aims for STC 55 to 60. Recording studio isolation starts at STC 60 and goes higher. A partition wall in a new-construction home achieves roughly the acoustic privacy of an interior office wall in a 1990s cubicle farm.

Fifty-three percent of homebuyers consider soundproofing essential or desirable, according to the National Association of Home Builders' "What 21st Century Home Buyers Want" survey. Noise is the second-largest contributor to construction lawsuits, according to the American Institute of Architects. Demand exists, liability exists, and the code addresses neither for the single-family market.

A $2,400 fix nobody specifies

What makes this gap infuriating is how cheap it would be to close.

Adding R-13 fiberglass batt insulation to the cavity of a standard 2x4 interior wall costs $1 to $2 per square foot of wall area installed. STC jumps from 33 to 39, a six-point improvement that moves the subjective experience from "normal speech understood" to "loud speech audible as a murmur." A typical 2,400-square-foot home has roughly 1,200 linear feet of interior walls, but not all of them matter acoustically. Targeting the six or eight partitions that separate bedrooms from living areas, the home office from common spaces, and the laundry room from adjacent rooms adds perhaps 400 to 600 square feet of insulated wall area. At $1.50 per square foot, the material and labor cost runs $600 to $900 for the entire home.

Step up to resilient channel (a thin metal strip that decouples the drywall from the studs, interrupting the vibration path through the framing) plus insulation, and the same walls achieve STC 44 to 48. Loud speech heard but not audible. Resilient channel adds roughly $2 to $3 per square foot on top of the insulation, putting the total at $1,400 to $2,400 for a meaningful acoustic upgrade across the critical partitions in a typical home.

For $2,400 in a home that costs $450,000 to build. That is 0.5% of construction cost. It would not appear as a line item on a closing statement. It barely registers as a rounding error in the framing budget. And yet nearly every production builder in America skips it, because the code does not require it and the buyer does not know to ask for it until they have been living with the walls for three months and the baby will not sleep through the dishwasher cycle.

A neural network that hears through walls

At Lund University and Université du Québec, researchers have been training artificial neural networks to predict sound insulation performance from physical wall and floor assembly characteristics. Published across a two-part series in MDPI's Acoustics journal in 2022, their work demonstrated that a neural network trained on 252 standardized laboratory measurements could predict the weighted airborne sound reduction index of lightweight wooden floor structures with a maximum error of 2 dB.

Two decibels. In acoustic engineering, a 1 dB difference is considered imperceptible. A 3 dB difference is the threshold of noticeability. In other words, the neural network's prediction accuracy falls within the margin of what a human ear can detect as different.

Inputs are straightforward: material type, thickness, density, dimensions, insulation properties, and the overall mass of the assembly. From these parameters, the network learns nonlinear relationships that analytical models handle poorly, such as the coincidence frequency where a panel's bending wavelength matches the sound wavelength and suddenly transmits far more energy than its mass would suggest.

Part II of the research extended the approach to facade walls, using 100 insulation curves, and achieved prediction accuracy within 3 dB for the weighted airborne sound reduction index. Feature attribution analysis revealed that insulation material thickness and panel density were the dominant predictors, followed by stud spacing and total density of clustered exterior layers.

These models work. Accuracy is sufficient for design-stage decision-making. For a builder choosing between wall assemblies, knowing that Assembly A will achieve STC 37 and Assembly B will achieve STC 44 is enormously valuable, even if actual field performance varies by 2-3 points from the prediction. Between 37 and 44 sits the difference between hearing your teenager's music and not hearing it, which is the difference between a satisfied homeowner and one who calls back within the warranty period asking what can be done about noise.

Tools built for the wrong buildings

COMSOL Multiphysics offers an Acoustics Module with full vibro-acoustic simulation capabilities, including sound propagation in ducts, HVAC systems, and enclosed cavities. Annual licenses run approximately $6,000 to $12,000 depending on modules selected. Hexagon's MSC Nastran includes an extensive acoustic simulation suite. Insul, developed in New Zealand, offers a more accessible analytical model for predicting sound transmission loss through building elements. Canada's National Research Council maintains prediction tools for wood-frame assemblies, and the American Wood Council has its own empirical models.

Every one of these tools was designed for commercial, industrial, or multifamily applications. Their user interfaces assume an acoustical consultant, a mechanical engineer, or an architectural firm with a building science department. Learning curves are measured in weeks. Licensing costs assume project budgets measured in millions.

Nobody has built the residential version. Nobody has taken the neural network research and wrapped it in a tool where a builder enters "2x4 stud, 16-inch on center, R-13 fiberglass, 5/8-inch Type X drywall both sides, resilient channel on one side" and gets back "STC 46, IIC not applicable, estimated cost premium $3.10 per square foot over standard assembly." That tool would take a weekend to build. Training data exists in the published MDPI datasets. Accuracy is sufficient. Every residential builder in America who does not currently employ an acoustical consultant is the market, which is nearly all of them.

Where the field performance fails

Even when acoustic requirements apply, field performance routinely falls short of laboratory ratings. An article in the American Apartment Owners Association publication quoted field practitioners observing that multifamily walls required to achieve STC 40 "more often perform in the mid- to high 20s," a gap of 12 to 15 points between what was specified and what was built.

Failure modes are banal. Back-to-back electrical outlets create a direct sound path through the wall. A quarter-inch gap beneath the drywall at the floor plate, invisible behind the baseboard, transmits sound as efficiently as an open window transmits light. Penetrations for plumbing, HVAC ducts, or recessed lighting that are sealed for fire but not for sound. Single-stud walls where the architect specified a staggered stud or double-stud configuration and the framer used what was on the truck. Each defect is small, easily overlooked during inspection (because nobody inspects for acoustic performance in residential), and devastating to the wall's actual STC rating.

A neural network trained on laboratory data will predict the assembly's theoretical performance. It will not predict what happens when the electrician drills three oversized holes for Romex runs and doesn't seal them, or when the insulation installer compresses the batts to fit around a junction box and leaves a 6-inch void. That gap between specified and installed is where much of the residential acoustic failure actually lives, and it is the same gap that plagues energy performance, fire stopping, and every other invisible building system whose compliance depends on trades that are incentivized to work fast and penalized for nothing except structural deficiency and fire code violation.

Working from home changed everything

Before 2020, the acoustic performance of a home office was mostly a matter of personal comfort. You could close the door and accept that the dishwasher was faintly audible. Since the pandemic made the home office permanent for roughly 35% of American workers, acoustic performance has become a functional requirement. A video call conducted in a room with STC 33 walls picks up every sound from the adjacent hallway, kitchen, or playroom. Your microphone does not care whether the sound is faint to the human ear. It hears what the wall transmits, and it transmits that to every colleague on the call.

An acoustical consultant specifying a functional home office would recommend STC 50 or above for the walls and door assembly, with attention to HVAC noise through supply and return registers, and acoustic sealing at the door frame and threshold. Applied to a 120-square-foot home office, that specification would add $1,500 to $3,000 to the construction cost, depending on the starting assembly and HVAC configuration. For context, the same home likely includes $4,000 to $8,000 in kitchen cabinet upgrades selected from a design center. An acoustic upgrade for the room where the buyer earns their living costs less than the cabinet hardware.

Production builders have not caught up. Model homes showcase kitchen islands and primary bath finishes and garage storage systems. Nobody showcases the STC rating of the wall between the bonus room and the master suite, because nobody walks through a model home and says, "This wall sounds inadequate." You cannot hear inadequacy in a model home. You hear it at 11 PM on a Wednesday when the wall between your bedroom and your teenager's room demonstrates exactly how much bass a standard 2x4 partition transmits, and you learn for the first time what STC 33 sounds like when someone else controls the source.

What would actually work

The intervention is not complicated. It requires three things that do not currently exist together in the residential market.

First, a prediction tool accessible to residential builders without acoustical training. Bader Eddin, Ménard, and their colleagues demonstrated the neural network foundation. Training data from 252 floor assemblies and 100 facade assemblies is a start, and supplementing it with the extensive STC testing databases maintained by the National Research Council of Canada, Owens Corning, CertainTeed, and USG would produce a model covering thousands of common residential assemblies. Picture an interface resembling an energy code compliance calculator: input assembly components, get back a predicted STC and IIC rating, see the cost delta between the current specification and alternatives that achieve the next performance tier. Such a tool could exist as a web application. It could run locally. Computational cost of inference on a trained neural network is trivial. What stands in the way is not technology. It is the absence of anyone connecting the research to the market.

Second, a disclosure mechanism. Buyers receive energy performance disclosures (HERS scores, energy labels, utility cost estimates) in many jurisdictions. They receive radon test results, lead paint disclosures, and flood zone classifications. They receive nothing about acoustic performance. A simple STC label for key interior partitions, generated by the prediction tool and disclosed alongside the HERS certificate, would create the market signal that currently does not exist. When buyers can compare the STC 33 walls in Builder A's spec home with the STC 44 walls in Builder B's, the market will do what the code has not.

Third, a field verification method that doesn't require a $3,000 acoustic test. Smartphone-based acoustic measurement applications have reached surprising accuracy for field estimation. Apps like NIOSH Sound Level Meter and REW (Room EQ Wizard) can measure ambient noise levels and frequency response with calibrated accuracy using the phone's built-in microphone. An AI-driven field test protocol, using a standardized sound source on one side of a wall and a calibrated smartphone measurement on the other, could provide an approximate STC estimate at virtually zero cost. It would not replace ASTM E336 field testing for code compliance purposes, but it would give a homeowner or inspector a ballpark number that distinguishes a well-built STC 45 wall from a defective STC 28 wall, and that distinction matters more than precision at the margins.

Silence in the spec sheet

I have reviewed hundreds of residential construction specifications over the past decade. I have seen wall schedules that detail stud gauge, spacing, sheathing type, moisture barrier, insulation R-value (for thermal purposes), drywall thickness, texture, and paint finish. I have never seen a residential wall schedule that lists an STC target. Not once. Not even in custom homes where the architect specified imported Italian tile for the bathroom floor and triple-glazed windows for the street-facing facade.

This omission is not negligence by any individual architect or builder. It is a systemic failure of the code framework that treats sound as a concern only at the boundary between legal occupancies, not at the boundary between lived experiences within a home. Code asks whether fire can spread through the wall, whether the wall can support the load above it, whether the insulation meets the thermal threshold for the climate zone. It does not ask whether a parent can hear a phone conversation through the wall of a sleeping infant's room, because the code was written to protect health and safety, and noise was not classified as a health concern when the acoustic provisions were drafted.

That classification is changing. In 2018, the World Health Organization published environmental noise guidelines identifying chronic noise exposure as a contributor to cardiovascular disease, cognitive impairment in children, sleep disturbance, and mental health effects. Research is extensive and growing. But the IRC has not incorporated it, and prospects for mandatory acoustic requirements in single-family residential codes remain distant in a regulatory environment where any new mandate faces resistance from production builders concerned about construction cost impacts.

AI tools will not change the code. What they can change is the information available to the buyer, which changes the market, which changes what builders build, which may eventually change what the code requires. That sequence is slower than a mandate. It is also the only sequence that has any realistic chance of happening in an industry where 53 percent of buyers want quiet and 100 percent of them buy homes that are not designed for it.

If you are building or buying

Ask your builder for the STC rating of the wall assemblies between bedrooms, between the home office and common areas, and between the laundry room and adjacent living spaces. If they cannot answer, they have not thought about it, which means you are getting STC 33 and the acoustic privacy of a motel room. If you are building custom, specify a minimum STC 44 for critical partitions. Cost delta is $1,400 to $2,400 for the entire home, buried in the framing and insulation line items. If you are buying production, ask whether interior partitions include cavity insulation and resilient channel. If the answer is no, budget $3,000 to $6,000 for a post-construction retrofit that will be more expensive and less effective than what $2,400 would have bought during framing.

Then ask yourself why nobody told you this before. The answer: the code didn't require it, the builder wasn't asked, and the technology to make acoustic performance visible to non-experts has been sitting in academic journals since 2022, waiting for someone to build the tool that puts it in your hands.
