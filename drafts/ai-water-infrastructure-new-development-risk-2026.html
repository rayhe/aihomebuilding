Your 30-Year Mortgage Will Outlive the Pipes It Depends On.

By Priya Greenwood · September 7, 2026

In February 2024, a water main broke under a street in Logan, Utah. Crews gathered around the trench in the cold while a newspaper photographer documented the scene, and somewhere upstream, a few hundred households boiled their water or did without. Nothing about the break was unusual, because America breaks about 260,000 water mains every year, roughly 700 a day, and every one of them starts as a pipe the utility knew was old and meant to replace.

One photograph from that scene ran with Utah State University's third national water main study, the largest of its kind ever conducted in the United States and Canada, built on survey responses from more than 800 utilities and break records covering nearly 400,000 miles of pipe. One finding landed like a shovel hitting clay: one in five of the country's water mains, about 452,000 miles of pipe, is already past its useful life, waiting on a $452 billion replacement that nobody has funded. In 2012, the same research team measured 8 percent; now it is 20.

Nobody checks this when they buy a house, because a standard inspection covers the roof, the panel, the foundation, and the termites, and it never covers the miles of pipe between your house and the treatment plant, or the age of the cast iron laid when your subdivision was a farm.

New construction does not solve the problem, because the house is new but the main it connects to often is not: developers tie subdivisions into whatever distribution system runs past the property line, and in older utility territories that system was sized for a smaller, poorer town fifty years ago.

An AI that grades your pipes

Utilities are not guessing about which pipes fail next. Xylem, the water technology company, built failure-probability models with the mapping firm Esri that score pipe segments on material, age, soil corrosivity, and break history, turning a city's entire break history into a risk score for every block. That ranking is how a utility decides which main gets replaced next year and which one gets to wait, and those models are sold to utilities, not to homebuyers, which means the forecast exists and you cannot see it. Nobody funded the fix. But the inputs feeding them are mostly public: pipe age and material from utility asset records, break histories from local news and city council minutes, compliance data from federal databases.

Your utility almost certainly has a ranked list of its dying pipes. You are simply not on the distribution list. The map is free. Use it.

Thirty years of breaks, one mile of pipe

Here is the math nobody runs at the closing table. Between them, the United States and Canada hold roughly 2.3 million miles of water main and break 260,000 of them a year, which works out to 11.3 breaks per 100 miles of pipe per year, or 0.113 per mile. Multiply by a 30-year mortgage and a single mile of main can expect 3.4 breaks before the loan is paid off, all of them on the utility's repair schedule and none of them on yours.

Now count the miles, because your water does not arrive by teleport. It travels through miles of local distribution main, and every foot of it is aging on the utility's schedule, not yours, and none of it appears in the disclosure packet your agent hands you at closing. That 3.4 is an average across every mile in two countries, and residential streets do not run on average pipe, because the small-diameter mains under your subdivision were laid cheapest, maintained least, and fail most. They run on smaller mains, which the Utah State researchers found fail five times more often than large ones, a finding that should make any buyer of new construction on the edge of an old utility territory sit up. The clock ran out. A mile of 8-inch pipe under your street is not an average mile.

Cast iron tells the story in miniature, with 86 percent of it more than 50 years old against an average failure age of 53, per the study. Much of suburban America drinks through iron that has reached the age where iron dies.

The bill arrives through the tap

Emergency repairs cost about $2.6 billion a year. Divide by 260,000 breaks and the frantic part of a break, the crew in the trench at 2 a.m., averages about $10,000, which is the number that makes a city council choose patching over replacement every single time. Patching is the cheap part, and utilities do not absorb shortfalls; they recover costs through rates, through bonds, through the surcharge line on your water bill that keeps getting longer. The ratepayer always pays. Actually replacing dead pipe instead of patching it is the $452 billion nobody has.

Consider the comparison your lender never makes before handing you the keys: the bank makes you insure against fire, flood, and wind, even though the pipe in the street is the one asset with a failure clock the researchers could actually measure. None of those carries a 3.4-in-30 chance of hitting the street in front of your house. Your water main does, and nobody asks about it.

The strongest objection

The case against worrying deserves a fair hearing. America's drinking water systems remain regulated under the Safe Drinking Water Act, and compliance data shows most utilities meeting standards most of the time, which is why your tap water is almost always safe even when the pipes carrying it are ancient. A pipe can be old without being dangerous. Most new subdivisions install new service laterals and often trigger main extensions in modern PVC, which the Utah State study found has the lowest break rate of any material, which means the newest houses on the oldest systems can have the newest pipes on the block and still drink through someone else's rust. Health risk is genuinely small, while the financial risk is diffuse, spread across every ratepayer in the system whether their street ever floods or not. You will pay for the pipes through your bill whether or not a break ever opens your street. None of that makes the risk imaginary; it makes it collective.

What this analysis could not prove

Here are the honest gaps. A free EPA tool, the Water ICAT map, lets anyone look up a utility's compliance record, violation history, and funding data, but it reports at utility scale, not pipe-segment scale. It cannot tell you the main under Elm Street is 1971 cast iron; only the utility's asset records can, and those are not always public, and in some states they are treated as security-sensitive infrastructure data, which means the exact age of the pipe under your street can be a documented fact that you are not allowed to see. Break rates vary enormously by soil, climate, and material, so a national average is a starting point, not a forecast for your block, which is why two identical houses on opposite sides of a soil boundary can have wildly different pipe risk. That 3.4-breaks-per-mile figure blends the U.S. and Canada; a house served by new PVC in sandy soil may never see a break, while a house on 1960s iron in corrosive clay could see several. And the average failure age of 53 comes from the study's sample, not a warranty stamped on any individual pipe, and a pipe that survives to 60 is not proof the number is wrong, any more than a pipe that bursts at 40 is proof it is right.

Check the water before you buy the house

You cannot inspect the main, but you can investigate the system. Before you buy, look up your utility on the EPA's Water ICAT map, pull the utility's Consumer Confidence Report, which federal law requires it to publish every year, and read the violations section before the marketing copy. Then ask the developer the question nobody asks, the one about whether this water system's capacity can absorb the demand the subdivision will generate at full buildout, when every lot is sold and every lawn is green. A blank stare is itself an answer.

If you are on a well, get independent testing and check your state's groundwater data, because a private well puts you outside the utility system entirely, which means no EPA map, no ranked replacement list, and no one to call but yourself. If you are building new, ask what material the distribution main serving your lot is made of, and how old it is, and get the answer in writing, because a verbal "it's all new" from a sales agent has the evidentiary value of a weather forecast. Fifty years of PVC ahead of you is a different purchase than 1971 cast iron with its best years behind it.

Your builder warranties the roof for twenty years and the appliances for one, but no warranty covers the pipe in the street, which is strange, because it is the one thing your house cannot function without and the one thing nobody priced into the purchase. A few minutes on a public map will not replace the pipe. It will tell you what you are really buying.
