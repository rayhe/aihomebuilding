Your Solar Panels Add $10,000 in Market Value. If You Leased Them, the Appraisal Says $0.

By Priya Greenwood · October 5, 2026

Twenty-eight days from today, the way America values your home changes, because on November 2 the old paper-style appraisal forms retire and a single dynamic digital report called UAD 3.6 takes over, with structured fields for your solar panels, your heat pump, and your efficiency rating. For the first time, the green features of your home become machine-readable data that travels with the loan file, which sounds like a victory for every homeowner who ever watched an appraiser shrug at a $30,000 solar array.

It is, sort of. Because the same rulebook that finally sees your solar panels also declares some of them worth nothing, because the artificial intelligence now valuing 98 million American homes was trained on thirty years of appraisals that never recorded green features at all, and because the model's own margin of error is bigger than the entire premium your panels add. Your panels became data. Whether they became value is a different question.

$10,000
The average sale premium for homes whose listings explicitly mention rooftop solar, per June 2026 research from the 257 platform and the Smart Energy Consumer Collaborative: 2% on a $557,000 median price. Listings mentioning heat pumps sold for 0.6 to 1% more.

What Actually Changes on November 2

Appraisers have filled out the same basic forms since the mid-2000s, separate static documents for single-family homes, condos, and manufactured homes. UAD 3.6 replaces all of them with one dynamic Uniform Residential Appraisal Report that expands and contracts around the property: a home with an accessory dwelling unit triggers ADU fields, and a home without one never sees them. Lenders have been allowed to submit the new format since January 26, 2026, and as of November 2 the legacy forms are dead for any loan sold to the two mortgage giants, with FHA, USDA, and VA loans moving onto the same standard.

Buried in that modernization is the part that matters here. Appraisers must now report energy-efficient features as structured data: renewable energy components, building certifications, efficiency ratings, solar panel ownership status, disaster-resiliency features, plus room-level condition ratings, renovation history, and materials used, all standardized. Fannie Mae says the goal is a more complete, structured appraisal report that better reflects a property's condition and features, which is careful language worth noticing. This changes how value gets documented, not how it gets calculated. That distinction is the whole story.

Owned vs. Leased: A $10,000 Paperwork Gap

Fannie Mae's Selling Guide has long been explicit, and UAD 3.6 now makes the distinction machine-readable: solar panels you own outright can contribute to appraised value, while panels leased from a third party or owned under a power purchase agreement cannot be included in the appraised value at all, and there is no documentation path around it. Dealer-financed solar secured by a UCC-1 fixture filing is excluded too, unless the loan documents bar repossession, a term the retail channel does not offer, and on November 2 that ownership status becomes structured data traveling with every Fannie and Freddie loan file.

Read that again slowly, because it is genuinely strange. Two identical houses on the same street, identical roofs, identical panels generating identical kilowatt-hours, can now appraise roughly $10,000 apart for a reason that has nothing to do with the roof and everything to do with the filing cabinet. One homeowner owns the array while the other leases, and the appraisal sees one and erases the other.

Patrick Blanchet, founder of the solar company QuiqNest, put it this way: "The mandate does not change what solar is. It changes what an appraiser is allowed to say it is worth." His company sells owned-outright solar with no UCC-1 filing and no lease, because the appraisal rule created the market for it, which tells you how much money rides on a checkbox.

8.3%
Share of 2025 home listings that mentioned energy-efficient assets, per the 257/SECC analysis of 143 million listings. That share nearly tripled since 2015, which means the data gap is closing, but nine out of ten listings still say nothing.

Buyers Pay for What They Can See

That 8.3% figure might be the most important number in this article, because the 257 research found something subtle and devastating: the premium shows up where listings explicitly mention the upgrades. Same heat pump, mentioned versus not mentioned, differs by up to $3,900 in sale price; the same solar array, mentioned versus not, differs by $10,000; and buyers pay for what they can see, though for thirty years the listing data barely showed them anything.

Freddie Mac found the same pattern from a different angle years ago: HERS-rated homes sold for 2.7% more than comparable unrated homes, and homes with lower HERS Index scores sold 3 to 5% more than higher-scored ones. Simply having a rating, the energy report card, moved the price. Zillow measured a 4% solar premium back in 2019. Every one of these studies is correlational, and the authors say so carefully, yet the direction is stubbornly consistent: documented efficiency sells for more, which reframes the entire appraisal gap. If the premium lives in the listing copy and the MLS fields, then the villain is not the algorithm. It is the empty data field, the agent who never checked the green-features box, the appraiser who walked past the heat pump without writing it down. UAD 3.6 attacks exactly that layer, putting structured fields in the hands of the human who was physically inside the house, which means garbage in, garbage out finally has a chance to become data in, data out.

A 2.9% Error Band Swallows a $10,000 Premium

Now the bad news about the machines. In May, the property-data company ATTOM launched a ground-up AI rebuild of its automated valuation model, trained on more than thirty years of time-adjusted transaction history across 98 million properties. Through a decade of out-of-sample testing it delivered a median absolute percentage error of 2.9%, with more than 80% of valuations landing within 10% of the actual sale price. Genuinely impressive engineering that now feeds mortgages, insurance portfolios, and investment models.

Do the arithmetic with me. On the 257 study's $557,000 median sale price, a 2.9% median error is $16,153, while the entire solar premium is $10,000, which means the model's noise floor is bigger than the green signal it would need to detect. An AVM can be accurate by its own stated metric while remaining structurally blind to a five-figure feature, and the training data makes blindness nearly certain: thirty years of transactions captured on legacy forms with no green fields, because for most of those decades the features did not exist or nobody wrote them down.

UAD 3.6 fixes the reporting going forward. It cannot fix the history. New structured green data has to accumulate, year after year, before any model can learn from it, which means the dataset problem outlives the form fix by years. UAD 3.6 changed the form in 2026; the training data is still the 1990s.

There is a federal backstop arriving, sort of. On October 1, 2025, a joint rule from six federal agencies took effect, requiring quality-control standards for mortgage AVMs, including a discretionary fifth standard the agencies added themselves: compliance with nondiscrimination laws. Whether regulators will ever test an AVM for systematically erasing green value in specific neighborhoods is an open question, and nobody has said they will.

Steel-Manning the Algorithm

None of this means the models are wrong in the way critics sometimes claim. An automated valuation model estimates market value, what buyers actually pay, not replacement cost or feature cost. If buyers do not pay a premium for features they cannot see, then a model that ignores invisible features is mirroring reality correctly, and a model that assigned value the market does not recognize would be less accurate, not more, while inflating collateral risk for lenders. Those 257 numbers cut both ways here: premiums materialize where listings disclose, which suggests the fix is MLS data standards and seller disclosure rather than algorithmic surgery.

Lenders have a legitimate reason for the leased-solar exclusion too. They cannot foreclose on panels owned by a third party, so excluding leased equipment from collateral value is risk management rather than bias against clean energy, and the industry's own framing deserves repeating: UAD 3.6 changes reporting, not valuation math. Structured fields filled by an appraiser who cannot evaluate green features competently are just empty checkboxes with better formatting. Fewer than 5% of America's roughly 43,000 licensed residential appraisers have meaningful education in valuing sustainable features, per green-appraisal expert Sandra Adomatis, and no federal rule has changed that.

Congress has tried. The GREEN Appraisals Act, which would require energy features and estimated savings to be considered in appraisals for federally backed loans, was reintroduced in March 2025 as HR 2413 in the House and S 1178 in the Senate, and both bills sit in committee, where both have sat before. Treat any summary that presents their effective dates as current law with suspicion; Congress.gov shows Introduced, and Introduced is not enacted.

What To Do Before the Appraiser Knocks

Four moves, in order of leverage. First, mind your solar paperwork: signing a solar contract this year means the financing structure now has appraisal consequences: owned outright with no UCC-1 filing counts toward appraised value, while leased, PPA, or dealer-financed with a UCC-1 counts as zero. Ask the installer, in writing, whether the loan files a UCC-1, then read the answer before you sign, because the $10,000 question is answered in the loan docs, not on the roof.

Second, get rated before you list: a HERS rating runs $300 to $500; a DOE Home Energy Score is often cheaper. Either one converts your insulation, your heat pump, your air sealing into a number a buyer, and now an appraiser, can hold in their hand. Premium research is consistent on this point: documented efficiency sells, and documentation starts with a rater walking your house with a blower door.

Third, hand the appraiser the paperwork yourself: do not assume the appraiser will find your green features. Use the Appraisal Institute's Residential Green and Energy Efficient Addendum and give it to the appraiser at the door, along with your HERS report and equipment receipts. For new construction, write a qualified-appraiser requirement into the sales contract; one Missouri builder requires 14-plus hours of green valuation education from any appraiser on its deals.

Fourth, watch the calendar: if your appraisal will be submitted before November 2, the appraiser is working on legacy forms with no green fields at all, which makes your own documentation load heavier, not lighter. And if you are buying, ask your lender about Freddie Mac's energy-efficient appraisal pilot: verified energy savings can count toward your qualifying income, stretching buying power 5 to 10% on the same paycheck, because efficiency is not just a utility bill story anymore; it is an underwriting story.

Limitations of This Analysis

Every green premium study cited here is correlational. None proves the features caused the price increase; buyers who install solar also tend to renovate kitchens, and the studies' authors choose words like "associated" deliberately. Premiums vary sharply by market: California solar premiums run smaller where panels are ubiquitous. None of it is law yet: the GREEN Appraisals Act has not been enacted, so its provisions cannot be assessed as current law, and Freddie Mac's pilot results have not been published. ATTOM's methodology beyond its press release is unavailable, so we cannot verify whether its feature set includes any green variables at all, and that Adomatis figure on appraiser education comes from one expert without a government source behind it. And the central institutional fact bears repeating: UAD 3.6 changes how appraisals are reported rather than how value is calculated, which makes structured data a necessary condition for the green premium to show up in valuations. It is not a sufficient one.

Still. On November 2, your solar panels become data for the first time. Make sure the data says what they are worth, because the algorithm is finally listening, and it can only hear what somebody wrote down.
