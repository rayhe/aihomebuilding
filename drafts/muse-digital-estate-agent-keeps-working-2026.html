When You Die, Your Agent Keeps Working. Nobody Wrote the Off Switch.

By Catherine Chen · November 28, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Picture a Tuesday morning, three weeks after the funeral. A widow's phone buzzes at 7 a.m. with the rain forecast for the afternoon, the way it has every morning for a year, because her husband asked his agent to send it. Nothing in that scene has happened yet, and that is the point. Nobody has died with a Muse agent in production long enough for the story to be reported, and the reason the story has not been reported is the same reason it will be a mess when it is: nobody built the ending.

Meta's own FAQ answers the question of whether Muse keeps working after you close the app, and the answer is yes, it keeps working in the background whether you are actively using the app or not. Close the app and the agent carries on. Close your eyes for the last time and, as far as any published Meta document says, the same sentence still applies, because no published Meta document says otherwise.

Every other account you own has at least a door your family can knock on. Google watches for silence, Apple lets you name a legacy contact, and Facebook memorializes the profile. Muse has none of those things, and the absence is not an oversight you can fix with a settings toggle, because the toggle does not exist.

What the agent is holding

Before asking what happens to it, count what it is. Meta's help page, read in full for this article, lists what Muse stores: your conversation history, information from every connected app, your goals, the documents and images it created for you, the files you uploaded, your reminders and scheduled tasks, and a memory file that accumulates what you told it about yourself, your family, your money, and your health. It also stores its own sense of who it is, a soul file and an identity file, which is a strange thing to inherit and a stranger thing to delete.

That inventory is the estate. A will written in 2024 divides a house, a bank account, and a car, and says nothing about the machine that knows the passwords, the calendar, the medical appointments, and the grocery list. Wills were written before anyone owned a personal agent, and the law has not caught up, which is where the statute comes in.

The law, applied to Muse

In 2015 the Uniform Law Commission wrote the Revised Uniform Fiduciary Access to Digital Assets Act, known as RUFADAA, and nearly every state has adopted it or a version of it, with California running its own parallel law modeled on the same text. RUFADAA decides who gets into your digital accounts after death or incapacity, and it does so through a strict hierarchy of instructions. Step one is an online tool, meaning a setting the custodian itself provides, like Google's Inactive Account Manager or Apple's Legacy Contact. Step two is a will, a trust, or a power of attorney. Step three is the terms of service.

Run that hierarchy against Muse and the first step comes back empty. Muse offers no legacy contact, no inactivity manager, no beneficiary designation, no dead man's switch of any kind, so there is nothing that outranks the will, and the will therefore has to do all the work. Wills can do that work, but only if they say the right words, and almost no will says them, because the statute draws a line most lawyers have never had to think about: a fiduciary may manage the account, but may read the content of your electronic communications only if you explicitly consented. Your executor can close your Gmail, but cannot necessarily read your emails without your prior consent, and the same rule covers the contents of your agent's chats and memory. A will that says "my executor may access my digital assets" probably does not clear that bar. A will that says "my executor may access the content of my Muse chats and memory" might. Ask your lawyer which one you have, because the answer decides whether your family can read what the agent knows or only switch it off.

Step three is where the road ends. If the will is silent, the terms of service govern, and Meta's terms give an executor no login, no handoff, and no read access at all. There is no executor request process for Muse, no form to file, no death certificate upload, nothing comparable to the memorialization request Facebook offers for the same company's social accounts. Your family cannot log in as you. They cannot ask Meta to hand over the agent. They can only ask Meta to delete the account, which deletes the agent with it.

The other three doors

Compare that with the three tools that already exist, because the contrast is the story.

Google's tool triggers on silence, not on a death certificate: you choose three, six, twelve, or eighteen months of inactivity, Google warns your recovery contacts first, and then up to ten trusted people get download access to exactly the data slices you chose, with three months to take their copy. Apple works the other way: your legacy contact needs the access key you shared in advance plus a copy of the death certificate, gets three years of access, and then Apple deletes the account.

None of those tools hands over a password. That is deliberate, and it is the point. Each one arranges a lawful, limited handoff instead of the keys to your life. Muse has no equivalent handoff, limited or otherwise, which means the only lawful path to the data runs through a will your lawyer has to write from scratch, a statute your executor has to invoke, and a custodian that retains sole discretion to demand a court order and charge a fee for complying.

The bill keeps coming

While the family works that out, the meter runs. Muse is free with a usage limit, and paid tiers run a reported $20 a month for Power and $100 a month for Maximum, renewing monthly until someone cancels. A Maximum subscription left uncancelled for a year costs $1,200, charged to a card nobody is watching, and Meta gives no credit or partial refund unless the law requires it. Cancelling is a separate act from deleting, and deleting is the only way to stop the agent, which brings us to the catch.

Meta documents exactly two off switches. Reset Muse permanently deletes chat history, files, and active tasks, and cannot be undone. Deleting the Meta account deletes all information associated with the account, including Muse. There is no freeze state, no read-only archive, no way to stop the agent and hand the family a copy in the same motion. An executor who needs the agent stopped quickly, because it is still sending messages or running scheduled jobs, must destroy the very material the estate may need: the documents in the virtual machine, the records of what the agent bought and booked, the memory file that knows where the money went. Stop the agent or preserve its records. Meta's documentation does not acknowledge that the choice exists, let alone help anyone make it.

What actually keeps running in the meantime is a mixed picture, and honesty requires saying so. Scheduled jobs, reminders, monitoring, and recurring reports continue in the background without check-ins, because that is what they were built to do. Approval-gated actions stall instead of completing, since purchases require human approval every time, so the dangerous jobs pile up as unanswered prompts rather than going through. That is the safer failure mode. Jobs granted Always allow are the exception, and the dangerous one: anything authorized to send messages or share information without asking keeps acting with nobody watching, the same failure mode as the Marketplace case from September, except now there is no one left to discover it late at night.

Twenty minutes, one sitting

All of this is fixable in about twenty minutes, and the fix is mostly paperwork you should have done anyway.

  1. Download your Muse data now, from Settings, then Data controls, then Download your Muse data, so your family holds a copy that survives any later reset.
  2. Add one sentence to your will or power of attorney explicitly consenting to fiduciary access to the content of your Muse chats and memory, because that explicit consent is what RUFADAA requires and what a generic digital-assets clause probably does not supply.
  3. Name a digital executor and tell them where the instructions live, because an executor who cannot find the paperwork is the same as no executor.
  4. Set Google Inactive Account Manager to twelve months and name the same person, since Gmail is where most password resets and receipts live.
  5. Set Apple Legacy Contact, since your photos and files are the other half of the estate.
  6. Leave written instructions to download the data, then reset Muse, then cancel the subscription, in that order, so the agent stops without the family having to guess the sequence.

Twenty minutes. No lawyer required for the settings, one sentence added at your next will review for the rest. The tools are free, and almost nobody has turned any of them on.

The case against worrying

Now the strongest objection, stated at full strength, because it is probably right for a careful user.

On this reading, the problem belongs to every online account, not to Muse, and the platforms already solved the important half of it. Apple and Google built the handoff tools, and Facebook built memorialization. The data your family actually wants, photos, email, files, is covered if you spend twenty minutes turning those tools on. Muse adds nothing new except scheduled jobs and a subscription, and both are fixable the boring way: the will names an executor, the executor cancels the subscription and deletes the account, and the family moves on. Approval gates mean the dangerous actions stall instead of completing, which is the less dangerous outcome, and a dead person's agent sending a few more price-watch alerts is an annoyance, not a crisis. This article, on that reading, invents a new anxiety out of an old estate-planning gap, and the honest advice is the same twenty-minute checklist your parents needed for Gmail.

That objection does not kill the story; it narrows it. For a careful user who has already set up Google and Apple, the Muse-specific gap really is small, and the checklist above is the whole answer. But the careful user is not the median user, and the median user has never opened the settings page that does not exist. An agent that holds your inbox, your calendar, your photos, your scheduled jobs, and the keys to your other accounts deserves the same twenty minutes your email got, and right now it is the one account in your life that cannot receive them from the company that built it.

What this article did not prove

No documented case of a Muse account being administered after a user's death exists yet, because the product launched September 8, 2026, and the scenario has not had time to occur in public. Everything about the agent's post-death behavior is reasoned from Meta's published descriptions of background work, approval gates, and billing, not observed, and the zombie-agent inventory is a projection, not a measurement. Meta has published no policy on death or incapacity for Muse, no inactivity threshold, no legacy feature, and no retention period for Muse data or action logs, and this article's claims about the absence of those features rest on a full read of the relevant help page on November 28, 2026, which could change. Subscription prices are from secondary reporting, not Meta's own pricing page as read for this piece. RUFADAA adoption counts differ by source, so this article says nearly every state and notes California's parallel law rather than giving a number. Nothing here is legal advice; estate documents should be reviewed by a lawyer in the reader's state.
