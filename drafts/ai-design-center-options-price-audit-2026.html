Your Builder Charged $4,200 for the Quartz Upgrade. The Builder's Own Math Says It Cost $2,940.

By Frank DeLuca · September 11, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Your design consultant slides the option sheet across the table like it is a menu. Four appointments, three hours each, and at the end of the fourth you sign a document that adds somewhere between five and fifteen percent to the price of your house. Nobody at that table will tell you what any of it actually costs to install. That silence is the business model.

Shea Homes, one of the country's largest private builders, has said its buyers typically spend ten percent or more of the home's value on upgrades. One Shea design center was projected to do $600,000 a month in options before it opened. It did over a million, immediately, and none of it is a secret. "The design center is a profit center for sure," the company's design executive told ProBuilder.

A large production builder's division manager told ProBuilder the company targets a thirty percent margin on every option, upgrade, and design-center selection. To hit a thirty percent margin on the selling price, you must mark up your installed cost by 42.85 percent. Margin is measured against the selling price while markup is measured against cost, and the trade publication walked through the arithmetic at length because builder staffs keep confusing the two, which tells you everything about how carefully these numbers are guarded from the buyer.

Do the reverse math on a $4,200 quartz countertop option, the single most common line item on any design-center sheet, and watch what happens to the number. Divide by 1.4285 and the builder's implied installed cost is about $2,940. The other $1,260 is margin. A fact you are allowed to know. One nobody will volunteer.

The Audit Workflow

No single product audits a builder option sheet end to end, so buyers are assembling the workflow themselves out of general-purpose AI tools. Home Depot launched an AI Material List Builder in January 2026 that turns a typed description, a voice note, or a pasted list into a draft material list with product recommendations, live pricing, and inventory availability. It was built for Pro Xtra contractors, but anyone with a tape measure and a question can use it. Photo-to-estimate apps now generate itemized estimates from jobsite photos with real-time material pricing. Someone is even selling a Contractor Quote Comparison Kit on Etsy, a PDF of structured prompts that walks a homeowner through feeding contractor quotes into an AI assistant to compare scope line by line.

None of these tools know your builder's option sheet. All of them can price the aftermarket alternative, and the aftermarket alternative is the number that matters. Photograph every page of the option sheet, feed it to an AI tool with your zip code, and ask for two things per line: the implied installed cost at a thirty percent builder margin, and the aftermarket installed range from live material pricing. What comes back is not a quote. It is a sort.

Buy the Bones, Defer the Finishes

Here is the audit, run on a $550,000 base price with $55,000 in options, the ten percent Shea reports as typical. At a thirty percent margin, the builder's installed cost on those options is roughly $38,500, which means about $16,500 of the option sheet is gross profit before the design center's own overhead, so now sort every line into two buckets.

Bucket one holds the things that are structurally impossible or economically absurd to do later: nine-foot ceilings, a bumped-out great room, the third garage bay, pre-wired low voltage, the 220-volt EV circuit, the gas stub at the range, the covered patio slab, the upgraded electrical panel. Buy all of that from the builder and do not flinch, because the aftermarket price is either infinite or involves a concrete saw.

Bucket two is finishes. Quartz counters, luxury vinyl plank, backsplash tile, cabinet hardware, light fixtures, closet systems, window treatments, upgraded interior paint. Every one of these can be installed after closing by a contractor you chose yourself, on a schedule you control, often at the builder's own implied cost or below it.

Take the quartz. Builder price $4,200 against an implied installed cost of about $2,940. An independent fabricator installing the same stone after closing charges roughly $3,000 to $3,600 in most metros, per the material-plus-labor math the AI tools assemble in about ninety seconds. You save somewhere between $600 and $1,200, you pick the slab yourself instead of choosing from the builder's three approved samples, and the fabricator who installs it answers to you rather than to a production schedule.

The Financing Trap Inside the Math

An honest version of this math includes the financing, because options roll into a thirty-year mortgage while aftermarket work is cash or a HELOC. At six and a half percent, $4,200 of quartz costs about $26.55 a month for thirty years, or roughly $9,560 all in. The $3,300 aftermarket job paid in cash costs $3,300. The mortgage does not make the builder option cheaper. It makes it feel cheaper, which is exactly why the design center prices in monthly payments. ProBuilder's own sales training tells builders to sell options as dollars per month rather than dollars, and the consultant across the table has practiced that sentence.

The Case for Buying Everything, at Full Strength

Nobody is suggesting you boycott the design center. Sort the sheet instead, because the steelman for buying it all is stronger than the audit crowd admits. One throat to choke: when the builder installs the counters, the counters sit under the builder's warranty, and there is no finger-pointing between the fabricator and the GC when a seam fails in year two. Schedule certainty has a price too: the Houzz 2024 study puts median aftermarket renovation spend at $24,000, up sixty percent since 2020, and finding a contractor willing to do a small post-close job in a hot market can take longer than the build itself. Financing cuts the other way for cash-constrained buyers: rolling $10,000 of options into the mortgage at six and a half percent beats putting it on a credit card at twenty-four while you wait for the HELOC to fund. And the AI side of this story deserves its own discount, because AI price estimates hallucinate, regional labor rates swing forty percent between metros, and no prompt template replaces three competing bids.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

Stated plainly. The thirty percent margin target comes from a ProBuilder trade article quoting one large builder's division manager, not from a published industry survey, and real option margins run roughly twenty-five to forty percent depending on the builder and the market. No public dataset of builder option-sheet prices exists, so the $55,000 worked example is a model built on Shea's ten-percent figure rather than a measurement. Aftermarket costs vary brutally by metro; the quartz math that saves $900 in Phoenix might save nothing in the Bay Area. I could not verify what share of buyers actually negotiate design-center credits, though agents report credits are common on inventory homes, which would change the effective option price on every line. The AI tools price materials well and labor badly, so treat every AI-generated installed price as a starting bid rather than a quote.

Run the Audit Before the Fourth Appointment

If you are buying new construction, sequence it the way a project manager would. First, photograph every page of the option sheet and feed it to an AI tool with one instruction: sort each line into must-buy-now versus can-do-later, with aftermarket installed-price ranges for your zip code. Second, buy the bones: structure, electrical capacity, plumbing rough-ins, and anything involving concrete or drywall you would have to cut open later. Third, defer the finishes, take the design-center credit if the builder offers one on an inventory home, and hire your own trades after closing. Homes.com's buyer survey found final cost and upgrades are the first questions buyers ask, and this is the first time they have had tooling to answer them before signing.

Ask the design consultant one question nobody asks: which options keep the full builder warranty if I defer them, and which become my problem. Get the answer in writing. A verbal promise from a design consultant evaporates the day the consultant transfers to another community. By the company's own admission to ProBuilder, the design center is a profit center. There is nothing wrong with a builder making thirty percent on options, just as there is nothing wrong with you knowing it before you sign.
