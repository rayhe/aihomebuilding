Your Gas Line Is Yellow. Lightning Doesn't Have to Hit Your House to Set It on Fire.

By Catherine Chen September 11, 2026

On July 23, 2018, Howard County firefighter Nathan Flynn entered a burning house in Maryland and never came out; the floor collapsed beneath him, trapping him in a basement crawl space where the fire was already burning. His widow later alleged in court that the fire started when lightning perforated the home's TracPipe, a flexible stainless gas line, releasing propane into the structure, and OmegaFlex eventually settled with her. Per the complaint, the company knew its product caused as many as 75 lightning-induced fires a year and sold it anyway.

Read that number again, seventy-five fires a year from lightning striking gas pipe, because that figure comes from the widow's complaint, not from an industry critic.

That pipe is called corrugated stainless steel tubing, CSST for short. It has been run through American homes since the 1990s because it is fast, flexible, and cheap: snake it through a wall cavity in minutes instead of threading rigid black iron pipe joint by joint. Roughly a billion feet of it has been installed. A 2012 Senate resolution put the figure at more than six million homes and businesses. Its wall is about a hundredth of an inch thick, wrapped in a plastic jacket. Its first generation was yellow. Yellow is the color that should make you climb into your attic with a flashlight, and the physics of what goes wrong with it are brutally simple.

A lightning strike does not need to hit your house. A nearby strike to a tree, a pole, or bare ground energizes every metal system in the structure: the electrical wiring, the plumbing, and the gas piping. Current travels along the thin stainless wall of the CSST until it finds somewhere to jump, arcing to another metal component. Where it jumps off, the arc burns through the tubing wall and gas leaks out of the perforation, which sometimes finds an ignition source immediately and sometimes waits in silence for a spark.

What the Code Knows, and Where It Stops

Building codes did respond: in 2009, bonding requirements went into the fuel gas code, and CSST gas piping systems have had to be bonded to the electrical service grounding electrode system ever since, with a jumper no smaller than 6 AWG copper wire, run no longer than 75 feet, attached with listed bonding devices. That is a wire, a clamp, and an electrician's hour. Cheap.

It also turned out to be insufficient, or at least unproven. Representing the people who walk into the aftermath, the International Association of Fire Chiefs has a position paper on this whose language is not that of a minor technical dispute: efforts to reduce fires by introducing bonding requirements, it says, "have not significantly reduced the occurrence of fires involving products that meet the current product standards." The association cites anecdotal evidence from state and local fire officials suggesting CSST may increase the likelihood of fires by 10 times in lightning-prone areas. Note the word "anecdotal." The precise number of incidents is, by the IAFC's own admission, difficult to estimate. Fire marshals do not have a national registry of perforated gas lines.

Then there is the question of who is even responsible for the fix. In Massachusetts, the board that regulates this stuff issued a policy stating that installing a bonding jumper "falls outside the scope of plumbing and gas fitting," meaning the plumber who ran your gas line cannot be held responsible for the bonding. That belongs to the licensed electrician, under an electrical permit. Two trades, two permits, one wire, and in the middle sits the homeowner who has never heard of any of it.

A Better Pipe in a Different Color

Credit where it is due. Eventually, the industry's answer was a better product rather than a better rule. OmegaFlex introduced CounterStrike in 2004: CSST with an electrically conductive black jacket that the company says is up to 400 times more resistant to damage from electrical arcing than traditional CSST. It is listed to the arc-resistant jacket requirements in ANSI LC 1-2016 and evaluated under ICC-ES PMG 1058 and IAPMO ER 0227 for lightning resistance "without additional bonding (unless required by local codes)." In 2011, OmegaFlex stopped selling its yellow-jacketed product in the US entirely. Gastite sells FlashShield+, a metallically shielded black-jacketed CSST listed to ANSI LC1 and ICC-ES PMG LC1027, marketed as proven against both electrical fault arcs and lightning strikes.

So the visual shorthand works like this: yellow jacket means the legacy product, which the fuel gas code says must be bonded, and which the fire chiefs say keeps burning houses down anyway. Black jacket means the arc-resistant generation, which generally needs no additional bonding unless your local code says otherwise. Color alone is not a code determination, and you should not treat it as one. But it is the fastest triage tool a homeowner has.

That triage matters because the product choice was never the buyer's. In a 2020 federal ruling out of Missouri, homeowner Brian Immekus testified that he "didn't know in advance that TracPipe was going to be installed during the construction of [his] home," that he "didn't make the decision," and that OmegaFlex "made no statement" to him about bonding. His builder picked the pipe. Millions of buyers inherited the decision with the house.

What the Lawsuits Say That the Codes Won't

When codes are a patchwork and incident data is anecdotal, lawsuits become the record. That record is long.

A 2004 class action against OmegaFlex, Parker Hannifin, Titeflex, and Ward settled for $29 million. Qualifying property owners received vouchers of $75 to $160 for a bonding jumper installation, or $200 to $2,000 for a lightning protection system, depending on location and building size. All of them denied any wrongdoing. Its claims deadline was September 5, 2007, and the coverage window was narrow. If you bought your house in 2010 with yellow CSST already inside, that settlement did nothing for you.

Then came the subrogation era. Insurers paid for burned houses and then sued the manufacturer to get the money back. State Farm sued OmegaFlex after a lightning strike on May 9, 2015 caused a fire at the Crocketts' Arkansas home, which was plumbed with yellow-jacketed TracPipe. Oklahoma Farm Bureau sued after a July 11, 2020 lightning strike caused an attic fire in the Diels' home; the insurer had paid to rebuild the entire house. Celeste Flynn's wrongful-death suit settled. Each case is a data point that never made it into a national incident database because no such database exists.

Here is the asymmetry the article keeps circling: a retrofit the 2007 settlement priced at $75 to $160, one electrician visit, one listed clamp, a run of 6 AWG wire, stands against insurers funding complete home rebuilds. Between those two numbers sits the story of every legacy-risk regulation that relies on homeowners to discover the danger on their own.

Where AI Actually Helps

This is a verification problem wearing a code-compliance costume, and verification is what cameras are for. Home inspectors already photograph attics, crawlspaces, and utility runs on nearly every transaction. Pre-drywall inspections document every gas run before it disappears behind drywall. That classification task is almost comically tractable: yellow jacket versus black jacket is a two-class, high-contrast computer vision problem that any current image model solves from a single photo. A second pass flags the presence or absence of a bonding clamp at the gas entry point.

What you do with the classification is the genuinely new analysis. Cross-reference the jacket color and bonding status against county-level lightning flash density, the code edition the local authority has adopted, and the age of the structure, and you get a per-home lightning-fire risk score that nobody currently produces. Insurers are pricing this risk blind; their loss history shows up as subrogation lawsuits years after the fire. A risk score built from inspection photos would let an underwriter price it before the rebuild, and let a buyer negotiate the $150 fix before closing instead of discovering the attic fire after.

Skepticism writes itself here, though, and it should. No camera changes the underlying physics of thin stainless walls in lightning country; the IAFC's position is that bonding didn't fix the legacy product, and a photo of a properly bonded yellow run is a photo of a system the fire chiefs still distrust. Vision models also inherit every bias of their training data: a crawlspace in a 1962 ranch looks different from a mechanical room in a new build, and a classifier trained on clean pre-drywall photos will fail on the dusty, cobwebbed reality of a forty-year-old attic. Worst of all, an AI that confidently labels a black-jacketed run as "safe" teaches the homeowner the wrong lesson, because arc-resistant is not arc-proof, and local codes can still demand bonding on the black product. Think of the tool as a triage flashlight, not a code official.

A Case for Not Worrying

Here is the strongest case against worrying about any of this: CSST is a listed, code-compliant product installed in millions of homes that have never burned down; the fire chiefs' 10x figure rests on anecdotal reports, not a controlled study; the $29 million settlement extracted no admission of wrongdoing; OmegaFlex engineered the problem out of the product in 2004 and discontinued the legacy version in 2011; and every year that passes replaces more yellow pipe with black through normal remodeling and replacement. That risk pool is shrinking on its own, the codes caught up, and the litigation tail is just the legal system digesting the last of the legacy installations.

There is real force in that. Set against it is the arithmetic of legacy risk: six million buildings, a failure mode triggered by weather, no registry, no recall, and a settlement window that closed in 2007. Risks that shrink on average still land on specific houses, and the Flynn case shows what "lands on" can mean. Honestly, the product generation matters enormously, the installed base is a slow-burn problem measured in decades, and the cost of checking which generation your house has is one trip into the attic.

What This Means for Your Project

If you own or are buying a home built or replumbed between roughly 1990 and 2015, look at the gas piping in the attic, basement, or crawlspace. Yellow jacket: call a licensed electrician and ask whether the system is bonded to the service grounding electrode per the locally adopted fuel gas code, with a 6 AWG copper jumper no longer than 75 feet. This is electrician jurisdiction, not plumber, and it may require an electrical permit. Black jacket: confirm it is a listed arc-resistant product (ask for the listing mark, ICC-ES PMG or IAPMO evaluation report) rather than assuming the color settles it, and check whether your local code demands bonding anyway.

If you are building new, specify arc-resistant CSST by listing, not by color, in the gas rough-in spec, and get the bonding determination in writing from the authority having jurisdiction before the drywall goes up. Photograph every gas run at pre-drywall; those photos are free, the pipe will be invisible in a week, and if anyone ever needs to know which generation went into your walls, the answer will be in the camera roll.

Your builder chose the pipe in 2006 and never told you. The fix costs less than a tank of gas used to. That is not a market failure you can wait out; it is a wire you can have installed next week.

Limitations

The IAFC's 10x fire-likelihood figure is explicitly based on anecdotal reports from state and local fire officials, not a peer-reviewed epidemiological study, and the association acknowledges the precise incident count is difficult to estimate. Manufacturer performance claims (CounterStrike's "400 times more resistant") come from manufacturer-published test data, not independent third-party testing I could verify. The $75 to $160 bonding voucher range comes from a 2007 settlement denominated in 2007 dollars; current electrician rates will differ by market. State and local code adoption varies widely, and this article does not constitute a code determination for any specific jurisdiction; the authority having jurisdiction is the final word. The proposed AI inspection-photo risk score is an original suggestion, not a deployed product, and its accuracy in real-world attics and crawlspaces is untested. Lightning flash-density data cited conceptually is not attached to a specific county-level analysis in this article.
