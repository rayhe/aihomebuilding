One Family Paid $193,000 for an ADU That Was Never Built. The Warning Signs Were Public the Whole Time.

By Catherine Chen · September 21, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

A family in Oceanside paid a contractor more than $193,000 for an ADU. Nearly two years later, the project had not begun. That is not an anecdote I picked for drama. It is in the California Contractors State License Board's own enforcement report, presented to the board in late 2024, naming the contractor and the number. That family is not getting the money back from a bond, because the bond on a California contractor's license is $25,000, which covers about thirteen cents on their dollar.

Here is what bothers me about that story. Every data point a buyer would have needed to avoid it was public before the check was written. The license board publishes complaint histories. Permit databases record every job a contractor pulls a permit for. Court records show who gets sued and how often. Secrecy was never the problem. It was synthesis: the records lived in forty different drawers, and nobody had ten minutes and a reason to open all of them.

That excuse is expiring: software now fuses those drawers for you.

What the machines actually read

BuildZoom, the largest of these aggregators, says it combines license data on 3.5 million contractors with 300 million building permits and more than 135,000 reviews from property owners. Its score for a contractor weighs license status, insurance status, verified work history, standing with consumer groups, verified reviews, and the contractor's own self-reported information, which means the contractor gets a vote in its own grade, a detail worth remembering when you read the number. In plain English: it checks whether the license is real, whether the insurance is current, how many permitted jobs the contractor has actually done, and what other owners said, which turns a background check that used to take a paralegal a week into a search box.

That last input is thinner than the marketing suggests, which matters. 135,000 reviews spread across 3.5 million contractors means fewer than four percent of contractors have a single owner review on the platform. It is mostly not star ratings. It is permits and license data. A permit is a government record that a job existed; a review is a mood. But you should know which one you are trusting.

The state's own numbers

California's license board took in 9,317 complaints in fiscal year 2024-25, settled about a third of them, and recovered $11.7 million in restitution for consumers, a number that sounds impressive until you divide it by the thousands of families behind it. Investigators took 4,245 cases; 1,493 were referred for legal action and 398 for criminal prosecution. These are not estimates; they are in the board's April 2025 enforcement committee minutes.

154x A contractor with 10 open complaints carries roughly 154 times the complaint volume of the average California licensee. The board routes those contractors to a special Multiple Offender Unit. As of April 2025, only 22 such contractors had faced formal accusations.

Let me show the arithmetic, since methodology transparency is not optional here. Board records show 242,847 active licensees as of December 2025 and 15,748 complaints involving licensed contractors in 2024, two numbers the board publishes without fanfare and almost nobody bothers to divide. That is 0.065 complaints per licensee per year. Ten complaints divided by 0.065 is about 154. Ten open complaints is the board's own threshold for "this contractor is a different species," at which point the case bypasses normal intake and goes straight to the Multiple Offender Unit, a unit created in late 2023 specifically because repeat offenders had learned to outlast the normal process. Between the unit's creation in late 2023 and April 2025, it handled roughly 1,359 complaints and got accusations filed against 22 licensees, covering 84 consumer complaints, a conversion rate that tells you the unit is a scalpel, not a net. Twenty-two, out of a quarter million licensees. That funnel is narrow by design, and that is exactly why the buyer's own ten minutes matter more than the board's ten months, because enforcement is a backstop and you are the front line.

The ADU warning that was already filed

That same enforcement packet named the pattern: Anchored Tiny Homes had its license revoked in November 2024, Two other ADU builders were generating dozens of complaints each. Board staff told the public that a contractor's $25,000 bond is the financial backstop, and confirmed there is no general fund that makes consumers whole when a contractor collapses, which means the state's answer to "what if they take my deposit and vanish" is, effectively, good luck. Read that sentence again if you are about to hand someone a six-figure deposit. California's consumer protection apparatus, at full stretch, recovered $11.7 million in a year. That is real money and real work, and it is also about sixty Oceanside families.

What to do about it

The ten-minute builder background check

  1. Look up the license: every state has a lookup; California's shows license status, bond, workers' comp, and complaint disclosure. A suspended license or a disclosed complaint history is not a vibe; it is a record, and it is free.
  2. Pull the fused record: BuildZoom's contractor pages show the score inputs, meaning license standing, permit history volume, and reviews, so read the inputs, not just the number. A high score built on 200 permits means something. Built on three, it is a rumor.
  3. Search the courts: county superior court portals let you search civil filings by name. A builder sued twice in five years by owners is telling you something a score cannot.
  4. Do the bond math: $25,000 in California, and your deposit is probably larger. Size your payments to completed, inspected milestones, never to promises.
  5. Ask two warranty questions: how many warranty claims per 100 homes last year, and who performs the warranty work, your own crews or the lowest-bid sub? Vague answers are answers.
  6. Visit a current job site: no database replaces standing in a half-built house and looking at the framing. Scores are a filter, not a verdict.

Skepticism

Now the strongest case against everything above, stated at full strength.

First, these scores are only as good as the records, and the records are lumpy: permits miss unpermitted work, courts lag by months, reviews cover a sliver of contractors, and the model cannot tell a craftsman from a ghost. Permit databases miss unpermitted work by definition, which is precisely the work most likely to be defective, and court records lag by months. Review coverage, as noted, is under four percent. A contractor who does beautiful work for repeat clients and never gets reviewed is invisible to the model, while a volume builder with a marketing department can look polished on paper, because the model cannot distinguish a craftsman with no web presence from a ghost, only a paper trail from the absence of one. Paperwork is what the model sees. Not craftsmanship.

Second, a clean record can be a new license, not a clean history. Contractors who lose licenses reincorporate under new entities with alarming regularity; the board's own Multiple Offender Unit exists because repeat offenders kept operating, which is the polite way of saying the system kept losing track of them. A score attached to a two-year-old entity tells you nothing about the twenty-year-old operator behind it. Check the people, not just the paperwork: names of the qualifier and officers, cross-referenced against the old entity, because licenses are cheap and reputations are supposed to be expensive.

Third, the numbers the aggregators advertise are company-reported. Nobody has independently audited the 300-million-permit figure, and the score formula is proprietary, which means you cannot verify what it weights or why, which makes the score an opinion wearing a database's clothes. A proprietary score is an opinion with a database: useful, but an opinion.

Fourth, no regulator treats any of this as a license action, and none should. A low score is not proof of wrongdoing, and a high score is not a warranty. Complaint disclosure rules decide what the public sees and when; not every complaint becomes public, and dismissed complaints generally do not follow a contractor around. That is a feature of due process, and it also means the absence of a public complaint is not the presence of quality.

What this analysis did not prove

My 154x figure comes from the board's published complaint and licensee counts, and it assumes complaints distribute evenly enough for the average to be meaningful; in reality a small number of contractors absorb a large share, which if anything makes the 10-complaint threshold more alarming, not less. Permit and contractor counts are BuildZoom's, unaudited. CSLB figures are California-only, and California publishes more than most states, so your mileage will vary in Texas or Florida. And the Oceanside case comes from the board's enforcement packet as reported; it is an allegation history, not a court judgment, and I am using it as an illustration of visible risk, not a verdict on any company.

Still, the records exist, the fusion exists, and the ten minutes exist. What stands between a buyer and the builder's paper trail is the habit of not looking, and the industry has spent decades cultivating that habit. Habits are cheaper to fix than foundations.
