Your Timeclock Photographs Your Face at 6:58 a.m. In Illinois, That Photo Costs $1,000.

By Marcus Washington · October 7, 2026

It is 6:58 on a cold October morning in Elgin, Illinois, and a framer is sitting in his truck holding his phone at arm's length like a teenager, waiting for a spinning wheel to become a green checkmark. His app wants three things before it will admit he is at work: his location inside the digital fence the company drew around the lot, a photo of his face that matches the one on file, and a cost code for the task he is about to do. Two of the three are fighting him. GPS is drifting. His breath is fogging the lens.

He is not paid for any of this. The clock starts when the app says so.

This is the new front door of the residential job site. Paper timecards, which let foremen pencil in whatever they wanted and let subs "forget" overtime with a shrug, are being replaced by geofenced, face-scanning phone apps that record exactly when a worker enters and leaves the site. Vendors sell them as the end of buddy punching and payroll fraud. They are not wrong about the problem. Wage theft is endemic in residential construction, and the numbers are uglier than most builders will say out loud: researchers at the Illinois and Midwest Economic Policy Institutes found that roughly one in five construction workers in Wisconsin, Minnesota, and Illinois face wage theft or payroll fraud, about 100,000 of 538,000 workers, costing taxpayers more than $362 million a year. A 2026 working paper from the UMass Amherst Labor Center calls it an epidemic in residential construction, finding that contractors who bled money in the Great Recession replaced their employees with misclassified "independent contractors" and cut labor costs by 30 percent. Between 2017 and 2020, enforcement agencies and class-action lawyers recovered $3.24 billion in stolen wages, and the Economic Policy Institute calls that a small fraction of what was taken.

Residential is where the theft concentrates. A landmark national study found 12.7 percent of residential construction workers suffered a minimum-wage violation in a single week, 70.5 percent of those working more than 40 hours were denied overtime, and 72.2 percent were not paid for work performed before or after their scheduled shift. That last number is the one to hold in your head, because the app era was supposed to fix it, and instead it moved the unpaid work to the phone in the worker's hand.

What the apps actually do, and what they cost

There is now a crowded field of construction timeclocks, and the honest ones have published their prices. busybusy runs $9.99 per user per month plus a $40 admin license, with GPS breadcrumb tracking and a geofence feature that requires workers to be on site to clock in. ClockShark is $40 a month plus $9 per user, with a KioskClock that does facial recognition, though only on the shared kiosk, not on personal phones. Workyard charges $6 per user plus a $50 base fee for continuous high-precision GPS. Arcoro Time, the renamed ExakTime, is $9 per employee per month and captures a FaceFront photo at clock-in for the office to review later. Connecteam covers 30 users for $29 a month. Buddy Punch, the budget option, is $4.49 per user plus a $19 base fee.

Under the hood the features rhyme: a geofence drawn around the job site that blocks or flags off-site punches, a camera check at clock-in ranging from a stored photo a manager eyeballs to an AI face match against a reference image, and cost codes that tie hours to budgets. Raken compares the clock-in selfie against a reference photo automatically. SmartBarrel sells a dedicated hardware clock with facial verification. Workyard's own help center draws a careful line, stating its photo capture cannot identify the individual from the image, which is a feature, not a bug, because identification is where the legal exposure starts.

The unpaid minutes nobody audits

Here is the part no sales deck includes. Clock-in is no longer instantaneous. It is a small ritual: open the app, wait for the GPS lock, hold the phone up for the face check, pick the cost code, confirm. On a good morning it takes two minutes. On a cold one, with gloves and a drifting signal, it takes five or six, and every one of those minutes happens before the first paid minute registers, inside a geofence the worker had to be standing in to begin the ritual at all.

Run it like a cost estimator. A 12-person crew, five minutes a day each, 240 workdays, at a $30-an-hour loaded rate. That is $7,200 a year of unrecorded time that looks an awful lot like compensable work, and if it pushes anyone over 40 hours, overtime liability stacks on top of the base claim. The federal position on this has been tightening in the worker's favor: the Wage and Hour Division's recent guidance says pre-shift compensable work performed regularly each day is "unlikely" to qualify as de minimis, precisely because, in the division's words, technological advances have made it possible to track employee work time with increasing precision. The employer owns the precise tracker. The employer is choosing not to start it.

Then there is rounding. Federal law, 29 CFR 785.48, permits rounding to the nearest five minutes, tenth, or quarter hour, but only if the practice "will not result, over a period of time, in failure to compensate the employees properly for all the time they have actually worked." It must be neutral on its face and neutral as applied. Courts have noticed that automated systems make neutrality checkable: the 8th Circuit told one employer that with automated systems there is no administrative hassle, "this is not like the old days of punch cards and hand arithmetic." A GC running a rounding policy that shaves eight minutes off every clock-in while the app holds second-level timestamps is holding the evidence of its own violation in its own dashboard. And the geofence itself has a known hole: one independent review found that on busybusy, if a worker forgets to select the assigned project when clocking in, the geofence restrictions do not apply, which means the anti-fraud feature can be walked around by accident, or on purpose.

The Illinois trap: your crew's faces are biometric data

Now the part that should worry every GC operating in Illinois, which is most of the big residential markets' supply chain. A scan of face geometry is a "biometric identifier" under the Illinois Biometric Information Privacy Act, and the law requires a written release before you collect it, a published retention schedule, and no disclosure to the vendor without consent. The Illinois Supreme Court has held that a five-year statute of limitations applies, and the claims keep coming: this year alone, a $925,000 settlement covered workers whose handprints were scanned on timeclocks, a $635,160 settlement covered finger and face scans used for timekeeping, and a $1.69 million settlement covered roughly 21,915 employees who used fingerprint time clocks, though an appeal filed in May has held up payments. Illinois reformed the law in 2024 to replace per-scan damages with a single claim per victim, and filings dropped from 427 in 2024 to 150 in 2025, but the math still bites: at $1,000 to $5,000 per person, a 40-person Illinois crew whose faces were scanned without the right written notice is a $40,000 to $200,000 exposure. The fix is a one-page written consent and a retention policy. It costs nothing. Skipping it costs everything the statute allows.

The case for the timeclock, stated at full strength

None of this is an argument for going back to paper. Buddy punching is real fraud, and a worker clocked in from his couch steals from every honest worker on the crew and from the GC paying the labor burden. GPS-verified hours are the best evidence a drywall hanger has ever had: when the sub "forgets" overtime, the worker's own app holds the record, and Department of Labor investigators have used exactly this kind of precision to win real money, like the $824,276 recovered for 680 workers from a Las Vegas drywall contractor that denied overtime to piece-rate and hourly crews in 2025. For a small GC, $500 to $1,500 a year in app fees replaces hours of Friday payroll admin and ends most pay disputes before they start, because both sides can see the same record. The geofence does not steal wages. It documents them. The problem is not the camera. The problem is everything around the camera that nobody audits.

What this does not prove

Be straight about the gaps. The BIPA settlements cited are logistics, warehousing, and software-vendor cases, not residential builders; they are applied here by analogy, and no residential-only BIPA timeclock dataset exists. Vendor pricing comes from published 2026 comparisons, not independent testing, and no vendor publishes face-match false-rejection rates, so the five-minute ritual is an observed-conditions estimate, not a measured mean. The NELP and ILEPI wage-theft figures rest on 2009 to 2021 data; the UMass 2026 paper is a working paper, not yet peer-reviewed. Courts still distinguish compensable pre-shift work from waiting around, and the $7,200 figure assumes the ritual is work, which a judge might not. Rounding law varies by circuit. And every enforcement example here is a case somebody caught, which means the true scale of what goes uncaught is, by definition, unmeasured.

What to do Monday morning

If you run a crew, start with the free stuff. Give every Illinois worker a written biometric consent form and publish a retention-and-destruction schedule before the next pay period; this is a one-page fix against a six-figure exposure. Then audit your own dashboard: export a month of raw punch times, compare them against the rounded payroll times, and check whether your rounding actually averages out, because the 8th Circuit expects you to, and the data is sitting in your own system. Count the pre-punch ritual honestly. If your geofence forces workers to stand in the lot tapping through a face check, pay from arrival, not from the green checkmark.

Shopping for a system, match the tool to the risk. A residential GC with 10 to 25 field workers who needs fraud control without legal complexity should look at the photo-capture-but-not-identification tier: Workyard's continuous GPS and explicit no-identification photo policy at roughly $6 a user is the cheapest way to get verified hours without collecting biometric identifiers at all. If you need real face matching for a big crew, Raken or SmartBarrel do it, but only with the written BIPA consent in hand, and budget the compliance like a line item, not an afterthought. Avoid any vendor whose geofence can be bypassed by forgetting to tap the project name, and get the vendor's detection and false-alarm rates in writing, because a face check that fails in the cold is a timeclock that steals minutes.

If you are the worker holding the phone in the cold truck: screenshot your own timesheets weekly, from inside the app, before payroll runs. The GPS record is the arbiter now, not the foreman's memory, and it cuts both ways. Keep the screenshots. The record that documents your hours is the same record that documents the minutes they didn't pay.
