# Research: Radon in New Construction — The $400 Fix Code Leaves Optional

**Slug:** radon-new-construction-appendix-f-2026
**Journalist:** Priya Greenwood (sustainability / green building / healthy homes)
**Angle:** Radon is the #2 cause of lung cancer in the US (21,000 deaths/yr per EPA). Installing radon-resistant features during construction costs a builder $250–$750; retrofitting costs $1,000–$2,500. But the IRC provision that would mandate it (Appendix AF) is optional — jurisdictions must actively adopt it. Meanwhile, the EPA's radon zone map that decides adoption is a 1993 county-level map, and a 2025 PNAS study built high-resolution national radon maps from massive indoor measurements, finding ~25% of Americans live in the highest-risk areas. The hook: the data now exists to target the requirement precisely (community-specific building codes), but most jurisdictions still use a 33-year-old map, and most builders still skip the $400 fix.

**Kill test:** Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes — a buyer can ask their builder for the 5 EPA RRNC features at contract signing for ~$400 in materials, and a builder in a Zone 1 county without Appendix AF adoption can verify whether local data justifies it anyway.

## Primary sources

1. **EPA, "Radon-Resistant New Construction for Home Buyers" (epa.gov/radon)** — RRNC typically costs a builder $250–$750; less than $250 if moisture-control techniques already used; retrofitting later costs more. Also: 21,000 US lung-cancer deaths/year from radon; radon is the leading cause among non-smokers. https://www.epa.gov/radon/radon-resistant-new-construction-home-buyers

2. **EPA Map of Radon Zones (epa.gov/radon)** — developed 1993 (three years after the 1990-era radon zone project; the page says 1993) using indoor measurements, geology, aerial radioactivity, soil parameters, foundation types; assigns all 3,141 counties to Zone 1/2/3. EPA warns it should not be used to decide whether individual homes need testing and recommends supplementing with local data. https://www.epa.gov/radon/epa-map-radon-zones

3. **Li et al., "High-resolution national radon maps based on massive indoor measurements in the United States," PNAS (2025), DOI 10.1073/pnas.2408084121** — new high-resolution model from massive indoor measurements; nearly 25% of Americans live in highest-risk areas; model suitable for community-specific building codes. (via phys.org coverage Jan 21, 2025: https://phys.org/news/2025-01-radongasamericanshighestareas.html)

4. **IRC 2021 Appendix AF, Radon Control Methods (codes.iccsafe.org)** — "The provisions contained in this appendix are not mandatory unless specifically referenced in the adopting ordinance." Scope: new construction in jurisdictions where radon-resistant construction is required; inclusion determined through locally available data or Zone 1 designation. https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/IRC2021P1/appendix-af-radon-control-methods

5. **Oregon Residential Specialty Code Appendix F (SB 1025, 2010 Oregon Laws)** — Oregon law mandates radon-mitigating construction in Baker, Clackamas, Hood River, Multnomah, Polk, Washington, and Yamhill counties. https://codes.iccsafe.org/content/ORRSC2021P1/appendix-f-radon-control-methods

6. **Virginia DHS radiological health: RRNC** — Virginia counties/cities in EPA Zone 1 may adopt IRC Appendix F locally; lists jurisdictions that have (Amelia, Buckingham, Louisa, Montgomery, Nottoway, Orange, Rockbridge, Shenandoah, Tazewell, Wythe counties; Blacksburg, Buena Vista, Christiansburg, Lexington, Radford, Roanoke, Salem). Notes retrofit systems are more expensive and visually intrusive (exterior fan + piping). https://www.vdh.virginia.gov/radiological-health/indoor-radon-program/radon-resistant-new-construction-rrnc/

7. **Wisconsin DHS: Radon Resistant Construction** — passive RRNC is 50%–70% of the cost of a retrofitted system (retrofit ~$1,200); passive stacks in properly built/sealed new construction typically cut indoor radon ~50% vs capped stacks; fan-powered retrofit adds ~$200/yr in energy in northern climates. https://www.dhs.wisconsin.gov/radon/radon-resistant-const.htm

8. **Fine Homebuilding: Radon-Mitigation Systems** — retrofit sub-slab depressurization $1,000–$2,000; fan operating cost $22–$110/yr at 12.5¢/kWh; older homes lack crushed-stone layer under slab, so passive rarely works as retrofit. https://www.finehomebuilding.com/project-guides/foundations-and-masonry-work/radon-mitigation-systems

9. **NJ DEP radon mitigation guide (nj.gov/dep)** — average retrofit system $1,300, range $500–$2,500; post-mitigation testing required. https://nj.gov/dep/rpp/radon/download/mitbas.pdf

## The five EPA RRNC features (from EPA / Wisconsin DHS)
1. 4-inch layer of clean coarse gravel under the slab (gas-permeable layer)
2. 6-mil polyethylene sheeting / vapor retarder over the gravel
3. 3–4 inch vent pipe from the gravel layer through the roof
4. Sealing/caulking of foundation cracks and openings (and pipe penetrations)
5. Wiring rough-in for a future inline fan (junction box in attic) — so the passive stack can become active if testing shows elevated levels

Key nuance: a *passive* RRNC stack typically halves indoor radon; it does NOT guarantee levels below 4 pCi/L. Testing after construction is still required; the fan junction box is the insurance policy. Passive-only systems in properly sealed construction are often sufficient; Wisconsin DHS data supports ~50% reduction.

## Original contribution (for the article)
- **The timing math:** EPA says RRNC costs a builder $250–$750. A buyer can offer to pay cost + markup at contract signing (before the slab is poured) — the cheapest health insurance in the build. The math: $400 at construction vs $1,300–$2,500 retrofit + $22–$110/yr fan operating cost. We will present this as a one-time contract ask with a deadline (it expires the day the vapor barrier goes under the gravel).
- **The code-adoption cross-reference:** Appendix AF is opt-in. Oregon mandates it in 7 counties by state law; Virginia lets Zone 1 jurisdictions adopt it locally (17 listed jurisdictions have); Washington includes it in IRC adoption. Most of the country has not adopted it. A builder in a Zone 1 county should not assume the code requires it.
- **The map critique (AI angle):** The EPA zone map that triggers code adoption is a 1993 county-level product (3,141 counties, 3 zones) — coarser than a single subdivision. The 2025 PNAS high-resolution model uses massive indoor measurement datasets and is explicitly positioned as suitable for community-specific building codes. Nobody in the trade press has connected this model to the Appendix AF adoption question: the same year (2025) that better risk data arrived, most jurisdictions were still using the old map to decide whether the requirement applies at all.

## Limitations to state explicitly
- Radon levels vary house-to-house even within the same neighborhood (soil, foundation, ventilation, maintenance); maps are probabilistic, not guarantees. EPA itself warns the zone map should not decide whether an individual home needs testing.
- A passive stack does not guarantee <4 pCi/L; post-construction testing is still required, and an active fan may be needed (rough-in wiring is what makes this cheap).
- Cost figures are builder-material estimates from EPA (2026 page) and state sources; contractor pricing varies by region; the $250–$750 EPA figure is builder cost, not the homeowner's contract price.
- State adoption data is partial (Oregon, Virginia, Washington checked directly); we did not audit all 50 states' appendix adoptions.
- PNAS high-resolution maps are a research product; they are not yet referenced in any building code adoption ordinance we found.

## Strongest counterargument (to state at full strength)
Radon risk is a lottery at the parcel level, and the people most harmed by it are rarely the ones who can buy a new-construction home. If testing is required anyway after construction, one could argue the $400 is better spent by *testing every new home* and mitigating only the ones that fail — a targeted approach rather than a universal one. The adoption-by-jurisdiction model also has a perverse effect: it blesses the idea that a Zone 2/3 home is "safe," when elevated readings routinely occur in Zone 2 and 3 counties. The fairest reading: RRNC is a cheap bet with a very long tail, not a proven requirement — and the strongest evidence-based policy is mandatory post-construction testing, not mandatory pre-construction plumbing.
