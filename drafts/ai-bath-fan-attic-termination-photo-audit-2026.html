Your Bath Fan Dumps 44 Gallons of Water Into Your Attic Every Year. The Duct Ends Three Feet Short of the Roof.

By Frank DeLuca · September 24, 2026
← Back to articles

Twenty years of walking attics teaches you to read them the way a mechanic reads an engine bay, and once you learn the language, the building tells you everything: where it leaked, who crawled where, and which trade left early. Stains on the sheathing tell you where the roof leaked three winters ago, compressed insulation tells you where somebody crawled, and every so often, swinging a flashlight across the rafters reveals the thing in the photograph above: a flexible silver duct, rising from a bathroom ceiling fan and ending in absolutely nothing. No roof jack, no wall cap, just four feet of duct aimed at the underside of the roof deck, faithfully pumping every shower your family takes directly into the attic.

Attics like that pass final inspection. More often than anyone admits. Last month I walked one in a $1.4 million new build, less than two years old, where the superintendent, a good man I have known for a decade, stared at a dangling duct and said the words I have heard a hundred times: "I thought the roofer handled that."

The code could not be clearer

Section M1501.1 of the International Residential Code says the air removed by every mechanical exhaust system shall be discharged to the outdoors. It further prohibits exhaust into an attic, soffit, ridge vent, or crawl space, a ban that has stood in substantially this form for decades, with no ambiguity to interpret and no local amendment that makes it optional. It is one sentence long.

Ask any home inspector what they find in attics and this defect comes up the way carpenters talk about weather. On the inspector forums, one Colorado inspector estimated that 75 percent of the bath fans he sees do not discharge to a roof vent at all, a number that should embarrass everybody who signed a final inspection card in that market. Certified inspectors post photos of ducts terminating over insulation in InterNACHI training threads, with mold already blooming on the sheathing below, and the comments are always the same mixture of recognition and resignation. By every field account, this is one of the most common defects in American housing. One sentence of code, a defect everybody knows about, and a failure rate that would get any other trade fired. How does that happen?

Follow the duct on a Gantt chart and the failure explains itself. An electrician or HVAC sub hangs the fan and runs flex duct during rough-in, months before the roofer cuts the roof jack, so the duct coils in the attic and belongs to nobody's punch list. Each crew did its own work correctly, but nobody owned the six inches of connection between them, and that is the signature failure of residential construction: not incompetence, but unowned interfaces.

The 44-gallon number

Let me show the math, because the number in the headline deserves its receipts. Heat-transfer textbooks put the moisture released by an average shower at roughly 0.5 pounds of water vapor per person. Two showers a day for 365 days gives 365 pounds of water, and dividing by 8.34 pounds per gallon gives about 44 gallons per bathroom per year, delivered as warm humid air by a fan moving the 50 cubic feet per minute that ASHRAE 62.2 requires for a bathroom. A three-bath house with attic-terminated fans injects something like 130 gallons a year into the roof assembly, and that is an upper bound: attic ventilation carries some moisture away, but it was sized for a dry attic, not for a 50 CFM moisture pump running twenty minutes per shower. Sheathing does not care about assumptions; it just gets wet, night after night, until the black spots show up.

What this means for your buildThis defect is a scheduling gap between the rough-in crew and the roofer, not a workmanship problem. Fix the schedule and the defect disappears: whoever hangs the duct photographs it connected to an exterior termination, and that photo becomes a line item on the pre-drywall checklist.

The AI plan reviewers cannot see it

2026 is the year plan review went AI-native, and the robots are reading our drawings. In June, UpCodes launched an AI Plan Review feature that analyzes drawings against 11 million locally adopted code sections across 6,000 jurisdictions, with discipline-specific checks spanning architecture, structural, MEP, fire protection, life safety, accessibility, and energy. In September, OFA Group brought PlanAId to market, an AI that validates blueprints for egress, travel distances, and corridor analysis, while 800,000 AEC professionals already use UpCodes for code research.

None of it will catch this defect. Not because the AI is weak, but because the drawing is innocent: on paper, every one of these houses shows a bath fan symbol with a duct run to the exterior, and the plan reviewer, human or artificial, sees a compliant design. What fails is a field shortcut that exists only in the physical attic: three feet of missing duct, hung on a Tuesday by a crew that assumed the roofer would finish it, that no drawing ever recorded and no algorithm ever saw. AI plan review audits intent; this failure is in execution.

That distinction matters more than any single product launch. Money is pouring into AI that reads paper, while the defects that actually rot houses live in the gap between paper and building, and the honest vendors know it. For the rest of us, the question is what we check, and when.

The termination ledger

Run the numbers the way a superintendent would. Three columns.

Column one: do it right at rough-in, when the attic is open and the roofer is already mobilized, so running insulated duct to a roof jack or wall cap is a marginal cost. Angi's 2026 data puts a complete new fan installation, fan plus electrical plus duct plus vent, at $200 to $800. As an incremental line item during construction, the duct run alone lands around $150 to $300 per fan, which is less than the delivery fee on the tile. That is the cheapest column.

Column two: fix it after the drywall goes up. Now you are fishing duct through a finished attic, cutting and patching drywall, paying a roofer for a standalone penetration on a finished roof, and scheduling all of it around a family that lives there. Angi prices the roof vent installation alone at $350 to $950, before drywall repair and attic gymnastics. Realistic all-in: $800 to $1,800 per fan, plus the conversation where you explain to the homeowner why the brand-new ceiling needs surgery.

Column three: remediate what the moisture did. Attic mold remediation averages $1,000 to $4,000, with large or long-undetected jobs reaching $7,000 to $10,000 or more once insulation replacement at $1 to $2 per square foot and sheathing repair enter the picture. Between column one and the median of column three, the ratio is roughly twenty to one, for six inches of duct that somebody decided not to run on a Tuesday.

The other side, stated fairly

A good foreman argues with himself, so here is the case for the defense. In arid climates, attic-vented bath fans run for decades without visible damage, because attic ventilation dilutes the moisture faster than it accumulates and the sheathing never stays wet long enough to grow anything. Even the Colorado inspector behind the 75 percent estimate said, of one such install, that it was good but could be better, and he is not wrong. Code is written for the worst climate, not the average one.

There is also a legitimate fear of roof penetrations: every hole in a roof is a future leak wearing a disguise, and some builders would rather risk slow moisture damage they cannot see than fast water damage they can. That is a real tradeoff, though it argues for wall terminations, not for terminating in the attic. And the 75 percent figure deserves its caveat: it is one inspector's field estimate from Colorado, not a national study. Nobody has actually counted, but inspectors everywhere describe this as routine, and the code section it violates is unambiguous.

What we did not prove

This article did not measure the national prevalence of attic-terminated bath fans; no such study exists, and the 75 percent figure is anecdotal. My 44-gallon calculation is an upper bound that assumes every shower's moisture reaches the attic, which overstates reality in ventilated attics and understates it in tight ones. Cost figures are 2026 national averages from Angi and will swing hard by market. Claims about AI plan-review limitations are inferred from how the products work, as described in their launch announcements; no independent audit has tested UpCodes Plan Review or PlanAId against field defects, because field defects are, by definition, not in the drawings they analyze.

The one photo

Here is the actionable part, and it fits on an index card. If you are building: add one line to the pre-drywall checklist, "photograph each bath fan duct connected to its exterior termination," with the photo dated and tied to the unit. Cost is zero; it rides in the superintendent's pocket already.

If you are buying: before closing, get into the attic, or pay someone who will, and trace each bath fan duct with a flashlight until it exits the building envelope. Not toward the roof. Through it. If the duct ends in air, you have found a $150 problem wearing a $4,000 disguise, and you have found it at the only moment when somebody else still has to pay for it.

Twenty years in this business, and the defects that cost the most are never the exotic ones. They are the six-inch gaps between two trades, the sentence in the code everybody has read and nobody enforces, the duct that ends three feet short of the roof. AI will get very good at reading our drawings. Somebody still has to climb into the attic.
