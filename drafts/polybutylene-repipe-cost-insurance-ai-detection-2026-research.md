# Research: Polybutylene Repipe — The Pipe of the Future That Failed

## Journalist

## Angle
Between 1978 and 1995, builders plumbed up to 10 million North American homes with polybutylene ("the pipe of the future") because it was cheap and fast. Chlorine in municipal water ate it from the inside out. Shell and Hoechst paid $950 million in a 1995 class-action settlement. The pipes are still in the walls. Now insurers' AI underwriting models (ZestyAI-style computer vision on property imagery, 97% of US properties) flag the risk at the parcel level, carriers refuse or surcharge policies, and the homeowner's real choice is a managed 4-day, ~$8,400 PEX repipe or an unmanaged midnight flood. Original contribution: a worked "patch trap" cost table (two prior repairs at $2k-4k each = $4k-8k sunk before the repipe you do anyway) and a day-by-day repipe critical path with the HomeAdvisor $4.50/sq-ft math.

## Key numbers
- 6-10M US homes with polybutylene (1978-1995); InterNACHI up to 10M; 1 in 4-5 homes built in the era.
- $950M settlement, 1995, Shell Oil + Hoechst Celanese, 21 states (VA/NC excluded).
- Repipe: ~$4.50/sq ft (HomeAdvisor); 2,000 sq ft ≈ $9,000; PEX $4,500-9,000 standard access, $7,500-14,600 slab; copper $9,000-20,000+.
- Patch trap: two slab-leak/patch repairs at $2,000-4,000 each = $4,000-8,000 spent before the repipe happens anyway.
- Failure mode: chlorine/chloramine oxidizes pipe from inside out; stabilizers exhaust, then walls crack. No warning.
- Insurance: sudden burst damage may be covered; pipe replacement never is; PB endorsement can void water-damage coverage entirely; nondisclosure can retroactively cancel.

## Limitations
- Repipe pricing is regional; $4.50/sq ft is a national average (HomeAdvisor). Sun Belt slab homes run higher; union markets higher still.
- The "two repairs then repipe" scenario uses GeekPowered's slab-leak repair range ($2k-4k); pinhole patch costs vary.
- Insurance treatment varies by carrier and state; some carriers still write PB homes with endorsements. The AI-underwriting claim is documented for roof/property attributes (ZestyAI); carriers do not publicly itemize which interior materials their models flag, so the extension to plumbing is inference from the same parcel-risk machinery, stated as such.
- No public dataset quantifies current annual PB failure counts; "end of useful life" is SageWater's characterization, not a measured failure rate.

## Strongest counterargument
Most polybutylene systems that were going to fail catastrophically already did, in the 1990s and 2000s. The survivors have run 30+ years on the same water. A homeowner could reasonably ask whether a pipe that survived three decades is the one to worry about, and whether $9,000 today beats the small annual probability of a leak. The honest answer: the degradation chemistry doesn't plateau (stabilizers exhaust, then walls thin), the insurance market has already priced the answer, and the cost of being wrong is a 2 a.m. flood plus a coverage fight. But the "it hasn't failed yet" position is not irrational; it's just uninsured.
