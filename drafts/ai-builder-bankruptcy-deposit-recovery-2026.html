Your Builder Took Your $40,000 Deposit. Then the Trucks Stopped Coming.

By Catherine Chen, September 16, 2026, Policy & Regulation

Dyandria Darel put her money down in 2022. She was leaving New York for a retirement home in Citrus Springs, Florida, a half-built house from a local builder called Van Der Valk Construction, and she paid the way buyers like her always pay: a deposit, then progress payments, then faith. In April 2025 the builder filed for Chapter 11. It sat unfinished in the Florida sun. Darel told ABC Action News she had no money left, that the house was virtually her entire life savings, and that three years after signing, in 2025, she was still waiting on a home that might never be finished. Read that again: three years, life savings, a company in bankruptcy court.

Her story is not an outlier; it is a pattern with a filing number. In November 2025, Renovo Home Partners, the parent company behind regional remodelers like NEWPRO, Remodel USA, and Minnesota Rusco, filed for Chapter 7 liquidation in Delaware. Those filing numbers deserve a slow read: liabilities of $100 million to $500 million, assets under $100,000. Not $100 million in assets, but one hundred thousand dollars, set against half a billion in obligations. Every customer with an unfinished job and a lost deposit became what bankruptcy law calls an unsecured creditor, which is the legal term for standing at the back of a very long line holding an empty cup, and the filing made the mechanics official: the deposits were not held in trust, not segregated, not insured, just absorbed into the operating accounts of a company that had already spent them.

So here is the question nobody asks at the design center, usually because the granite samples are right there: if your builder goes broke mid-project, where exactly does your deposit sit, and which of the legal protections you assume are standing guard will actually be standing anywhere at all when the trustee starts sorting claims? What follows is a waterfall, and you are at the bottom of it. Let me show you the math, because the math is the whole story.

The waterfall, in dollars

Take a $400,000 custom build. You put 10 percent down, $40,000, and six months later the builder files Chapter 7. Here is who gets paid, in order, from whatever value remains in the half-built house and the company's accounts.

First, the secured construction lender, starting with the bank that financed the build: it holds a lien on the property, and secured creditors eat first. Whatever equity exists in the structure flows to the bank before anyone else gets a look, because the construction loan was recorded before your contract was signed and secured debt is senior by design.

Second, the mechanics lien claimants, beginning with the framers, the electricians, the lumber supplier who never got paid. They all hold lien rights against the property in most states, and they stand ahead of you. Note the cruelty of the geometry: the same unpaid subs whose absence stalled your project are now ahead of you in line for its corpse, which is how a builder's cash crisis becomes your title problem months before it becomes your construction problem.

Third, your deposit, via a provision most buyers have never heard of. Section 365(j) of the Bankruptcy Code gives a buyer whose purchase contract is rejected a lien on the debtor's interest in the property for recovery of the purchase price paid, which sounds protective and is mostly theoretical. That lien is subordinate to pre-existing liens, which means the construction lender and the mechanics liens from steps one and two, and where the property is already encumbered, which a half-built house with a construction loan always is, practitioners describe its value with a single word: worthless. Some states add a vendee lien on top, which does not change the arithmetic much.

Fourth, the contractor license bond. In California that bond is $25,000, and the Contractors State License Board is explicit about the catch: the amount "is not per job; it is the amount available for all the jobs a contractor takes on during the life of the bond." Your $40,000 deposit is already $15,000 more than the entire bond before a single competing claimant shows up, and every other burned customer from every other job the contractor ran during the bond's life is drawing from that same $25,000 pool.

Fifth, your general unsecured claim, alongside the credit card companies and the office supply vendors. Renovo's ratio tells you what that is worth: up to $500 million owed against less than $100,000 in assets, so do the division and watch it round to zero.

Expected recovery on a $40,000 deposit in this scenario: low single-digit cents on the dollar, unless the money was escrowed or separately bonded, which is not a warning but a calculation.

The protections, and their fine print

Buyers usually name three protections. Each one is real, and each one is narrower than it sounds.

Down-payment caps. California's Business and Professions Code section 7159.5(a)(3) says a down payment on a home improvement contract "may not exceed one thousand dollars ($1,000) or 10 percent of the contract amount, whichever is less," which on a $400,000 project, 10 percent is $40,000, so the cap is $1,000. A contractor who demands more has violated the statute, and the violation is grounds for board discipline. Useful, except the statute governs home improvement contracts. A tract-home purchase deposit with a developer runs under different rules, typically 1 to 3 percent held in escrow, and buyers routinely conflate the two tracks, so know which contract you are signing before you count on it.

The license bond, covered above: twenty-five thousand dollars, shared across all jobs, depletable, and cancelable: the bond dies 30 days after the board receives a cancellation notice, which can happen simply because the contractor stopped paying the premium. A lapsed bond is itself a distress signal, and it is public record.

Escrow. Deposits held by a title company or escrow agent are not the builder's money, and a bankruptcy does not touch them. This is the single most effective protection on the list, and it is entirely a function of how your contract was written. Money wired directly to the builder's operating account is working capital; money in escrow is yours until milestones are met, and that is the difference between two sentences: $40,000.

Warranties deserve their own paragraph, because buyers treat them as assets. In bankruptcy, a builder's warranty is typically a contingent, unliquidated, pre-petition general unsecured claim. Translation: it is a promise from a company that no longer exists, enforceable against an estate with no money, which is why the warranty line in your purchase contract deserves the same scrutiny as the price. Unless the builder carried private warranty insurance, which survives because the insurer is a different company, the warranty's value after a filing is, in the Lexology authors' careful phrase, "insignificant." Ask who underwrites your new-home warranty. If the answer is the builder itself, you do not have a warranty; you have a hope.

Five signals, one afternoon

No buyer can audit a builder's books. But distress leaves tracks, and most of them are public. Run these five checks before you sign: two hits merit hard questions, and three merit walking away.

One, the license page, where every state board publishes license status, bond history, complaints, and disciplinary actions. California's lookup shows the bond history, and a canceled bond means the premium went unpaid, which means cash was tight enough to skip a few hundred dollars. That is not a clerical error; it is a vital sign.

Two, permit velocity: pull the city permit portal and search the builder's name. A builder who pulled thirty permits a quarter and now pulls two has a cash-flow story it is not telling you. Permits are revenue for a builder the way orders are revenue for a factory, and when they stop, something stopped first, usually cash flow, sometimes the credit line, occasionally the will to continue, all of it visible in the permit portal months before the for-sale sign comes down.

Three, preliminary notices: when subs and suppliers go unpaid, they file preliminary notices to preserve lien rights. A notice filed on your job means someone upstream is not getting paid, and the lien that follows attaches to your property, not the builder's, which means the plumber's unpaid invoice can cloud your title even though you paid your own bills on time. Ask the title company for a lien search before every draw, every single draw.

Four, review forensics: a burst of five-star reviews in a single month after years of silence, identical sentence structures across twenty reviews, reviewers with no local specifics, no truck color, no neighborhood, no permit number. Real reviews mention specifics and manufactured ones stay generic; meanwhile the same generative AI that writes marketing copy now writes reputations, and a reputation assembled in an afternoon should be read accordingly.

Five, the deposit terms themselves: any demand above the statutory cap, any pressure to wire funds directly instead of through escrow, any reluctance to put the payment schedule in the contract tied to milestones rather than calendar dates. Lenders who finance construction publish vetting checklists that read like autopsy reports from deals that died: cash-only terms, "we can start tomorrow" from a supposedly busy contractor, verbal quotes, no certificate of insurance from the carrier. Each flag is survivable alone, but in combination, the checklist says, they predict a relationship that ends badly.

None of this requires AI, but AI is quietly good at exactly this kind of pattern work: aggregating permit pulls across jurisdictions, clustering complaint language, flagging review bursts, cross-referencing license data against project volume. Those tools exist on the contractor side already; buyers just have not been told they can use them too.

The case against, at full strength

Now the other side, because most builders do not go broke. Failures cluster among small custom builders operating on thin margins, and private-equity roll-ups like Renovo that loaded regional remodelers with debt and extracted the cash. Large production builders carry balance sheets, surety relationships, and lender oversight that make Chapter 7 disappearances rare, because their draws are policed by construction lenders with auditors, their trades are paid through controlled disbursement, and their deposits sit in escrow accounts a bankruptcy trustee cannot reach. Your deposit with a top-ten national builder, held in escrow, is a different risk object entirely from a $40,000 wire to a three-person custom shop.

Escrow, properly used, converts the whole waterfall into a non-event, because money the builder never held cannot be clawed into the estate. Performance and payment bonds, where the contract requires them, do the same. And there is a cost to treating every builder as a suspect: good contractors book four to eight weeks out, and interrogating a reputable builder's solvency over coffee can lose you the crew you actually wanted. Treat the five-signal screen as a filter, not a verdict, because its job is to tell you which questions to ask over coffee, not to convict a builder who might simply be having a slow quarter. This is not paranoia, because the downside is total and the screening costs an afternoon.

There is also a structural critique worth stating plainly. Consider what the system asks of individual homebuyers, people buying the largest asset of their lives once or twice ever, who must perform due diligence that regulators and lenders are better positioned to do. That $25,000 bond has not kept pace with construction costs; it was set when $25,000 meant something closer to a whole house. Down-payment caps cover home improvement but leave the new-construction purchase track to contract law and escrow practice, which means two buyers putting the same 10 percent down on the same street can hold completely different legal positions depending on whether they signed a home improvement contract or a purchase agreement. What remains is a patchwork, and patchworks have holes exactly where you step.

Limitations

Renovo's and Van Der Valk's figures come from press coverage of court filings, not audited claims data, and ultimate creditor recoveries are not yet public. This Section 365(j) analysis rests on a practice article; lien priority is state- and fact-specific, and nothing here is legal advice. That $25,000 bond figure is California's; other states range from no bond requirement to six figures, so check your own board. Note that the down-payment cap in Business and Professions Code section 7159.5 applies to home improvement contracts, not tract-home purchase deposits, and the article's waterfall math assumes a custom-build contractor relationship. As a method, the five-signal screen is a heuristic synthesis, not a validated model; no backtested accuracy figures exist for it. Permit portal data quality varies wildly by jurisdiction, and some cities publish nothing machine-readable at all.

Before you sign: the four contract provisions

If you take nothing else from this, take the contract language. First, the deposit goes to escrow, held by a title company and released only against verified milestones, never to the builder's operating account. Second, lien waivers at every draw: conditional waivers when the check is cut, unconditional when it clears, from every sub and supplier, collected before the next dollar moves. NPR's reporting on lost deposits found contracts "drafted in favor of the developer" as the norm; lien waivers are how you draft back. Third, retention: hold 5 to 10 percent until the punch list is signed off, so the last 10 percent of the work, the part builders are worst at finishing, has 100 percent of its funding still in your hands. Fourth, a termination clause with a cure period, so a stalled project is a breach with a remedy instead of a hostage situation.

Dyandria Darel put her money down in 2022. The trucks stopped coming. As written, the law will return her pennies, but as it could have been written, the contract might have returned her house. That is the entire difference between consumer protection on paper and consumer protection in practice, and it fits on one page, signed before the first check clears, which is the cheapest insurance in residential construction and the least purchased.
