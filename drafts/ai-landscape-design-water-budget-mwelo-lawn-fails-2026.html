Your AI Drew 2,000 Square Feet of Lawn in 60 Seconds. Your Water Budget Covers 740.

By Priya Greenwood, September 14, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

At 9:47 PM the render landed in the group chat, forty seconds after my friend uploaded a photo of her dirt lot. Emerald lawn wall to wall, front and back, the kind of green that exists in about three weeks of a California April. Nine dollars, one minute of compute, and a yard that looked like a golf course had a baby with a magazine cover. Her builder looked at it the next morning and said the kind of thing builders say when they want to be gentle: we can try.

Generous of him. In California, that lawn is not a design preference. It is a permit problem.

Every new home in this state with more than 500 square feet of landscaping has to file a piece of paperwork most buyers have never heard of, the Water Efficient Landscape Worksheet, which is the compliance heart of the Model Water Efficient Landscape Ordinance as finalized in January 2025. Your estimated water use has to come in under your maximum allowed water budget, full stop, or the landscape plan does not get approved. That nine-dollar render is a very pretty rejection letter.

The minute that skips the math

What the apps do is genuinely impressive, which is exactly why the gap matters. AIGardenPlanner charges $9 a month, takes a photo of your yard, and returns a photorealistic redesign in under a minute, with fifty-plus styles running from Japanese zen to desert xeriscape. Gardenly does the same in about thirty seconds and matches plant picks to your climate zone, starting around $4.50 a month. LandscapioAI, which launched on Hacker News to real enthusiasm, goes further than either: it hands you a line-item material and labor budget it claims is accurate within 15 percent, plus a contractor-ready PDF carrying square footages and plant lists. Yardzen keeps a human designer in the loop at $250 for a botanical makeover up to $1,500 for a full yard.

Not one of them computes a water budget. They will sell you the shopping list for a lawn the state will not let you plant. Renders are fast. Permits are not.

This confusion has a second source, and it is worth untangling because I hear it constantly. AB 1572, the law phasing in a ban on watering decorative turf, has real deadlines that sound sweeping: public properties by January 2027, commercial and industrial sites by January 2028, HOA common areas by January 2029. Homeowners hear "turf ban" and assume it covers them. It does not, because single-family residential properties are explicitly exempt from AB 1572. What covers new residential landscapes instead is something stricter in practice: the water budget. You can legally keep your existing lawn. You cannot legally build the new one the AI just drew for you.

The math nobody ran

So I ran it, using the state's own formula and the same reference values your county plan checker uses. Picture a typical new Bay Area home with 2,000 square feet of landscaping once the house, driveway, and patio are subtracted, and take the annual reference evapotranspiration for the Bay Area, 50.1 inches, the figure San Mateo County prints on its own sample worksheet.

Maximum Applied Water Allowance equals ETo times 0.62 times the landscape area times an adjustment factor of 0.55 for residential yards, which for our example works out to 50.1 x 0.62 x (0.55 x 2,000), or 34,168 gallons a year. That is the entire water budget for the yard, roughly 17 gallons per square foot per year, and every drop has to be accounted for on the worksheet before a shovel touches dirt. The worksheet itself divides the yard into hydrozones, which are simply areas grouped by plant water need and irrigation type, and then it prices each zone separately against the single budget, so every thirsty patch has to be offset by a frugal one somewhere else on the lot.

Pricing the lawn is where the render dies. Cool-season turf carries a plant factor of 0.8, meaning it drinks at 80 percent of the reference rate, while a typical overhead spray system delivers water at 75 percent efficiency, so each square foot of lawn demands 33.1 gallons a year, which is nearly double what the budget allows per square foot. A full 2,000-square-foot lawn would consume 66,260 gallons against an allowance of 34,168. It fails the worksheet before a single sprinkler head is installed, and no amount of pretty rendering changes the arithmetic, because the state does not grade on beauty, it grades on gallons, and 66,260 gallons is the kind of number that gets a plan sent back with a red stamp regardless of how the lawn looked in the group chat.

Run the inequality the other way and the compliant ceiling falls out cleanly. If every other square foot of the yard somehow received zero irrigation, the budget could carry 1,031 square feet of lawn, just over half. Real yards are not lawns plus desert, though, so fill the remainder with low-water drip-irrigated plantings that sip about 7.7 gallons per square foot per year and the maximum lawful lawn shrinks to roughly 740 square feet. Thirty-seven percent of the yard. The render showed two thousand.

San Mateo County's sample worksheet demonstrates how real designs thread this needle, because its 5,000-square-foot example landed at a sitewide efficiency factor of 0.549 against a legal maximum of 0.55, carrying only 1,000 square feet of spray-irrigated turf. One bad hydrozone sinks the sheet. County staff are doing exactly the arithmetic the apps skip, with a red pen.

The strongest counterargument

None of this is what the apps claim to do, and the point deserves its full strength. AIGardenPlanner is a concept tool, not a permit document, and nobody serious mistakes a sixty-second render for engineering; a licensed landscape architect can complete the real worksheet in a few hours, and the MWELO math is forgiving by design. Under the sitewide-average approach you trade lawn for drip plantings and pass, microclimates genuinely matter, coastal yards with lower evapotranspiration earn a looser budget while inland yards get a tighter one, and a nine-dollar app that carries a buyer eighty percent of the way to a planting plan in a minute is honestly useful. Blaming it for skipping civil-engineering paperwork is like blaming a sketchbook for not being a blueprint.

Fair enough. But these apps are marketed to people who have no idea the paperwork exists, and their renders keep showing the single thing the budget forbids, which is wall-to-wall emerald. A compliance check inside the product is the honest fix. All of the data is public, the formula is four multiplications, and there is no technical reason a landscape app cannot warn you that your design consumes 194 percent of its water budget, the way a tax app warns that you owe money. That gap is a product decision, not an engineering limit, and it would take one engineer an afternoon to close. Picture the version every buyer deserves, which is an app that renders the dream yard and then overlays the water budget in the same sixty seconds, flagging the lawn in red and proposing the drip conversion that would bring the whole design under the legal line before anyone pays a designer or files for a permit.

What I could not prove

My math is Bay Area specific, and quoting it elsewhere would be malpractice. Inland and Southern California run much hotter evapotranspiration numbers, Fresno at 51.1 inches, parts of the Imperial Valley above 70, which tightens the budget considerably, while coastal microclimates run lower and loosen it. Look up your own city's value in the state's Appendix C table before repeating my 740 square feet at a dinner party. I used standard plant factors and standard irrigation efficiencies, so your actual plant selections and a high-efficiency system will move the numbers, and first-year establishment watering runs hotter than the worksheet assumes, which means even a compliant design can drink over budget while roots establish. I also did not verify turf-replacement rebate amounts, since they vary by water district and change yearly, so check your local agency instead of trusting a figure I could not source.

What to do with this

Anyone building new in California should ask for the MWELO worksheet before falling in love with any design, AI-generated or otherwise. Check the lawn fraction first, because now you know its rough shape: about a third of the yard in spray-irrigated turf at Bay Area climate, less as you head inland. When a designer hands you a render with a full lawn, ask for the sitewide efficiency factor, and if they cannot answer, the design is not permit-ready no matter how good it looks.

One move carries more weight than everything else in the worksheet. Switching a hydrozone from spray to drip roughly quadruples your water headroom per square foot, because efficiency sits in the denominator of every calculation the state runs. That single change is the difference between a yard that passes and a render that does not.

Nine dollars bought my friend the render. The worksheet turned out to be the expensive part, and nobody charged her for it because nobody mentioned it existed. She is keeping the AI design, shrinking the lawn to the back corner where her kid will actually play on it, and going native out front. An app that drew her yard in sixty seconds could have checked the math in one, and until it does, the prettiest picture in the group chat is the one most likely to fail at the permit counter.
