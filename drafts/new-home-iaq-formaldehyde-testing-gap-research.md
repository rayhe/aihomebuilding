# Research: New Home Indoor Air Quality — Formaldehyde Testing Gap

## Core Thesis
New homes are built tighter than ever for energy efficiency, trapping volatile organic compounds (VOCs) and formaldehyde inside. Studies show 98% of new California homes exceeded the state's own chronic formaldehyde exposure limit. No state requires IAQ testing before occupancy. Consumer air monitors exist but most can't distinguish formaldehyde. The gap between what builders could measure and what homebuyers know is widening as construction gets tighter.

## Key Data Points

### Formaldehyde Levels in New Homes
- **98% of 105 new California homes (2002–2005)** exceeded OEHHA chronic and 8-hour REL of 9 µg/m³ (Offermann 2009, LBNL)
- **5% exceeded WHO 30-minute guideline** of 100 µg/m³ (0.1 mg/m³)
- **All 68 newly built homes with low-emitting materials** still exceeded OEHHA chronic REL (Chan et al., LBNL)
- **Low-VOC homes averaged 33 µg/m³** (3.7× the OEHHA limit); conventional new homes averaged 45 µg/m³ (5× the limit)
- **352 existing California homes** averaged 14 µg/m³ — still 95% exceeded OEHHA REL (Dodson et al.)
- **398 US homes** mean indoor formaldehyde: 21.6 µg/m³ (Salthammer et al.)
- Formaldehyde decreases to ~35% of initial concentration 3 years post-construction, but even after 65% decrease, 99% of homes still above OEHHA REL
- Source: MDPI Int. J. Environ. Res. Public Health 2022, 19, 6603

### The Tight Construction Paradox
- NIST Net-Zero Energy Residential Test Facility: even with source control (no urea-formaldehyde resins, low-VOC products), could not achieve formaldehyde below 20 µg/m³
- "Most common building products release low amounts of formaldehyde" — it cannot be fully avoided in new construction
- LBNL: increasing ventilation was up to 60% less effective than predicted at reducing formaldehyde (because emission rates change with concentration)
- DOE Building America: mechanical ventilation only reduced formaldehyde by 7% in 51 southeastern homes
- Most homes with ventilation systems failed ASHRAE 62.2 requirements — homeowners didn't know how to use them

### The Regulatory Gap
- California OEHHA chronic REL: 9 µg/m³ (7.3 ppb)
- WHO guideline: 100 µg/m³ (0.08 ppm) for 30-minute exposure
- EPA threshold for respiratory problems: ~7 µg/m³ (per ProPublica)
- CARB ATCM regulates formaldehyde emissions from composite wood products (phased in 2009-2013)
- EPA TSCA Title VI adopted CARB standards nationally in 2017
- But: no regulation requires testing the *completed home* before occupancy
- Blower door tests required (for energy efficiency) but not air quality tests
- LEED/Indoor airPLUS homes still exceeded limits (just 27-42% lower)

### Sensor Technology and Cost
- Professional formaldehyde testing: $200-500 per home
- Consumer IAQ monitors (Airthings, Aranet, uHoo): $100-300, measure CO2, PM2.5, TVOC, temperature, humidity — but most DON'T specifically measure formaldehyde
- Low-cost electrochemical formaldehyde sensors exist (~$30-100) but accuracy varies widely
- Real-time formaldehyde monitoring being developed at UT Austin (ASHRAE-funded)
- ProPublica tested homes with passive badges (~$50-100 per badge through certified labs)

### Kill Test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? **YES.**
- Homebuyer: demand IAQ testing before closing; add as inspection contingency
- Builder: add $200-500 IAQ test to pre-occupancy checklist; differentiate against competitors
- Renovator: understand that new cabinets/flooring increase formaldehyde
- Policy: push for IAQ testing alongside blower door testing in codes
