# Research: NEC 2023 GFCI Expansion — article #974

**Slug:** gfci-nec-2023-expansion-nuisance-tripping-2026
**Journalist:** Catherine Chen (Policy & Regulation)
**Date:** September 26, 2026

## The Story

The 2023 National Electrical Code rewrote Section 210.8 and quietly turned nearly every
outlet in a new home into a GFCI-protected outlet: all 125-through-250-volt receptacles
in kitchens (not just countertop ones), ranges, wall ovens, cooktops, dryers, microwaves
(210.8(D)), and outdoor outlets including hardwired equipment (210.8(F)). Appliance
manufacturers say the code was written for 60 Hz devices and modern appliances leak
high-frequency current the GFCI misreads. Electricians report induction ranges that trip
every breaker they are put on. And states are fragmenting: Massachusetts lets you remove
the GFCI when it nuisance-trips; Ohio exempts garage door openers and sump pumps; only
19 states were on the 2023 NEC as of August 2026 while six moved to the 2026 edition.

## Primary sources

1. **NFPA 70, NEC 2023, Article 210.8** (code text itself) — 210.8(A) lists 12 dwelling

   circuits rated 150V or less to ground; 210.8(D) pulls the 422.5(A) appliance list
   (ranges, wall ovens, cooktops, dryers, microwaves) into 210.8; 210.8(F) requires GFCI
   on all outdoor outlets <=50A single-phase; Exception 2 exempts listed HVAC equipment,

## Original contribution (worked arithmetic)

**The patchwork premium**: NAHB's 2019 figure ($272 for two 240V appliances) is the floor.
A typical new 2,400 sq ft all-electric home in a 2023 jurisdiction now needs, at minimum:
two 240V GFCI breakers (range 40-50A, dryer 30A, ~$90-130 each retail vs $8-12 standard),
dual-function AFCI/GFCI breakers where both apply, plus GFCI receptacles or breakers for
all remaining kitchen receptacles (fridge, disposal, microwave, island). Call it $400-700
in device premium per home at retail pricing, plus 1-2 callbacks for nuisance trips at
$150-250 each. Nationally: ~1.4M housing starts/year x fraction in 2023 jurisdictions
(~40% of starts are in the 19 adopting states, roughly) = ~$250-400M/year in GFCI device
premium, most of it protecting circuits where the field evidence of compatibility is the
AHAM white paper nobody read before the code panel voted.

**The Sep 1, 2026 hinge**: Exception 2 to 210.8(F) — the HVAC carve-out — expired
September 1, 2026, 25 days before publication. Outdoor condensers and heat pumps in
2023 jurisdictions now legally require GFCI, and AHAM's field data says VFD-driven
heat pumps are among the worst nuisance-trip offenders. Massachusetts and Ohio already
amended around this. The article lands as the exemption expires.

## Kill test

Helps someone building/buying/remodeling a home? Yes: (1) budget line item for new
construction in 2023 states; (2) know before inspection which receptacles trigger GFCI
in your jurisdiction; (3) induction-range buyers: confirm GFCI compatibility before the
$10,000 appliance becomes a paperweight after the inspector leaves; (4) heat pump
owners in 2023 states: the expired exemption may bite at the next service call.

## Angle / headline

"The 2023 Code Requires GFCI on Your $10,000 Range. The Range Manufacturer Says It Will
Trip Every Time. Massachusetts Lets You Rip It Out."

## Counterarguments (full strength)

- GFCIs are the most successful life-safety device in residential electrical history:
  83% electrocution reduction since 1973 (ESFI), 2/3 of ~300 annual home electrocutions
  preventable (CPSC). Every expansion follows the same pattern: industry cries cost,
  bodies stack up anyway on the unprotected circuits.
- Nuisance tripping is partly a UL 943 standards lag, not a code error: the GFCI
  standard predates modern power electronics; updating UL 943 (task group in progress)
  fixes the device without removing the protection.
- The Massachusetts removal allowance creates its own hazard: once removed, nobody
  reinstalls, and the next homeowner inherits an unprotected kitchen.
- Post-inspection breaker swaps (field-reported practice) are a code violation that
  transfers liability to the homeowner and voids the electrical permit's protection.

## Limitations

- NEC adoption data is a moving target; IAEI table crawled Sep 26, 2026 — verify
  against NFPA's official adoption map before relying on any single state's status.
- NAHB's $272 figure is 2019; retail GFCI breaker prices verified Sep 2026 via
  big-box listings (Eaton/Siemens 2-pole 30-50A GFCI ~$90-130) but prices vary.
- Nuisance-trip rates for specific appliance models are anecdotal (forum reports,
  AHAM white paper) — no published controlled study of trip rates across range brands.
- "19 states on 2023" per EC&M/NFPA Aug 2026; the six 2026-adopting states (incl. CO
  per IAEI) complicate the count depending on whether partial/local adoption counts.
