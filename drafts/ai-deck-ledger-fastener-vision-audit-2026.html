90% of Deck Collapses Start at One Board. Count the Bolts Before You Buy the House.

By Jake Kowalski · September 17, 2026

Twenty-two people were standing on a Jersey Shore rental deck on a September night in 2019 when it let go. Injuries, lawsuits, the whole disaster movie. Then in January of this year, a second-floor deck at a Ponte Vedra beach rental collapsed and sent eight people to the hospital, and the rental company's statement afterward was silence. Investigators in both cases started in the same place, underneath, at the one board that connects the deck to the house, because every deck failure investigation is really a ledger investigation with extra steps.

That board is the ledger, and every deck joist hangs off it, which means every pound of people, furniture, hot tubs, and snow load funnels through one pressure-treated 2x into the rim joist of your house. InterNACHI, the home inspector trade group, puts it bluntly in its inspection course: the most common cause of deck collapse is when a ledger pulls away from the band joists of the home. One industry estimate pegs ledger connection failures at 90 percent of all deck collapses, and when the ledger goes, the deck swings away from the house like a trapdoor.

Here is the part that should send you crawling under your deck this weekend: InterNACHI estimates 45 million decks in North America and fewer than half of them are safe, while the Consumer Product Safety Commission counted roughly 2,900 injuries from deck, porch, and balcony collapses and structural failures between 2016 and 2019, a number that sounds small until you picture the emergency room on the other end of it. Most decks are held up by a board that was fastened by someone who figured decks were simple carpentry, which is exactly why inspectors keep finding them hung on nails. Sometimes that someone used nails.

Nails Are Not Fasteners

Say it with me. A 16d nail has essentially zero withdrawal resistance against the kind of load a ledger carries, which is why the code wants 1/2-inch lag screws or through-bolts, staggered vertically, at least 2 inches from the top and bottom of the ledger and 5 inches from the ends, while through-bolts with washers on both sides remain the gold standard even though they need access to the inside face of the rim joist, which usually means cutting drywall. Inspectors keep finding decks hung on nails alone, with no flashing, the ledger rotting silently against wet sheathing for a decade. Ten years of quiet rot.

Flashing is the second half. People skip it because it is invisible when done right, and catastrophic when it is not. The ProBuilder trade press notes that decks rank as the second-worst offender for flashing violations in ICC surveys, and the mechanism is brutal, because even a perfectly bolted ledger will pull away once the wood behind it rots, which is why the fix is a Z-flashing lapped under the water-resistant barrier above the ledger plus peel-and-stick membrane on the wall behind it to seal around every bolt hole. Materials cost on a new build runs about 150 bucks, and skipping the flashing saves an afternoon while eventually costing a deck, a trade no competent builder would make twice.

Turn a Photo Into a Number With the 100-Rule

Now the part nobody has written up as a field procedure. InterNACHI gives inspectors a rule of thumb for ledger fastener spacing: on-center spacing in inches equals 100 divided by the joist length in feet. A deck carrying substantially fewer fasteners than the rule recommends is a deck the rule says may be unsafe, which is inspector language for do not stand on this thing.

Run the arithmetic on an ordinary deck, say a 16-foot-wide deck with 12-foot joists, the most common suburban configuration in America, and the result is startling. The rule says fasteners every 100 / 12 = 8.3 inches on center, which across a 192-inch ledger works out to 23 fasteners minimum, staggered top and bottom.

Now picture the under-deck photo from a typical 1990s build: six lag screws, maybe eight, often clustered near the ends where the installer could reach comfortably. Six screws against a 23-screw requirement is a 74 percent deficit, and you do not need a structural engineer to call that out; you need a flashlight, a tape measure, and the willingness to get cobwebs in your hair.

This is where the new inspection AI earns its keep: Spectora shipped AI Report Assist this year, and inspectors in early access are cutting about 25 percent of their time per inspection by snapping photos and dictating observations while the software matches what it sees to their approved defect narratives. Paraspot's computer vision identifies common defects from phone photos, auto-categorizes them by building component, and turns a crawlspace photo dump into a sorted defect list before you stand up. Hover builds a photo-realistic 3D model from a guided phone walkthrough, so a structural reviewer can virtually crawl the under-deck framing and count fasteners from a desk, and none of these tools were built specifically for ledgers even though a ledger is a perfect computer vision target: a straight board, a known 1.5-inch thickness for scale reference, and fasteners that are either visibly present or visibly missing.

Feed a decent under-deck photo to a current vision model and ask it to count fasteners, estimate spacing against the ledger's known thickness, and flag nail-only attachment, missing flashing, or screws closer than 5 inches to the ledger ends, and it will do a credible job while the inspection report practically writes itself: the buyer gets a number instead of a vibe, and the seller gets a repair list instead of a deal-killer.

What the Camera Cannot See

Before anyone declares the problem solved, the honest list. A camera cannot see through siding, sheathing, or a finished basement ceiling, so it cannot verify the rim joist the ledger is bolted into, which is the other half of the connection, and it cannot identify the wood species, the moisture content, or whether the band joist behind the ledger has rotted, even though all three determine how much load each fastener actually carries. A freestanding deck has no ledger at all, and an AI that flags "zero fasteners" on one will look like an idiot, which is the false-positive problem in miniature: the model is confident, specific, and wrong. Hidden through-bolts with interior washers read as missing from the outside, and wood rot behind intact flashing stays invisible until the ledger comes off, which is why the camera gets a vote but not a veto. The camera counts what is visible, and a licensed engineer still signs the repair, because no photograph has ever carried a professional stamp and no algorithm takes the stand in a deposition.

And the base rate deserves respect: roughly 2,900 injuries over four years across 45 million decks works out to about 1.6 injuries per 100,000 decks per year. Your deck will almost certainly never collapse, which is why a mandatory re-inspection regime for every deck in America would cost billions to chase a risk that small, and an AI that cries wolf on safe decks will burn its credibility with sellers and agents within a month. Use the tool as a screen, not a verdict.

The Money

For a buyer, the ask costs nothing: tell your inspector to photograph the ledger from underneath, count the fasteners, apply the 100-rule, and check for flashing and nail-only attachment, and put the deficit in the inspection objection. For an owner with a deck past its 15-year design life, a pro ledger retrofit with structural screws, new flashing, and membrane runs $1,200 to $2,800 on an accessible deck, which is one day of carpentry. A full ledger replacement with rot repair runs $4,000 to $9,000, while a collapse with injuries on a deck you knew was sketchy runs six figures and a deposition.

For a builder, the math is the most lopsided on this site: structural screws, Z-flashing, and a roll of peel-and-stick membrane add roughly $150 in materials to a new deck and eliminate the single most common cause of deck collapse. Nothing in residential construction insures cheaper. Build it right the first time. Nobody ever has to crawl under it with a flashlight wondering.

What We Did Not Prove

The 90 percent figure comes from InterNACHI-cited industry estimates, not a peer-reviewed failure database, and no national registry of deck collapse causes exists, so treat it as the trade's best consensus rather than a measured rate, while the "six screws in the photo" scenario is drawn from inspector forum reports that skew toward problem decks, since nobody has published a representative survey of fastener counts on ordinary decks. Retrofit cost ranges are 2026 national estimates, and coastal or union markets run 30 to 60 percent higher, so get the quote before you budget the project. The CPSC injury figure covers 2016 through 2019 and is the most recent published collapse-specific research we could locate, because nobody funds deck-collapse epidemiology the way they fund everything else with a lobby. No published study benchmarks AI accuracy at counting ledger fasteners in under-deck photos, so the vision-model capability described here is prospective, demonstrated by general-purpose models rather than a validated inspection product. Wood species, moisture content, and hidden rot remain outside what any photo audit can verify, which is why the vision model writes the punch list and the engineer writes the letter.

Get under the deck. Count the bolts. The board holding up your barbecue has a number attached to it now, and the number is 100 divided by your joist length. If what you count comes up short, you know exactly what to do about it, what it costs, and who to call, which puts you ahead of roughly 90 percent of deck owners in America. That is more than most homeowners knew yesterday.
