Your Staircase Is the Prettiest Thing Your Architect Drew. Stairs Send a Million People to the ER Every Year.

By Elena Vasquez · September 25, 2026
← Back to articles

Stand at the foot of the staircase in any architect's portfolio and look up. White oak treads cantilever from a plaster wall like a held breath, with no risers and no stringer, while light slides down two stories of glass and lands across the steps in bars. It is the most photographed object in the modern house, and it is the room's sculpture. Everyone photographs it.

It is also, by the injury data, the most dangerous thing in the building.

CPSC groups stairs, ramps, landings, and floors as one injury category. In its 2020 NEISS highlights, that category ranked first among all consumer products: an estimated 2.66 million emergency department injuries, ahead of beds, chairs, and bathtubs. A 2018 study in the American Journal of Emergency Medicine put the 23-year stair-only total at 24.76 million ER patients, an average of 1.08 million a year, one injury every 30 seconds. The medical bills and lost wages behind those injuries run about $92 billion a year, and roughly six in ten of the injuries happen at home, where the stairs are yours.

None of this is the floating stair's fault, exactly. Architecture fell in love with the open-riser cantilever because it turns circulation into sculpture, and the love affair produced some genuinely beautiful objects. Beneath the beauty sits the problem: a staircase is a machine for moving bodies between levels, and its tolerances are tighter than almost anyone imagines, as the code's exacting numbers show. Section R311.7.5.1 of the International Residential Code caps riser height at seven and three-quarter inches. Section R311.7.5.2 floors tread depth at ten inches. Between the tallest and shortest riser in any flight, the spread may not exceed three-eighths of an inch, and the same tolerance governs tread depth. Open risers are permitted, but only if the gap will not pass a four-inch sphere, a test dimensioned around a child's head. Headroom must clear six feet eight inches, and the stair must be at least thirty-six inches wide. Those numbers leave little room for interpretation.

Three-eighths of an inch is the whole story

In 1978, the researchers Archea, Collins, and Stahl described how people actually climb stairs. A person sizes up the first step or two, calibrates a stepping rhythm from the feel of them, and then runs the rest of the flight on autopilot. Bodies trust the pattern, and that trust is the mechanism. When one riser in the middle of a flight breaks the pattern, the foot arrives where the step is not.

In 2020, a team at London South Bank University tested the failure directly. Young and older adults climbed a staircase with one inconsistent riser while motion capture tracked their feet. On ascent, foot clearance over the rogue step dropped by about nine millimeters, enough to turn into a toe-catch. On descent, foot contact on the step shortened by three percent, raising the odds of an overstep. Crucially, participants did not adjust their trajectories. They neither noticed the odd step nor compensated for it. Autopilot held, and autopilot was wrong.

That pattern has a second property that matters for anyone auditing stairs. An EHS Today summary of the stairway literature finds that pedestrians look at only the first and last three steps of a flight and negotiate the middle blind. Ascent trips trace mostly to riser variation; descent trips to treads too shallow to hold the ball of the foot, so the implication is blunt: if you audit six risers, audit those six.

Templer's stair

John Templer spent the 1980s and 90s at Virginia Tech making people trip. Funded by the National Science Foundation, he built collapsible stairs, harnessed volunteers so they would not fall all the way down, and filmed how the body fails. His conclusion was geometric: the common nine-inch tread and eight-and-a-half-inch riser did not fit modern feet, which overhang the edge and shift weight forward off balance. He proposed eleven-inch treads with seven-inch risers, a gentler machine. Templer's proposal prompted a lobbying response: the National Association of Home Builders said the larger stair would add at least 150 square feet and $1,500 to a typical house. It died there, and the code still permits ten by seven-and-three-quarters. In other words, the law allows a stair the research called too steep.

Compare the stair in your office: commercial stairs, under the International Building Code, are capped at a seven-inch riser with an eleven-inch tread, which is Templer's prescription nearly to the inch. Your office stair is gentler than your home stair, by law. Residences got the steeper allowance because somebody decided houses could not spare the floor space, and nobody priced what the steeper stair would cost in emergency rooms.

A camera can read your stairs

Measuring a staircase used to mean a carpenter's square and a patient afternoon. Computer vision has started to take the tedium. A 2022 paper introduced StairNet, a neural network that finds stair nosings in ordinary photographs and classifies each line as convex or concave, trained on 2,670 annotated images. A mixed-reality research project generated holographic stair features from a headset's depth sensors and checked the measurements against authority standards, with reasonable accuracy on simpler flights. iGUIDE operators have flipped their laser cameras vertical to extract rise and run from stair point clouds. None of this is a consumer product yet, and the MR researchers are candid about the limits, but the direction is set: the dimensional audit is becoming a photographic one.

Here is the part the audit industry should have noticed. IRC's three-eighths-inch uniformity tolerance is 9.5 millimeters. A current phone's LiDAR scanner measures depth to within one or two millimeters at stair distance. That makes the instrument in your pocket roughly five times finer than the tolerance it would need to check. A side photograph of a staircase, scaled against a known reference like a dollar bill laid on a tread, gives a vision model enough to flag any riser that breaks the uniformity rule, which is the measurement almost no inspector takes, because fourteen risers with a tape measure is forty minutes on a ladder that nobody pays for. Everything needed to audit every staircase in America already shipped. It shipped as a phone. Check yours.

Start with six, finish with all fourteen

Start with six: the top three and the bottom three, the steps the research says people actually look at and where the stepping rhythm gets calibrated. Then measure the rest, because the documented trip mechanism is the rogue riser in the middle of the flight, the one nobody looks at and nobody compensates for. Stand to the side so the camera sees the nosing profile of the whole flight. Lay a dollar bill, 6.14 inches long, on one tread as scale. Any measurement app with perspective correction will do the rest, or simply compare risers by eye against the bill: a rogue riser three-quarters of an inch taller than its neighbors is visible to the naked eye once you know to look, and it is a documented trip mechanism, not a cosmetic quirk.

Then run the grapefruit test on open risers. A grapefruit is roughly four inches across, the code's sphere, which makes the produce drawer a go/no-go gauge for the open-riser test. If it passes through between treads more than thirty inches above the floor below, the stair fails the code. A tennis ball, at about 2.6 inches, slipping through the gap is the early warning to measure properly. Nobody checks.

What the safer stair costs

In 1993, the builders' association priced Templer's safer stair at $1,500 of extra house. Prices have more than doubled since, per the consumer price index, so that claim is about $3,300 in today's dollars for the gentler geometry on a typical two-story home. Set it against the injury ledger, where ninety-two billion dollars a year in direct and indirect costs, about sixty percent of it at home, spreads across the roughly seventy-three million American households whose homes contain stairs. That works out near $750 a year in expected injury cost per stair household. At that rate the safer stair pays for itself in under five years, before counting a single avoided fracture.

Those inputs are rough, and the risk is not evenly distributed. Toddlers and the very old carry far more than their share of the $92 billion, which means the payback math is too generous for a house of healthy thirty-somethings and too grim for a house with a toddler and a grandmother. But the order of magnitude is what matters. A geometry upgrade costs about what a mid-range appliance costs, once, which is a strange price for the difference between a staircase your grandchildren can navigate half-asleep and one that puts them in the emergency department, and almost nobody has ever seen the two prices written next to each other. Nobody priced the steeper stair against the emergency room, so here is the pricing.

Honest objections

Here is the strongest case against the staircase audit, stated fully. Most stair falls are behavioral, not geometric. People fall carrying laundry in socks at eleven at night, distracted by phones, in bad light, on loose carpeting, in unfamiliar houses. Templer's critics at the builders' association said as much: fix the lighting and the clutter first. A code-perfect stair will still collect its share of the million annual ER visits. And the per-household arithmetic above assumes the $92 billion spreads evenly, which it does not, as the previous paragraph conceded. Geometry is a minority cause of a majority problem, and no photograph of your staircase will change how you behave on it at eleven at night, but it is the minority cause you can fix with a tape measure and a camera while the behavioral majority would require changing human nature itself.

Do not blame the floating stair; some of the most beautiful stairs ever drawn would pass every one of these tests, because uniformity and the four-inch sphere are not enemies of elegance. Blame instead the staircase that was never measured: the basement stair the framer laid out by eye, the attic conversion where the last riser came up short by an inch, the flip where the open risers grew past four inches and nobody owned a tape measure. Architecture asks the staircase to be sculpture, and the code asks it to be a machine, and both are possible, so measure it.

Five moves before you walk the stairs
  1. Photograph the stair from the side during any showing or final walkthrough, and lay a dollar bill on a tread for scale. A riser visibly off from its neighbors is a trip mechanism, not a cosmetic quirk.
  2. Run the audit on every riser, starting with the top three and bottom three, against the 3/8-inch uniformity rule in IRC R311.7.5. The middle risers are the ones nobody looks at, which is exactly why they trip people.
  3. Test open risers with the grapefruit rule. If a four-inch sphere passes between treads more than thirty inches above the floor below, the stair fails the code. A tennis ball slipping through is the early warning.
  4. If you are writing a stair subcontract, put the tolerance in the contract: all risers and treads uniform within 3/8 inch per IRC R311.7.5.
  5. Designing the floating stair of your dreams? Pass the sphere test on paper before the steel is cut. Deflection under load counts: test the gap with weight on the tread above, since a floating tread can flex enough to open a passing gap.

What this article did not prove

The NEISS data counts emergency department visits, not falls, and mixes commercial with residential stairs. That $92 billion figure comes from cited cost studies, not from new analysis here, and the per-household division is illustrative arithmetic, not an actuarial table. It assumes roughly half of America's 146 million housing units contain stairs and that sixty percent of stair injuries happen at home, both rough. Templer's trip experiments were small, lab-based, and harnessed; Francksen's inconsistent-riser study used one rogue step in a lab, not a lived-in staircase. Phone LiDAR accuracy figures are manufacturer-adjacent, and no published field study has validated stair auditing by phone. Untouched by any of this: the behavioral majority of stair falls, lighting, footwear, distraction, clutter. A measured stair is a safer stair, not a safe one.
