The $14,000 Generator Quote Covers 8 Hours of Darkness a Year. Do the Division.

By Frank DeLuca · September 27, 2026
← Back to articles

After every big outage, there is a particular quiet on the street. Not the quiet of sleeping in. It is the quiet of forty households doing the same math at the same kitchen tables, each holding the same business card. Mine had a lightning bolt in the logo and a number printed on heavy paper: $14,600, installed, for a 22-kilowatt Generac with an automatic transfer switch, whole house, never sit in the dark again, the salesman said, the way men say things when the street still smells of everybody else's spoiled freezers.

Twenty years in residential construction teaches you to read a proposal the way a mechanic reads an engine noise, and this was a well-built proposal, with line items for the gas run, the concrete pad, the permits, the startup test, and the first year of monitoring. Everything added up. That was the problem, because I have learned to distrust any proposal where everything adds up and nothing divides.

A denominator nobody prints

Once a year, the Energy Information Administration publishes a number that will never appear in a generator proposal: SAIDI, the System Average Interruption Duration Index, the total hours the average customer spends without power in a year. In 2024 the number was 11 hours, nearly double the prior decade's average, and 80 percent of those hours came from three hurricanes. Strip out the major events and the figure has sat near two hours a year since 2013, which is the denominator your $14,600 gets divided by while nobody at the kitchen table does the division.

So let us do it here: a 22kW Generac Guardian lists at $6,119 for the unit alone, or $6,979 with the 200-amp whole-house transfer switch, and installed, with the gas run and the pad and the permits and the startup test, most homeowners land between $11,800 and $18,500. Call it $14,000, because installation is only the first line item on a ten-year schedule.

Generac wants the machine serviced every year, and the five-year warranty requires proof you did it, so budget $200 to $500 annually for the visit, a starter battery every three or four years, and somewhere in years six through ten, after the warranty expires, a control board or a starter or a voltage regulator, because that is what aging electronics do outdoors. Budgeted honestly, a decade of ownership runs $11,200 to $18,700 all-in on top of the purchase, and then there is fuel: the spec sheet says 2.53 gallons of propane per hour at half load, plus the weekly exercise cycle burning fuel just to prove the machine still works. Ten years, eighty outage-hours, roughly $19,000 soup to nuts.

Nineteen thousand dollars divided by eighty hours is $240 an hour, which is what the heavy paper is really selling: electricity at $240 an hour, delivered automatically, in the dark, while you sleep, so say it that way at the kitchen table and watch the room change.

Ninety dollars of listed hardware

There is a second proposal nobody hands you, because there is no commission in it. Article 702 of the National Electrical Code has blessed it for years, requiring transfer equipment that keeps your generator from backfeeding the grid and killing a lineman. An automatic transfer switch is one compliant form; another is a manual interlock kit, a $50 to $150 piece of listed hardware that mechanically prevents the main breaker and the generator breaker from ever being on at the same time. Interlock kits are named explicitly in the 2026 NEC draft, inspectors approve them every day, and the one catch that matters is that the kit must be listed by your panel's manufacturer for your exact panel, not the universal kit from the internet, which is where red tags come from.

Pair the interlock with a portable dual-fuel generator. Westinghouse's WGen9500DF lists at $1,249, makes 9,500 running watts, and runs twelve hours on 6.6 gallons of gasoline or seven hours on a twenty-pound propane tank, the tank already beside your grill. Kit, breaker, inlet box, two hours of electrician time, and a permit come to roughly $880, for about $2,130 all-in; a decade of oil changes and fuel for the same eighty outage-hours adds maybe $450.

Twenty-five hundred and eighty dollars divided by eighty hours is $32 an hour for the same darkness at one-seventh the price, and I will wait while you re-read that.

When the expensive machine wins anyway

None of which makes the Generac a scam. I have specified standby units on projects where they were the only responsible answer, and I would do it again tomorrow, because an interlock cannot help you when you are not home. Outages that matter arrive at 2 p.m. on a workday while the sump pump sits idle in a rising pit, or in a January freeze while the family is away and the pipes are making decisions. A portable needs a person: somebody home, somebody able-bodied, somebody willing to drag two hundred pounds through the rain at midnight and remember the startup sequence. Portables get stolen off driveways during long outages, they are louder and dirtier per kilowatt-hour, and they do not text you when they fail a self-test.

California adds its own wrinkle: Sacramento will stop the sale of new portable gas generators in 2028 while explicitly exempting stationary standby units, which means the state is phasing out the $1,249 answer and protecting the $14,000 one. If you live in wildfire country where public-safety shutoffs are a season rather than an event, plan around the law as written, not as you wish it were.

Run the outage-hour audit before you sign anything
  1. Pull your utility's SAIDI from its annual reliability report before believing any national average. Shutoff zones and hurricane counties live in a different denominator than the suburbs.
  2. Size the portable to the loads that actually matter: fridge, freezer, sump or well pump, a few lights, the router. A 9.5kW portable will not start a 5-ton air conditioner. Do not buy watts you cannot use.
  3. Order the interlock kit by your panel's make, model, and catalog number, and pull a permit. A $50 universal kit is the most expensive $50 in the project if the inspector red-tags it.
  4. Price the standby honestly: installation midpoint, ten years of service, two batteries, one out-of-warranty repair, fuel for the exercise cycles. Divide by your utility's outage-hours. Above $150 an hour with no medical equipment in the house, sleep on it for a week.

What we did not prove

Eight outage-hours a year is a blended national figure. Check your utility's published SAIDI before applying this math to your address. Installation costs swing from $10,500 to $22,000 with gas-run length, trenching, and panel work; $14,000 is a representative midpoint, not a quote. Propane at $3.50 a gallon is a national residential assumption, and New England winter propane is a different fuel economically than Texas summer propane. This interlock math assumes your panel manufacturer sells a listed kit and your inspector accepts it, which is usually true. And California's 2028 portable-generator sales phaseout reflects CARB's 2021 vote and AB 1346; verify the current rule before planning a purchase around it.
