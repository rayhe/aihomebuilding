Your New Subdivision Runs Its Own Power Grid. Nobody Told You Who Owns the Battery.

By Priya Greenwood, September 16, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

Picture a Saturday afternoon in Menifee, California, forty-five minutes inland from the coast, where a young couple is touring a KB Home model while a salesperson waits for the moment she has clearly been saving all week. The home has no gas line, which is new. The roof has solar panels, which is not. And in the garage sits a battery the size of a small refrigerator, which the salesperson describes as one piece of the neighborhood's own power grid, two hundred nineteen homes generating, storing, and trading electricity among themselves. The couple nods. They lived through the blackouts. They have opened the August electric bills. A house that makes its own power sounds like the obvious answer, and in most ways it is, except that nobody in the model home is really selling a battery. They are selling a relationship with a piece of software that forecasts tomorrow's weather to decide tonight's charge state, a utility that may own the battery bolted to your garage wall, and two hundred neighbors whose freezers compete with yours for the same stored electrons, and none of those relationships appear in the brochure. Read the fine print.

What is being sold here is genuinely new, and genuinely worth understanding before you sign. Across the country, three very different versions of the same idea are under construction right now, and they differ in exactly the place that matters most to a buyer: who owns the equipment, who controls it, and who profits when it works. Start there.

Three neighborhoods, three owners

Start with Menifee, because it is the most ambitious. KB Home's Durango and Oak Shade communities put 219 all-electric homes on a microgrid designed with a $6.65 million Department of Energy grant and engineering from Schneider Electric, with Southern California Edison brought in early so the handoff between grid power and island power would not strand anyone. Every home gets a SunPower Equinox solar system sized for net-zero energy, a 13 kWh SunVault battery, heat pump water heaters and HVAC, and Schneider's Square D Energy Center orchestrating the whole thing. The homes are built to the DOE's Zero Energy Ready Home standard, which the project says cuts average energy use by up to 40 percent, and some homes demonstrate bidirectional EV charging, so the car in the driveway can feed the house during an outage. That battery is baked into the purchase price, and it arrives with the mortgage.

Now drive to Lakeland, Florida, where the model is inverted. Myrtlebrook is a 77-home pilot from the municipal utility Lakeland Electric, microgrid developer BlockEnergy, and builder Highland Homes, funded with $4.2 million of the utility's money. Each home carries an 8 kW solar array, every pair of homes shares a 43 kWh battery, and a central battery serves the whole neighborhood, a design the partners say should make the community 77 percent self-sufficient. Lakeland Electric owns and operates everything, and it projects $1.1 million in savings, savings that accrue to the utility, not the homeowners, who are essentially hosting grid infrastructure on their roofs. The utility's emerging-technology manager put the pitch in one sentence: because generation happens inside the neighborhood, customers are less likely to lose power to storms, squirrels, or cars hitting poles. You are not buying resilience. You are being sold a roof the grid can use. Remember that.

Then there is Ann Arbor, Michigan, where the software is the star. Veridian at County Farm is 170 mixed-income, all-electric, fossil-fuel-free homes built by the THRIVE Collaborative with sonnen batteries in every house and more than 1.3 megawatts of rooftop solar across the development. Geothermal and electric heat pumps handle heating, induction handles cooking, and sonnen's virtual power plant software runs all 170 batteries as a single clustered system with dispatch response under two seconds, fast enough to shave the neighborhood's peak demand in real time and feed stability back to the wider Michigan grid. Founder Matt Grocoff's claim is the bold one: not just net-zero, but a neighborhood that is beneficial to the grid rather than a drain on it. A grid asset that cooks dinner.

The algorithm is the actual product

Notice what changed between the old solar home and these. Panels and batteries are commodities now. The thing that makes a microgrid a microgrid is the software deciding, minute by minute, which battery charges, which discharges, and which house goes dark last, every minute, for every battery.

sonnen's sonnenConnect algorithm shows what that looks like up close. It forecasts tomorrow's weather and watches for planned grid events; when it predicts the panels will fall short, it pre-charges the battery on cheap super off-peak grid power overnight, and it injects solar into the grid only during the narrow windows when California's NEM 3.0 rules make export valuable. sonnen claims the result restores savings comparable to the old, generous NEM 2 rates. Whether or not you believe the marketing, the mechanism is the point: your battery's charge state at 6 p.m. is decided by a forecast you will never see.

Behind every such system sits a layer with an unglamorous name and total authority. A Distributed Energy Resource Management System, DERMS, pulls telemetry from meters and gateways in every home, forecasts consumption and generation a few hours out, groups batteries into fleets by location and grid constraints, and sends each device its orders: charge now, discharge now, hold. The utility's control room never sees your battery, never learns your name, never knows whether the electrons it is buying came from your roof or your neighbor's, because the DERMS has already collapsed ten thousand private decisions into one number the operator can act on. It sees one clean dial, something like "3.2 megawatts available in this substation zone, ready in 90 seconds." That translation, from ten thousand noisy devices to one number a grid operator can trust, is the whole job, and it runs on open protocols with thrillingly boring names: OpenADR, IEEE 2030.5, MQTT. The protocols matter because the early VPP pilots of the 2010s stalled on exactly this: one vendor's battery could not understand another vendor's grid. Standards fixed it. Standards are why your neighborhood can be a power plant. Boring is the point.

The math nobody prints on the flyer

A 13 kWh battery, the size KB Home installs, holds roughly one day of electricity for an efficient all-electric home, less in a heat wave, more if you are careful. That number matters because it reframes the resilience pitch: the battery is not a generator. It is a buffer, and the buffer only lasts as long as the software manages it well and the sun cooperates the next day. Redwood Energy, designing an all-electric California apartment community on the same principles, sized its system against 25 years of historical weather data using the worst year on record as the baseline, and even then expects the complex to cover 94 percent of its annual demand, leaning on the grid for about three weeks a year. Modeled. Against history. Not measured through a single real storm season yet.

That distinction, modeled versus measured, is the one buyers should carry into the sales office. "77 percent self-sufficient" and "94 percent of annual demand" are engineering targets derived from weather archives, not warranties. They are also genuinely impressive targets, which is why they work as marketing. Hold both thoughts at once.

Then there is the macro trend the pitch leans on, and it is real. Between 2013 and 2020, the duration of major-outage blackouts in the U.S. roughly tripled, according to Energy Information Administration data. Wildfire shutoffs, hurricanes, heat-driven grid stress: the grid is less reliable than it was a decade ago, and a neighborhood that can island itself is selling insurance against a documented deterioration. That is a legitimate value proposition. It is also a value proposition priced into the home, whether or not the buyer ever sees the line item.

The case against, at full strength

Now the skepticism, because a microgrid community concentrates risk in ways the brochure will never mention. When 219 homes share one vendor's software and one battery chemistry, a single bad firmware push or one manufacturer's recall becomes a neighborhood-scale event instead of a single-home inconvenience. Vendor lock-in is real: sonnen's fleet optimization, Schneider's Energy Center, BlockEnergy's operations are ecosystems, and ecosystems are hard to leave.

Governance is the deeper problem. Homeowner association documents were written for paint colors and parking, not dispatch algorithms. If the fleet's optimization leaves your house dark during a storm because the software prioritized the grid's peak over your freezer, who is liable? Nobody has litigated that yet, and "the algorithm decided" is not an answer a homeowner will accept at 2 a.m. In California, the whole complex must stay connected to the utility grid by law, which means "energy independence" is a dispatch strategy, not a legal reality; the grid can still bill you, still set the rules, still be the counterparty in every dispute.

And the ownership question from the opening paragraph deserves its final form. In the utility-owned model, the $1.1 million in projected savings belongs to the utility. Your roof hosts the asset; the utility banks the return. In the builder-bundled model, you own the battery, which means you also own its degradation: lithium batteries fade, and in twelve years the "13 kWh" on your spec sheet will be a memory. At resale, is that battery a fixture the appraiser credits or aging hardware the buyer discounts? Nobody knows yet, because no microgrid community has turned over enough homes to find out. Priced in? Unknown. You are buying into an experiment with excellent engineering and unpriced exit risk.

Limitations

KB Home's figures, the $6.65 million grant, 219 homes, the 40 percent energy reduction, come from a vendor press release; no independent post-occupancy utility-bill data was found. Myrtlebrook was announced in November 2023 with completion expected in late 2025 or early 2026, and this article did not independently verify its current status. Battery capacities are design targets, not measured installations, and homeowner bill outcomes have not been published for any of the three communities. sonnen's algorithm behavior and its NEM-2-comparable savings claim are vendor-described; no third-party audit of the algorithm exists. The "one day of electricity" figure for a 13 kWh battery assumes an efficient all-electric home at roughly 30 kWh per day; heavy cooling or EV charging changes it substantially.

Five questions before you sign

If you are touring a microgrid community, this is the entire playbook, and it costs nothing. First, ask who owns the battery hardware: you, the HOA, the utility, or a third party, and get the answer in writing, because ownership decides who pays when it degrades. Second, ask who controls dispatch and what happens during an outage: does the software prioritize your home, the neighborhood, or the grid's peak, and can you override it? Third, ask whether the solar and storage are in the mortgage or a separate lease, lien, or HOA assessment, because a second lien changes your debt-to-income math and your resale paperwork. Fourth, ask what VPP enrollment does to your bill in plain dollars: who gets paid for the electricity your battery exports, and what share, if any, reaches you? Fifth, ask how the equipment transfers at resale: is the battery a fixture, does the software license transfer, and has any home in the community actually resold yet?

The grid is getting less reliable, the software is getting better, and the neighborhoods being built this year will outlive both trends. A microgrid community can be the smartest housing purchase you ever make. Just make sure you are buying the power plant, not donating your roof to it.
