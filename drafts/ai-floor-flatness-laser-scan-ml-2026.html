Your Slab Is Off by an Eighth of an Inch. The Laser Found It Before the Concrete Cured.

By Jake Kowalski, September 13, 2026, Construction Technology

Nobody measures a residential slab. That is not an exaggeration; it is standard practice. The crew screeds it, floats it, and walks away. Weeks later the tile setter runs his straightedge across the floor, finds a hump, and quietly adds a leveling line item to the bid. You paid for the concrete twice.

Here is what the commercial world does instead. It scores floors with F-numbers, a system invented by a man named Face in 1987 and standardized in ASTM E1155. Two numbers, always paired: FF for flatness, FL for levelness. FF limits slope changes over one foot; FL limits how far the floor drifts from design grade over ten feet. (ASTM E1155) In practice the numbers run 12 to 45, and the scale is linear: an FF30/FL24 floor is twice as flat and twice as level as an FF15/FL12 floor. The whole thing can be summarized as a scoreboard for the thing nobody in residential construction scores.

And the old way of getting those numbers is miserable. A technician marks the slab with lines, walks it with a profiling tool, collects readings point by point, and spends hours or days crunching. ACI 302, the bible for floor and slab construction, just shrugs and points to ACI 117 for the tolerances. (ACI 302.1R-04, section 8.15) On a spec'd commercial job, measurements happen within 48 hours of placement, before the shores and forms come off, with reports to the contractor inside 72 hours, and any section below the minimum gets remediated at the contractor's expense. That is how seriously warehouses take this. Your house gets nothing.

Enter the lasers. In June 2025, Oak Ridge National Laboratory announced FLAT, the Flat and Level Analysis Tool. A 360-degree laser scan of the foundation feeds a point cloud into ORNL-developed segmentation algorithms and machine learning that detect flatness and levelness deviations as small as one-eighth of an inch. Total time from scan to answer: under one minute. The point of the minute is not convenience. It is that the concrete is still wet, so the fix is a crew re-pass, not a grinder. (ORNL technology page) ORNL ran the demos at two housing developments and reported foundation measurement time cut by more than 90 percent, with zero slab preparation required. (Newswise, June 2025) "With this digital tool, you can quickly get estimates for the foundation's smoothness with minimal human involvement," said ORNL's Nolan Hayes. "FLAT can tell you within a minute if any mistakes were made and where they are."

FARO got there first commercially. Its Flatness Check app, launched in 2022 and embedded in the FARO Sphere ecosystem, pulls scan data from FARO's laser scanners and paints an augmented reality heat map of the slab on an iPad: red where the floor dips, blue where it humps, numbers for the deviations. (Engineering.com) A Chicago GC called Skender has been running it on job sites; their construction technologist called the on-site AR view "a much more immersive experience" for everyone from the foreman to the owner. (Lidar News) Different color, different problem, fixed same day.

Now run the arithmetic on the alternative. Concrete grinding in 2026 runs $1.50 to $6 per square foot depending on how much has to come off. (LatestCost) A 1,500-square-foot slab is $2,250 to $9,000 to remediate after the fact. Angi's 2026 cost data puts concrete resurfacing at $3 to $5 per square foot, and full removal plus replacement at $2 to $6 per square foot on top of the $4 to $8 per square foot for a new slab. (Angi) Or take the homeowner route: one guy on Bogleheads with a wavy 625-square-foot slab priced out leveling compound at $950 just to cover a quarter inch, and his slab needed much more than a quarter inch. (Bogleheads) Against any of those numbers, a laser scan while the concrete is wet costs about as much as the diesel to get the scanner to the site.

Before anyone prints brochures: FLAT is a research tool with two housing demos, not a product. Do not expect it at your foundation sub's shop next month. FARO's app is real but assumes you own or rent a five-figure laser scanner, which puts it in the GC's toolbox, not the homeowner's. And a scan measures geometry, not the future. A slab that is flat on day one can still curl as it cures, shrink away from the subgrade, or settle where the fill was soft. The laser tells you what the concrete looks like today. It does not file the warranty claim for you.

Also worth saying plainly: there is no code requirement for residential floor flatness anywhere in the IRC. F-numbers exist, the commercial world enforces them, and homes are exempt by tradition, not by physics. That gap is the story. Borrow the commercial spec language and you get most of the benefit for free: require a flatness reading within 48 hours of placement, before the shores come off, with the numbers delivered to you within 72 hours. You do not need an F-number to reject a hump you can see on a heat map.

So here is the playbook. If you are pouring a slab this year, ask your concrete contractor for a flatness reading within 48 hours of placement. If they own or rent a laser scanner, they can hand you a deviation map the same day; if they look at you like you asked for a satellite, hire the scan yourself and let the results do the negotiating. If you are buying new construction, put the scan in the contract. It is the cheapest line item in the entire build and the one that prevents the $6,000 surprise. If you are remodeling over an old slab, borrow or rent a laser level before you buy a single bag of leveling compound; knowing where the lows are turns a guessing game into a parts list.

What this analysis could not establish: how many residential slabs are actually out of tolerance, because nobody measures them. The ORNL demo claims, 90 percent time reduction and 1/8-inch detection, come from the lab's own reporting with no independent verification published. And the cost comparisons assume your slab needs grinding or leveling at all, which a good finisher makes moot. The strongest case for the scan is also the simplest one. You are already paying five figures for the slab. Spend the hour confirming it is flat.

Nobody measured the slab. That was the sentence the industry could always hide behind. A minute-long scan, an eighth of an inch, and a heat map on a tablet just made it indefensible. The concrete cures either way. The only question is whether you find out what you bought before or after you pay to fix it.
