Your Super's Daily Log Is Three Days Late and Missing the Rain. AI Fixes That for $119 a Month.

By Frank DeLuca, September 14, 2026, Project Management & Operations

Three years after the framing job finished, the lawyers finally got around to the daily logs. Across the table, the owner's attorney held up a single page and asked the superintendent, on the record, under oath, with a court reporter taking down every syllable, what happened on March 14th, a date the super had not thought about since the job ended. He squinted at his own handwriting and said, "I think that was the rain day." His log said nothing about rain, nothing about anything, really: four lines, two of them illegible, and a delay claim worth forty thousand dollars hanging on what a man could remember about a Tuesday three years ago.

I have watched this scene play out, in various forms, for twenty years. On a residential job, the daily log is the most boring document and the most valuable one in a dispute. Every lawyer I have ever sat across from asks for the same stack of paper first: the schedule, the change orders, and the daily reports, because the first two tell you about the plan and the money and only the third was written by someone with mud on his boots. The schedule tells you what was supposed to happen; the change orders tell you what got paid for. Only the daily log tells you what actually happened on the ground that day, which crew showed up and which one did not, what the weather did to the schedule, what the inspector said at 10 AM, and it only tells you any of that if somebody bothered to write it down while it was still true instead of reconstructing it from a fading memory at the kitchen table three nights later.

Nobody writes them well

Here is the honest mechanics of how daily logs get written on most residential jobs. Picture the routine: the superintendent finishes a ten-hour day, drives home, eats dinner, and somewhere around eight o'clock opens the log template on a laptop and reconstructs the day from memory, which trades were on site, what got done, whether it rained. Memory is doing all the work, three hours after the fact, and memory is a terrible foreman.

That time gap is not just a quality problem, it is a legal one, and the construction litigators at Miller Nash have published the piece that explains why. Their first rule is the one that should worry you: a daily field report written days or weeks after the event can be excluded from evidence entirely. Document at or near the time of occurrence, they say, or do not bother documenting at all. Your super's 8 PM reconstruction is already drifting into the danger zone, and the Friday log he writes on Monday, the one everybody pretends is fine because the week was busy, is arguably inadmissible under the exact rule Miller Nash quotes.

From there the rules get sharper. Second, the author must have personal knowledge of what is in the report, which means the project manager's tidy summary built from what the super told him over the phone, the document most companies actually produce, can be excluded because the person holding the pen never stood in the mud. A project manager's summary built from what the super told him over the phone can be excluded. And the record-keeping has to be a regular practice, consistent and uniform, because inconsistent reports can be thrown out too. Three conditions: timely, knowledgeable, regular, and most residential daily logs fail at least one of them.

The robots started taking notes

These AI tools arrived sideways, through the camera roll. CompanyCam, the photo-documentation app half the residential world already uses, now turns a day's worth of site photos, voice notes, and project context into a written Daily Log without anyone typing a paragraph. Their own FAQ says the AI can summarize completed work and flag remaining work from the captured information. Sensera Systems launched an AI summary layer this July that generates a morning brief, progress reports, idle-equipment tracking, and safety summaries from jobsite cameras. Sitemetric went further on July 2nd with an agentic AI that answers plain-language questions about who is on site and how trade hours are trending, across a platform with a million registered workers in forty states. Benetics takes the voice-first route: crews talk into their phones and the system structures the updates into reports, which is how EJH Construction, a full-service GC, runs daily reporting across hundreds of projects.

For once, the pitch is honest about what it is actually selling, which is not intelligence but contemporaneity: nobody has to remember March 14th at 8 PM because the photos were timestamped at 2:47 PM and the voice note was recorded at the curb. The log writes itself from evidence instead of memory.

The math nobody ran

So I ran it, starting with the honest number for a conscientious superintendent: twenty-five minutes a day on logs. Two hundred fifty workdays a year is roughly 104 hours, and at a loaded cost of seventy-five dollars an hour, which is mid-market for a residential super, that is about $7,800 a year of labor spent writing logs by hand.

CompanyCam's Crew tier, the one that auto-generates job reports, is $119 a month, or $1,428 a year on their published annual-billing price, three users included. Say the AI cuts the daily log ritual to five minutes: photos captured during the walk, plus two minutes reviewing the generated draft. That saves about eighty-three hours a year, worth roughly $6,200 in super labor. Payback lands a little over four to one, and that is before a single dispute, before a single avoided delay claim, before a single afternoon the super spends building instead of typing, before the change-order argument where the other side's lawyer asks for the daily logs and yours actually has something in it.

Those assumptions are mine, and you should interrogate them. Union markets pay supers more, which improves the math. Small crews where the owner is also the super pay less in loaded cost but bleed more in opportunity cost, because every hour of paperwork is an hour not selling the next job, not walking the next bid, not catching the framing error that becomes a five-figure fix after drywall. Twenty-five minutes is an average, not a promise, so run your own numbers with your own burden rate. What matters is the structure of the calculation: this is one of the rare AI tools where the ROI does not depend on believing the vendor's accuracy claims, because the time savings are mechanical.

The admissibility question

Now the part the vendors do not put in the brochure. Cross-reference those three evidence rules against an AI-generated log and you get a split decision nobody has published yet.

Timeliness: the AI wins outright. A log generated the same afternoon from timestamped, GPS-tagged photos is the definition of contemporaneous. It beats your super's 8 PM reconstruction and it demolishes the Friday-on-Monday special.

Regular practice: the AI wins again, with a caveat. Automated generation makes logs more consistent than any human, but only if you run it on every job. Under the evidence rules, records prepared for the specific purpose of litigation look cooked. Turn the AI logs on for the dispute job and leave them off everywhere else, and a good opposing counsel will notice. Not just the litigious ones; every one.

Personal knowledge: this is where it gets uncomfortable. It was not on site and did not see the rain; it inferred the rain from a photo and a voice note, and inference is not knowledge. But the rule, read carefully, allows a record written from information transmitted by someone with knowledge, and the better practice, the one the litigators actually recommend, is a human who saw the thing confirming the record, which turns a generated draft into a witnessed account. That is the two-minute sign-off ritual: the super reads the generated log the same day and signs it. That signature is what converts AI output into a business record. Two minutes is the whole ritual, and skip it and you have a very expensive autocomplete with no witness.

The strongest counterargument

Against all of this, the counterargument deserves its full weight, because it is stronger than the vendors admit. An AI that hallucinates a work description into a legal document does not just fail to help. It hands the other side's lawyer a weapon. One fabricated entry, one crew the AI claimed was on site that was not, and the credibility of the entire log series is impeached. Worse than the error are the questions that follow: who edited what, when, and why. A sloppy sign-off ritual turns a forty-thousand-dollar delay claim into a credibility fight you lose before the schedule analysis even starts, because once the other side shows the jury one invented entry, every timestamp in the file becomes suspect and your expert gets to explain why the computer lied.

CompanyCam's own FAQ tells users to review and edit generated documents before using them as project records. Read that as the admission it is. And consider the perverse incentive: the whole selling point is that nobody has to pay attention anymore, which means the review step, the one step that makes the document legally meaningful, is the step most likely to get skipped. A super who signs AI logs without reading them is arguably worse off than the one who wrote terse honest logs by hand, because the volume of fluent AI output makes cursory review feel sufficient when it is not.

What I would actually do

If you run two million a year or more in residential work, put the tool on every job. Not just the litigious ones; every one. No exceptions. Consistency is what the admissibility math demands, and the ROI math works on labor alone.

Institute the sign-off: same day, two minutes, digital signature on the generated log, done as the last thing before the truck leaves the site instead of the first thing after dinner. Contemporaneous means contemporaneous.

Feed it photos, not prose. Timestamped, GPS-tagged site photos are the raw material that both the AI and the courtroom trust. A voice note at the curb beats a paragraph at the kitchen table.

And keep sending your formal delay notices on the contract deadline, because the log supports the claim and the notice preserves it, and the two documents do different jobs the way a foundation and a frame do different jobs. Miss the notice provision and the best-documented delay in the world can be waived entirely, and the 2025 edition of the Global Arbitration Review's construction arbitration guide confirms that contemporaneous documents are treated as the most credible evidence available, more reliable than reconstructed timelines or witness recollection.

Twenty years of watching projects go sideways taught me that disputes are never won by the party with the best story. They are won by the party with the best records. Let the AI keep the records. Somebody still has to sign them.

Limitations

Start with what this analysis did not prove. No independent audit of AI daily-log accuracy exists; the vendor case studies are marketing, not controlled research. My labor math rests on a seventy-five-dollar loaded super rate and twenty-five minutes a day, and your market will differ. The legal reading comes from published law-firm guidance and a construction contracting textbook, not from a court ruling on AI-generated logs, because as of September 2026 no such ruling is known to exist. And I have never been deposed about an AI-written daily log, which means some part of this advice is a forecast. The honest kind of forecast, with the assumptions showing.
