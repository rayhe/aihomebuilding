The Five Feet Around Your House Are Now Fire Code. 2.1 Million California Homes Have Three Years to Redesign Them.

By Elena Vasquez · September 4, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

On August 19, 2026, California's Board of Forestry and Fire Protection voted unanimously to regulate the five feet around your house.

Not the hundred feet of defensible space, not the thirty, just the five closest to your walls, where juniper bushes hug the foundation and bark mulch softens the line between architecture and earth. That strip now has a legal name, Zone 0. Your mulch is contraband. For roughly 2.1 million structures in the state's highest fire-risk areas, everything combustible inside it has a compliance clock ticking; five feet, so start measuring.

Walk around almost any California home built before last month and you will see the same gesture repeated: foundation plantings ringing the base of the house, mulch beds edging the walls, a wooden fence running into the side of the garage. For seventy years this was simply how a house met the ground. As of the Board's vote, in the State Responsibility Area and the Very High Fire Hazard Severity Zones, it is a violation in progress. Wood mulch, firewood, dead vegetation, and combustible fencing attached to the structure all have to leave the five-foot band. What stays is a new palette: gravel, pavers, decomposed granite, concrete, and plants only where they are low, watered, and maintained. E&E News reports the final rules were softened after Los Angeles officials and residents protested the cost and the loss of mature shade trees, so living trees can remain if their lower branches are pruned and nothing dead hangs within five feet of the roof or walls.

A Design Brief Disguised as a Fire Code

Read past the enforcement language and Zone 0 is the most interesting residential design brief California has issued in decades. For seventy years, the foundation planting bed, that dark mulch moat every suburban home wears like a shadow, was never really designed. Nurseries and habit defaulted it into existence. Now 2.1 million households must actively compose the five feet instead, and the compliant vocabulary is narrow enough to force invention: stone, steel, succulents in noncombustible planters, the six-inch vertical strip of bare noncombustible material between soil and siding that the regulations specify for maximum effectiveness.

Landscape architects have noticed. Jim Neri, a La Jolla landscape architect, told Fire Engineering that compliance could be expensive for owners with flammable plantings in the band, but that Zone 0 has already become "another aspect of any design program." A UC Berkeley landscape architecture competition for wildfire-prepared home design is collecting entries. For new construction in the State Responsibility Area, there is no phase-in at all: architects must detail the ember-resistant perimeter on the plans now, at the same drawing table where they place windows and doors.

This is the part of the story that deserves the design press rather than the fire press. Somewhere in the next three years, the five-foot band will develop a vernacular. Whether it becomes a genuine regional landscape language, all decomposed granite and steel and sculptural agave, or a compliance aesthetic, the same gray gravel apron poured around two million houses by contractors racing a deadline, is the open question.

Nobody Is Coming to Inspect

Here is the arithmetic nobody at the Board's celebratory vote mentioned. California estimates 1.25 million structures in the State Responsibility Area plus 870,000 in Very High zones of local responsibility areas fall under Zone 0, about 17 percent of every structure in the state, according to the League of California Cities' Zone 0 FAQ. Enforcement rides on the existing defensible-space inspection system: CAL FIRE inspectors in the SRA, local fire departments in the very-high local zones.

That system's capacity is public record. Per the Legislative Analyst's Office, CAL FIRE completes a little over 200,000 inspections a year against a goal of 250,000, with some rural units reaching only a tenth of their parcels annually, a shortfall the office attributes to seasonal staffing and vast territory rather than indifference, which matters because it means the gap is structural instead of managerial and cannot be scolded away. In 2026 testimony, CAL FIRE Deputy Director Frank Bigelow told a Senate budget subcommittee the department needs $6.1 million and 31 permanent positions just to hold the line, warning that without ongoing funding annual inspections could fall below 130,000 once one-time wildfire money expires.

Do the division, because it is not close. Divide the mandate by the throughput, and the numbers refuse to cooperate. Ten years. Maybe sixteen. At the demonstrated 200,000 inspections a year, one complete pass over 2.12 million structures takes 10.6 years. At the aspirational goal of 250,000, it takes 8.5. At the funding-cliff floor Bigelow described, 16.3. Inside the three-year compliance window for existing homes, the state can physically reach somewhere between 28 and 35 percent of covered structures, and every one of those visits now carries a brand-new inspection dimension, because the five-foot zone never existed as an enforceable standard before. More to check per visit means fewer visits per year, not more.

Even the Board's own FAQ concedes the posture: enforcement will be education-first, with inspectors helping owners prioritize over the phase-in period, a posture that sounds compassionate until you realize it is also the only posture available to an agency that can physically reach barely a third of its regulated population before the deadline arrives. Read plainly, the state has written a rule it cannot inspect its way into existence. Something else will have to do the seeing, and it already exists, and it flies.

Eyes in the Sky

That something is beginning to take shape in public, in open-source repositories, before any company has productized it. A project called zone-zero runs drone orthomosaic imagery through a U-Net semantic segmentation model that classifies every pixel as building, vegetation, ground, car, water, or clutter, then measures exact distances from building footprints to flag violations inside the five-foot band. On seven held-out residential test scenes, its Zone 0 violation estimates landed within 3 percent of ground truth: 23,441 square feet predicted against 22,806 actual, a 97.2 percent detection rate on 10-centimeter aerial imagery.

A second project pairs a vision-language model with a regulatory knowledge graph encoding CAL FIRE and Insurance Institute for Business & Home Safety requirements, and its central finding is the one that matters for the inspection math: roughly 88 percent of defensible-space requirements are aerially detectable. As a horizontal band around a footprint, the five-foot zone is almost perfectly suited to overhead assessment. What the drone cannot see, the under-deck fuel, the six inches of siding clearance, the dead leaves in the gutter, remains stubbornly human work, performed by the same inspectors whose calendars already cannot fit the mandate, which is where the celebrated 88 percent ceiling quietly becomes a confession about the other 12.

None of this is a product you can buy. Seven test scenes produced the 97.2 percent figure. Vegetation pixel accuracy in the same project was 81.1 percent. That gap is the difference between a research result and a citation you can defend. No vendor currently sells Zone 0 compliance scans to homeowners, which means the most consequential inspection technology in California fire policy currently lives in volunteer-maintained GitHub repositories rather than in any product catalog. But the direction is unmistakable, and the defensive prior-art filings are already appearing: a public-domain disclosure from July 2026 describes exactly this system, drone photogrammetry plus segmentation plus per-zone scoring against Public Resources Code 4291. When the inspector cannot visit 2.1 million homes, the inspection becomes a flight plan and a segmentation map. In 2029, the inspector is a drone pilot reviewing violation polygons. The future is already in beta.

What It Costs, Priced Honestly

The Board says compliance costs vary case by case, which is regulator for "we did not price this." Some of it prices cleanly. For the single highest-leverage fix, the League of Cities FAQ names the wood fence or gate touching the house. It acts as a wick carrying flame directly to the structure, so start there; month one, done. Replace twenty feet of it with steel privacy fencing at 2026 installed rates of $10 to $20 per linear foot, plus removal and disposal at $1.50 to $4 per foot, and the most dangerous five feet on the property costs $230 to $480 to neutralize. That is the math:

20 ft x ($10 to $20 install + $1.50 to $4 removal) = $230 to $480.

Zero dollars and a weekend is the floor: pulling weeds and woody mulch from under the deck, relocating the firewood stack to the thirty-foot zone, clearing the gutters. That is the FAQ's own recommended starting sequence. Swapping hundreds of square feet of bark mulch for hardscape across the full perimeter band is the expensive middle. No verified 2026 installed price exists for it in this reporting round; any article quoting one would be inventing it, so get three quotes. That line item is where the sticker shock lives, and it is also where the design opportunity lives, because money spent on the five-foot band is money spent on the most seen square footage of the property.

Objections, at Full Strength

Now the steelman, stated at full strength. A skeptic can fairly call Zone 0 security theater that punishes gardeners. Political pressure already diluted the final rules. Enforcement is explicitly education-first with no penalty schedule. My inspection math guarantees most violations will never be seen by anyone official. That means the conscientious pay while the indifferent free-ride, the classic failure mode of unenforceable mandates. Speculative is the generous word for the insurance payoff. Insurance sits outside the Board's authority by its own admission, and no carrier has published a Zone 0 discount schedule. And the AI inspection narrative in this article deserves its own discount. Seven test scenes do not make a validated product, 81 percent vegetation accuracy does not make a legal finding, and the 12 percent of requirements that are not aerially detectable include exactly the violations most likely to kill a house, like fuel under a deck that reads as shadow from above.

There is a subtler objection too. Ninety percent of California homes predate the 2008 ignition-resistant building code, the FAQ notes, which means the five-foot band is being conscripted to compensate for vents, eaves, and siding that embers were already defeating before the Board ever voted. Zone 0 is real protection; the burn demonstrations at McClellan proved the physics. But it is protection layered onto structures whose underlying vulnerability the state chose not to retrofit.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The inspection arithmetic mixes units and should be read as an order-of-magnitude illustration, not an audit. In that arithmetic, the 2.12 million figure counts structures while the 200,000-per-year throughput counts parcel inspections. Some parcels hold multiple structures; some structures share parcels. That conclusion survives the unit mismatch. Even under generous assumptions the gap is a factor of three. But the 10.6-year figure is not a number to cite against the state in a hearing. I could not verify 2026 installed pricing for mulch-to-hardscape conversion, so the cost model covers only the fence fix with sourced unit rates. I found no data on how many insurers currently price Zone 0 specifically as distinct from general defensible space. And the compliance deadlines in the record span three years for existing structures in the Board's FAQ and up to five in the final adopted rules; treat the window as three-to-five, phased.

What to Do With Five Feet

If you own in a covered zone, sequence the work the way the fire science sequences the risk. First, break the wick: replace the wood fence and gate section touching the house, $230 to $480 per the math above. Second, clear under and around any attached deck, pulling woody mulch and vegetation back past the five-foot line. Third, convert the mulch beds to hardscape, and spend the design effort there, because this band is the foreground of every photograph of your home and it is about to be rebuilt anyway.

If you are buying in a Very High zone, know that AB 38 already requires the seller to hand you defensible-space inspection documentation. Ask specifically whether it addresses Zone 0, because most documentation in circulation predates the August vote.

If you are building in the State Responsibility Area, the phase-in does not apply to you. Detail the ember-resistant perimeter on the plans now, including the six-inch noncombustible vertical strip at the siding base, or the first inspection will redline what the drawings omitted.

Two million houses are about to redesign their most intimate landscape under a deadline the state cannot enforce and a camera that has not launched yet. Whether the five feet get cleared was never the interesting question. What gets planted, poured, and built in their place is, and whether anyone will call it beautiful comes after, once two million households have finished negotiating with gravel, steel, and the stubborn memory of the gardens they tore out.
