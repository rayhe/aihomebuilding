A lumber quote came in on a Tuesday. By the Friday the builder was ready to order, it was $4,300 higher. Nothing had changed about the house. Plans unchanged, takeoffs unchanged, same lumberyard. What changed was a Tuesday in Washington, and a builder in Minnesota learned that his framing package was now a bet on trade policy. I have been watching builders price jobs for twenty years, and I have never seen a cost input behave like this: lumber behaving like a tech stock, cabinets with a tariff deadline printed on them like a coupon about to expire.

So when a startup called Field Materials AI announced a new "Pricing Intelligence" module this year, promising to read a contractor's invoices, track every material's price history, and tell him exactly when to lock in before the next spike, I did what a foreman does with any new tool on site. I checked whether it solves a real problem, what it costs, and where it breaks. The problem, at least, is real. The rest deserves the full walkthrough.

A price spike with a federal return address

Start with the numbers, because they set the size of the wound. A Brookings Institution analysis estimated that the tariff policies then in effect would add roughly $30 billion to residential-structure investment costs, with about 90 percent of it landing on new homes and apartments. That model predates the 2026 metal changes, so treat it as a floor with the date stamped on it, not a current invoice. The current invoice is uglier.

Softwood lumber now carries a 10 percent tariff layered on top of the antidumping and countervailing duties that more than doubled last year to about 35 percent for most Canadian producers, according to the Wall Street Journal, which also reports lumber prices at their highest in years even as homebuilding sputters. Canadian softwood imports were down 15 percent through May. Canfor is permanently closing its Fox Creek sawmill in Alberta at the end of September, and Weyerhaeuser's CEO told investors flatly that shuttered mills are not coming back: "There's a certain amount of volume that is just out of the system." Fastmarkets, which forecasts this market for a living, expects North American softwood capacity to decline by more than 1.3 billion board feet and calls industry confidence the lowest since the Global Financial Crisis. Forecasting in this environment, their analysts admit, has been "challenging to say the least," which is forecaster for "we are guessing too."

Then there is the calendar. Kitchen cabinets and vanities carry a 25 percent tariff today, with a planned increase to 50 percent postponed until January 1, 2027, subject to later trade agreements or policy changes. Certain steel and aluminum products face 25 percent duties through the end of 2027. An NBC News model of an 1,800-square-foot house, published via a House oversight document, put numbers on the exposure: 24 percent of framing lumber imported from Canada with a projected 6.9 percent price increase, sheet goods up 7.7 percent, rebar up 5.5 percent. ConstructConnect's 2026 outlook puts it bluntly: under recent tariff scenarios, construction materials face 10 to 15 percent more tariff exposure than the broader economy.

What the dashboard actually does

Field Materials AI is an AI procurement platform founded in 2022, with about $19 million in venture funding and a claim of processing more than $700 million in material purchases a year across 12 trades, 30 states, and 4 countries. Pricing Intelligence is a real-time dashboard built on a simple observation: most construction companies create lump-sum purchase orders in their accounting software with no line-item detail, which makes it impossible to see what materials were bought, at what volume, and at what price, across jobs or across the year.

It reads vendor quotes and invoices, extracts individual material prices, and builds a per-material price history. From that history it does three things a purchasing manager would do if he had forty free hours a week: it predicts how much the company could save by locking in prices with suppliers, it assembles the volume data needed to negotiate bulk discounts, and it identifies which materials are worth stocking in the warehouse based on purchase volume and price volatility. When bidding or starting a new job, the builder can lock in buyouts for the materials most likely to swing. CEO Eldar Sadikov's pitch is that contractors are coming to the company on their own, looking for exactly this visibility. Field Materials claims the platform saves 5 to 10 percent on material costs, and says it grew threefold in the last year.

It is not the only tool pointed at this problem. LLAIE, the Lund Lumber AI Estimator, generates a full lumber material list from a PDF plan set in under five minutes, imports the builder's supplier price lists, compares bids across suppliers, and tracks material cost trends with markup recommendations. Togal.ai and the estimating services do the takeoff side. What Field Materials is selling is the layer on top: not the price of the two-by-fours today, but the decision of whether to buy them today.

Math that matters to a residential builder

Take the claim seriously for a minute and run the arithmetic the way you would on a job. A custom builder doing $3 million in annual material spend at a 7 percent savings keeps $210,000 a year. That is a full-time superintendent, or the margin on a bad year, depending on which year you ask about. Even at half the claimed savings, the number pays for any software subscription many times over. That is the pitch, and on paper it clears the bar easily.

A timing playbook writes itself from the tariff calendar. Cabinet and vanity packages: order before the 50 percent rate potentially takes effect on January 1, 2027, assuming the postponement is not extended again, which is itself a gamble. Lumber buyouts: ahead of the spring building season, when seasonal demand meets a supply base that just lost another 2 billion board feet of Canadian shipments. Steel packages: the 25 percent duty runs through 2027, so there is no deadline to beat, only a floor to plan around. And for the buyer on the other side of the table, the tariff era makes three contract questions non-negotiable: a dated price sheet with a written validity period, the purchase agreement's escalation, substitution, and allowance clauses in plain language, and written confirmation of whether the cabinet, HVAC, and metal packages are ordered and in inventory or still exposed to vendor pricing. Per The Close's reporting on 2026 tariff pricing, this is the new due diligence, and any agent who is not asking these questions before a pricing decision is doing disclosure by hope.

Where the dashboard stops and judgment starts

Now the part the launch announcement left out, because every tool has a failure mode and the honest ones print it on the box. First, the 5 to 10 percent savings figure comes from the company's own launch materials. No independent party has verified it. Treat it the way you would treat a subcontractor's self-reported production rate: directionally interesting, unverified until you measure it on your own jobs.

Second, the dashboard tracks your invoices, which means it is only as good as your purchasing history. A builder running two custom homes a year through a single lumberyard on relationship pricing does not generate the volume or the multi-supplier spread that makes lock-in math work. This kind of tool is built for companies with purchasing departments, and most residential builders are the purchasing department.

Third, and this is the one that keeps me up, forecasting tariff exposure is forecasting politics. No model predicted the cabinet increase would be postponed to 2027, and no dashboard can tell you whether it gets postponed again. Here the essential warning from The Close stands: tariff exposure varies by product classification, country of origin, U.S. content share, and USMCA eligibility. A national price trend is not a per-job answer. The builder still has to know what is actually in the shipment.

Fourth, locking in prices is a bet, and bets lose. Lumber has a long history of crashing as violently as it spikes. A buyout that saves 8 percent on the way up costs 8 percent on the way down, and the dashboard that tells you to lock in at the peak is the same dashboard that looked brilliant a month earlier. Volatility is not a one-way street, even when the street is currently on fire.

What I would do with it

Here is the foreman's verdict, after the full walkthrough. If you run a residential operation spending seven figures a year on materials, the invoice discipline alone is worth the look. Most builders I know cannot tell you what they paid for LVL beams in March versus June, because the information is buried in lump-sum POs exactly the way Field Materials describes. A tool that extracts it and hands you a per-material price history is genuinely useful, even before any prediction enters the picture. Use it to negotiate volume discounts with your suppliers first; that is the low-risk, high-certainty win. Use the lock-in predictions second, and treat them as one input among several, weighted by how much of your purchasing actually runs through competitive bids.

If you are a smaller builder, the cheaper move is LLAIE-style bid comparison and the unglamorous discipline of dating your price sheets and reading your escalation clauses. The tariff calendar is public information. You do not need AI to know that cabinets face a rate change in January; you need a calendar and a purchasing plan.

If you are buying a new home, understand what your builder is dealing with. His quote is not padding when it moves between bid and contract; it is exposure management in a market where Washington can add six figures to a subdivision's material bill with a proclamation. Ask the three contract questions. Get the answers in writing. And remember that the most sophisticated pricing dashboard in the world cannot tell your builder what the tariff rate will be next quarter, because nobody in Washington has decided yet. Plan for the range, not the point estimate. That has always been the job. Software just finally admitted it.
