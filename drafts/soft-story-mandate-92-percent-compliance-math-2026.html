San Francisco Ordered 4,900 Apartment Buildings Earthquake-Proofed in 2013. 92 Percent Complied. The Other 8 Percent Still House 9,600 People.

By Catherine Chen · September 27, 2026
← Back to articles

In October 2013, the City and County of San Francisco did something no major American city had done before. It mailed retrofit orders to the owners of 4,921 wood-frame apartment buildings and told them, in the polite language of municipal code, to earthquake-proof their ground floors or face the consequences. Thirteen years later, the results are in, and they are the clearest natural experiment American housing policy has ever run on a single question: does the government have to order people to make their buildings safe, or will they do it on their own?

They will not do it on their own. Before the mandate, San Francisco tried the friendly approach for years: breaks on planning fees, expedited permitting, a general sense that everyone agreed retrofits were a good idea. About 50 property owners took the city up on it. After the mandate, 98 percent of owners applied for permits and 92 percent have now finished the work, making more than 40,000 homes safer and protecting over 110,000 people. Voluntary compliance got 50 buildings; the mandate got 4,500. That ratio, 90 to 1, is the entire argument for mandatory retrofit law, expressed as a fraction.

What a soft story is, and why it kills

Picture a classic San Francisco apartment building: three or four stories of wood framing, with the ground floor given over to tuck-under parking or a wide commercial storefront. Upstairs, walls full of windows and partitions make the structure stiff. Downstairs, the open floor is held up by skinny posts and not much else. Engineers call this a soft story, and in an earthquake the physics is brutally simple. Upstairs, the whole stiff block of upper floors moves as one while the weak ground floor absorbs all the deformation, and then the posts fail. Then the building above comes down on the space where people parked their cars and, in mixed-use buildings, where people were sleeping.

This is not theoretical. In the 1994 Northridge earthquake, 16 people died in the collapse of a single soft-story apartment building. In 1989, Loma Prieta turned soft-story buildings in the Marina District into the disaster's most photographed wreckage. A retrofit fixes the asymmetry by adding steel moment frames or plywood shear walls to the ground floor until it is at least 80 percent as strong as the floors above, which is the performance target the building codes of San Francisco and Los Angeles both adopted. Construction runs two to four months and stays on the ground floor. Tenants can usually remain in place. It is, by the standards of seismic work, the easy version.

Where the power comes from

For years the legal question was whether cities could even compel this. California answered it in 2015, when AB 304 amended Health and Safety Code sections 19162 and 19163 to expressly authorize cities to adopt mandatory retrofit standards for soft-story residential buildings. San Francisco had already moved in 2013, covering wood-frame buildings of three or more stories with five or more units built before 1978. Los Angeles followed in 2015 with the largest program in the country: roughly 13,500 soft-story buildings, seven years to complete construction once ordered. Oakland adopted its ordinance in 2020 for about 1,500 buildings, with tiered deadlines stretching to 2025. Berkeley, Alameda, and Fremont run smaller programs. Palo Alto spent years considering one for 300 buildings.

Consider what that patchwork means for a renter. Two identical 1960s fourplexes with tuck-under parking, one in San Francisco and one in a city that never passed an ordinance, face entirely different legal realities. In one, the owner received a notice and an order with a deadline. In the other, the retrofit is a suggestion. The USGS earthquake early-warning coordinator Douglas Given put the structural problem plainly: building codes are a patchwork of mandates and rules, and even cities that identify dangerous buildings struggle to get the work done. Identification is the easy part; money is the hard part.

Counting the money

Let us price it, because nobody complies with a mandate they cannot finance. Direct construction costs for a soft-story retrofit run $60,000 to $150,000 per apartment building, per the estimates cities published when writing their ordinances. A current cost guide puts a soft-story-over-garage retrofit on a single home at $15,000 or more, and a professional seismic inspection at $100 to $400. For a small apartment building, the honest planning number in 2026 dollars sits above the decade-old city estimates, not below them.

Now the question the code does not answer: who pays? Los Angeles settled it by negotiation. The city council voted unanimously to let owners pass half the retrofit cost to tenants through rent increases spread over ten years, capped at $38 per month. Run that cap through the arithmetic and it becomes the binding constraint on the whole program. Thirty-eight dollars a month for 120 months is $4,560, the maximum any owner can recover from a single tenant. At the $5,000-per-unit cost figure Los Angeles itself cited, the owner absorbs roughly $440 per unit even in the best case. At a realistic $100,000 retrofit on a ten-unit building, which is $10,000 per unit, the owner absorbs $5,440 per unit and recovers less than half. On paper, the 50-50 split sounds fair. The $38 cap makes it something else: a program in which the owner finances most of the work and waits a decade to recover less than half of it.

Berkeley ran the experiment the other way. Tenant activists there pointed out, correctly, that it is strange to bill current tenants for structural work they may not live to benefit from, and the city's program got many owners to retrofit without passing costs through at all, after an economic survey found most owners could afford it. Two cities, two theories of who owes what for a building that predates everyone living in it. The code is silent on fairness, which is more than the rent board can say.

Counting the holdouts

Back to San Francisco's headline number, because it deserves a closer read than the press release gives it. Ninety-two percent of roughly 4,900 buildings retrofitted, housing over 110,000 people. Do the subtraction the city did not publish: about 390 buildings never complied. If occupancy runs proportional across compliant and non-compliant buildings, and there is no published reason to assume it does not, those 390 buildings house on the order of 9,600 people. Nine thousand six hundred people sleeping above unreinforced ground floors in a city that ordered the problem fixed thirteen years ago.

Who are the 8 percent? The program's own history suggests an answer. The first 65 percent, done by 2019, were the easy cases: owners with capital, buildings with straightforward geometry, contractors available. The climb from 65 to 92 percent took four more years and the full weight of enforcement. What remains is the hard tail: owners who cannot finance $100,000 of structural work, buildings with complicated ground floors, and the genuinely non-responsive. A mandate moves the middle of the distribution. It does not dissolve the tail. Nothing in the ordinance mints money.

This is where the grant programs enter, and where their limits show. California's Earthquake Multi-Unit Retrofit grants cover 5-to-10-unit soft-story buildings in cities with mandatory ordinances: pre-1991 wood frame, tuck-under parking, an owner who received a notice and order, and a full ground-floor retrofit to FEMA P-807, IEBC Chapter A4, or ASCE 41 standards. Line-only retrofits do not qualify. Registration for the current cycle ran August 19 through September 30, 2026, which is to say the window is six weeks a year and then it closes. A grant program that covers a slice of eligible buildings, once a year, for six weeks, is a real thing and also a rounding error against 390 non-compliant buildings in one city.

Steel-manning the mandate

The strongest case against all of this writes itself, and it deserves its full weight. Mandatory retrofits tax the exact people they claim to protect. Small landlords, many of them immigrants who bought one building as a retirement plan, get a six-figure order with a ten-year partial reimbursement schedule. Tenants get $38 a month in higher rent for work done on a building they rent, not own, in a housing market where $38 is not nothing. The USGS warned that retrofit costs built into rents could take units off the market or price people out of buildings that were affordable precisely because the owner had deferred every capital project since 1978. And the mandate's 92 percent flatters the program by counting buildings rather than difficulty: the remaining 8 percent are the buildings whose owners are least able to pay, which means the enforcement tail lands hardest on the most marginal owners. A mandate without money is a fine schedule with extra steps, and the fines fall on people, not abstractions.

Now the defense, which is shorter and heavier. Sixteen people died in one building in Northridge. The Marina District in 1989. Voluntary compliance produced 50 retrofits; the mandate produced 4,500. Forty thousand homes are safer and 110,000 people sleep above reinforced ground floors because the city sent a letter that said "or else" and meant it. Every objection about cost is real, and every one of them was also true in 2013, when the alternative on offer was to wait for the earthquake to do the enforcement. Earthquakes do not negotiate pass-through caps.

Catherine's compliance checklist for renters, buyers, and small landlords
  1. Look up the building before you sign or buy. San Francisco publishes its soft-story list and retrofit status online; the city's earthquake preparedness page links directly to the lookup. Oakland, Berkeley, and Los Angeles building departments maintain their own lists. An unretrofitted building with an open order is a six-figure liability wearing a rent roll.
  2. If you received a notice and order, the sequence is fixed: confirm the building is subject to the program, confirm your compliance tier, hire an engineer or architect for the evaluation, get the retrofit design permitted, then build. Oakland's published steps run in exactly this order. Do not start with a contractor; start with the evaluation.
  3. Budget the unrecoverable portion, not the sticker price. In Los Angeles the tenant pass-through caps at $38 a month for ten years, which is $4,560 per unit, period. Everything above that comes out of the owner's financing. Price it before the engineer prices it for you.
  4. Check grant eligibility the week the window opens, not the week it closes. California's EMR grants cover 5-to-10-unit buildings in mandatory-ordinance cities with a notice and order in hand, full ground-floor retrofit to FEMA P-807 or equivalent. The registration window runs about six weeks a year.
  5. Renters: ask in writing whether the building is on a mandatory retrofit list and whether the work is complete. A landlord's answer, or non-answer, is information. In San Francisco the list is public; you can check before you ask.
  6. Buyers of pre-1991 multifamily in a mandatory city: add soft-story order status to due diligence next to the sewer lateral and the unpermitted bathroom. Your inspector will check the water heater strapping. Nobody checks the ground floor unless you ask.

What this article did not prove

San Francisco's 92 percent completion figure comes from city reporting circa 2023; no newer official percentage was available in the public record as of September 2026, so treat it as the latest published, not the current. The estimate of roughly 9,600 people in non-compliant buildings assumes occupancy is proportional between compliant and non-compliant buildings, which the city has never published data to confirm; non-compliant buildings could skew smaller or larger, and the honest statement is "on the order of ten thousand." Retrofit cost ranges of $60,000 to $150,000 per building come from 2015-2016 ordinance-era estimates; actual 2026 contractor pricing is higher and no public dataset tracks realized per-building costs, so the pass-through math uses the cities' own figures and understates the owner's burden if anything. The EMR grant program page reviewed did not publish per-building award amounts, so this article describes eligibility and timing, not dollars. Oakland's tiered deadlines ran through 2025 and current compliance counts for its 1,500 buildings were not available in the sources reviewed. Finally, none of this is legal advice: mandates, pass-through rules, and grant windows change, your authority having jurisdiction has the final word, and the inspector with the red tag is always right.
