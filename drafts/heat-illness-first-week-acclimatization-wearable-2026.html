Three in Four Heat Deaths Happen in the First Week. Your New Hire Started This Morning.

By Marcus Washington, September 19, 2026, Workforce & Labor

The OSHA accident report is eleven lines long. Single-family dwelling, new construction, project cost between $250,000 and $500,000. He was on the forming crew; nature of injury, heat exhaustion; degree of injury, fatality. That is the whole story the paperwork tells, and it is the story of an ordinary residential job site on an ordinary hot day, the kind of project ten thousand small contractors are running right now across the Sun Belt (OSHA accident report, inspection 308651173).

What the paperwork does not say is the part I keep coming back to. OSHA went back through its heat enforcement cases and found something that should change how every superintendent in America runs a Monday morning. Nearly three out of four heat fatalities happen during the worker's first week on the job. More than half of those deaths happened on the first day. OSHA's review of twenty heat enforcement cases from 2012 and 2013, published by the CDC, found that of the thirteen cases involving a fatality, nine of the workers died within their first three days, four of them on day one (Public Citizen, Scorched States report, citing OSHA case review; CDC MMWR summary via Occupational Health & Safety).

Read that again, because the most dangerous variable on a hot job site is not the thermometer. It is the hire date.

Construction owns this statistic

Nobody dies of heat at a desk. Researchers at the Center for Construction Research and Training pulled apart Bureau of Labor Statistics fatality data from 2011 through 2023 and found that construction reported the most fatal heat injuries of any industry in America: 34 percent of all heat deaths in 2023, eighteen workers. Agriculture, forestry, fishing, and hunting came in second at just under 21 percent (CPWR Data Bulletin, August 2025), and the state-by-state map tells the rest.

Texas recorded 25 construction heat deaths over the study period, California 13, Florida 9, and those are just the states with the biggest construction payrolls, which is another way of saying the states building the most houses. Arkansas logged only 8 deaths but the worst rate in the country, 0.69 per 100,000 full-time-equivalent workers, more than four times the rate in Texas. Between 2021 and 2024, heat exposure killed an average of 48 American workers a year and put an average of 3,793 more out of work with injuries serious enough to cost days away from the job. From 1992 to 2021, the running total was 999 dead. OSHA says plainly that all of these numbers are undercounts, because heat deaths get misdiagnosed and misclassified, and industry was missing or suppressed in more than one in four heat deaths in 2023 (OSHA National Emphasis Program background, April 2026).

Everybody nods, and then nobody changes the schedule. Nobody wants to talk about the schedule. That is where the money is.

What the law actually requires

In California, the law is specific and old enough to vote. Title 8, Section 3395 has required outdoor heat illness prevention since 2005: free drinking water, at least a quart per worker per hour, placed close enough that workers actually drink it; shade or cool-down areas when it hits 80 degrees; high-heat procedures at 95 and up; a written prevention plan; training for workers and supervisors; and acclimatization methods for new hires. In July 2024, California added Section 3396 for indoor work at 82 degrees and above, which covers the attic crews and the garage conversions and the enclosed framing where the air does not move (Cal/OSHA Heat Illness Prevention enforcement guidance).

Enforcement has teeth when the state decides to use them. In December 2024, Cal/OSHA hit Parkwood Landscape Maintenance of Van Nuys with $276,425 in penalties for willfully violating the heat standard. Its workers were buying their own drinking water. There was no shade, no training, no written procedures, and temperatures regularly topped 95. It was the agency's first willful heat citation in more than five years, and it followed a 2022 citation for the same violations, meaning the company had been handed model procedures and ignored them (Cal/OSHA news release, December 2024). Under the penalty schedule that took effect in January 2025, a serious violation runs up to $25,000 and a willful or repeated one up to $162,851 per violation.

Federally, there is no heat standard at all. OSHA proposed one in August 2024, held hearings in the summer of 2025, and then the rulemaking stalled. As of this writing it has no target date for finalization, and in July 2026 the House Education and Workforce Committee approved legislation that would bar OSHA from finalizing it (OSHAcademy regulatory update, August 2026). So OSHA enforces heat under the General Duty Clause, the broad requirement that employers keep workplaces free of recognized hazards, and it does so aggressively. In April 2026 the agency renewed its Heat National Emphasis Program for five more years. Between April 2022 and December 2024 it ran roughly 7,000 heat-related inspections, issued 60 citations, sent nearly 1,400 hazard alert letters, and pulled about 1,400 workers out of dangerous heat conditions. Heat penalties topped $2 million in 2024 alone (Environment+Energy Leader, 2026).

Here is the part small residential contractors keep missing. Under the emphasis program, any day the heat index hits 80 degrees is a "heat priority day," and inspectors can walk onto a high-risk site proactively, no complaint and no incident required. In Phoenix, Dallas, Houston, or Miami, that describes most workdays from April through October. Your job site is inspectable for heat on the majority of the building season, and most supers I talk to have never heard of the program.

A sensor that sees it coming

Water, rest, and shade are the law in California and common sense everywhere else. A newer question is whether a sensor can see heat illness before the worker feels it, because by the time a framer feels dizzy, his core temperature has been climbing for an hour.

Kenzen built an armband that predicts core body temperature from heart rate, activity, and skin and ambient temperature, then buzzes the worker and pings the supervisor's dashboard when the prediction crosses the danger line, with a second alert when it is safe to return to work. A peer-reviewed validation study compared the algorithm against the gold standards, rectal probes and ingestible thermometer pills, and found a correlation of 0.94 with an average error of three-tenths of a degree Celsius, with accuracy holding up above 38.5 degrees, the range where heat injuries actually happen (International Journal of Environmental Research and Public Health, validation study). McCarthy Building Companies has deployed core-temp wearables from Kenzen and StrongArm Technologies on solar installation crews, which one of their safety executives called among the riskiest work for heat in the company, and uses the data to adjust schedules and break counts per shift (HR Brew, 2022).

I want to be straight about what this is and is not. Three-tenths of a degree of error means the device will cry wolf sometimes, and a vest that beeps for no reason gets ignored by the third day, which makes false alarms a safety problem of their own. Validation trials were also small, nineteen pill trials and thirty-three probe trials run under controlled conditions, nothing like a full shift of sheathing walls at 105 degrees, and the surveillance question is real. Kenzen's design lets only the worker see his own health detail while the supervisor sees only the alert, but not every vendor builds it that way, and a day-labor crew with immigration worries is not going to strap on a company-monitored biometric device because a safety consultant said so. Trust is the deployment bottleneck, not the algorithm.

Strip the hype and the pitch gets narrower and stronger. No wearable replaces water, shade, or a foreman who watches his people. It catches the worker who will not admit he is in trouble, which on a framing crew is most of them, because admitting it costs you the day's pay and maybe the next job.

Cheapest fix: a schedule

Strip away the sensors and the statutes and the acronyms, and the prevention program that would have saved most of those first-week deaths fits on an index card. OSHA calls it acclimatization, and the rule of thumb is the 20 percent rule: a new or returning worker does no more than 20 percent of a normal workload on day one in heat, then ramps up by about 20 percent a day. Day one is orientation and light duty. Day five is full speed. Pair the new hire with a veteran buddy for the first three days, because the buddy system exists for exactly the worker who will not report his own symptoms (OSHA Beat the Heat training materials).

Run the cost against the alternative. A serious Cal/OSHA heat violation now runs up to $25,000. That willful Parkwood case ran $276,425, while a 20 percent ramp schedule costs one week of reduced output per new hire, plus a pop-up canopy, a water cooler, and a supervisor who actually checks. In the CDC's review of twenty heat enforcement cases, every single one had an incomplete or absent heat illness prevention program with no provision for acclimatizing new workers. Not most cases. All twenty.

That is the uncomfortable conclusion, and I will not soften it. Everyone in the industry knows the fix, the fix is cheap, and still it is not being done. Construction's heat death toll is not a technology problem. It is a management decision repeated at ten thousand job sites every Monday morning of every summer, usually by people who have never been told that the new guy's first day is the most dangerous day of his working life.

What we did not prove

BLS heat fatality counts are acknowledged undercounts, and industry was suppressed or missing in more than a quarter of 2023 heat deaths, so the true construction share is unknown. Heat is also not the leading killer on residential sites; falls are, and a heat program that displaces fall-protection spending is a bad trade. One more honest accounting: the wearable validation study was small and lab-controlled, and no published study yet measures whether core-temp wearables reduce actual heat illness rates on residential job sites. Those penalty figures are California's schedule; federal OSHA penalties differ. No dataset tracks what share of residential general contractors actually maintain written heat plans, and the CDC's twenty-case review was enforcement-selected, which skews toward the worst actors. Acclimatization timelines vary by fitness and medical condition; Oregon's standard notes seven to fourteen days for fit workers and longer for others, so the five-day 20 percent ramp is guidance, not a guarantee.

Day one

Every crew has a first day for somebody, and in the summer hiring surge there are a lot of somebodies. You cannot change the thermometer, but the schedule is the thing you control.

Start the new hire at 20 percent. Give him a buddy and a water bottle and a shady place to sit that is actually close to the work. Write the plan down, because the inspector will ask for it and the family will ask why there wasn't one. Every year the technology gets better, and someday the armband will be as standard as the hard hat. Until then, the most advanced heat safety system on a residential job site is a foreman who knows that day one is the day that kills, and acts like it.
