# Research: AI Lift Planning for Residential Crane Picks (Truss Sets)

## Angle
Truss-set day is the most dangerous and most expensive single day of a residential framing job. A crane dangles your $25,000-40,000 roof system over your crew, and almost nobody on a residential job writes a lift plan. The software to model the lift in 3D — crane selection, load charts, ground bearing pressure, rigging angles — exists, is mature, and the biggest crane rental company in North America gives the planning service away free to customers. There is even a VR simulator so the operator, riggers, and signal person can rehearse the lift before arriving. Residential builders barely use any of it.

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes. If you are building: demand a written lift plan before truss day, verify the operator's certification, and know what the crane day costs when it goes wrong. If you are a small GC: the arithmetic of one lost crane day versus planning.

## Original contribution: the lost-crane-day arithmetic
Nobody publishes this comparison. A typical residential truss set: one boom truck at ~$300/hr for a 4-hour minimum = $1,200 base, realistically $1,600-$2,400 all-in with mob/demob and fees. A six-person framing crew at ~$35/hr fully loaded = $210/hr, $1,680 for the day. If the crane is wrong-sized, the ground is too soft for outriggers, or a pick can't clear the power lines, you lose the day: ~$3,300-$4,100 burned, plus remobilization, plus the schedule slip that cascades into every trade behind framing. A 3D lift plan — crane selection against the actual load chart, ground bearing pressure under the outriggers, the swing path checked against obstructions — costs a fraction of that and the largest rental fleet in the country gives the service away free to its customers. The article will run this math explicitly.

## Strongest counterargument (full strength)
Software models the lift, not the jobsite. The 3D plan assumes the ground bearing capacity the engineer typed in, not the saturated clay after Tuesday's rain. It assumes the operator follows the plan, not the foreman shouting to hurry because the concrete truck is waiting. Most residential crane work isn't even done by crane companies: it's a boom truck rolled by the lumberyard or a "crane for hire" driver who shows up, eyeballs the setup, and starts picking. No lift director, no rigging plan, no documented ground assessment — and OSHA's Cranes and Derricks in Construction standard (1926 Subpart CC) applies whether the GC knows it exists or not. A VR rehearsal is a genuine advance for fleets that use it; it does nothing for the jobs where the planning step never happens because nobody knew it was a step.

## Limitations
- BLS crane-fatality detail is dated (2011-2017 granular set); recent years are grouped under broader construction categories, so the residential share of crane deaths cannot be isolated from public data.
- No independent audit exists of safety-outcome claims for 3D lift planning software; the evidence is adoption by major fleets (ALL Erection) and face-validity of the physics, not a controlled study.
- Crane rental rates vary enormously by market; the arithmetic uses published 2025-2026 rate sheets as representative inputs, not a quote for any specific job.
- The article could not verify 3D Lift Plan subscription pricing from a primary source; it relies on the documented fact that ALL offers lift planning free to rental customers.

## Skepticism notes
- Katerra/Veev rule applies to construction tech generally: the graveyard is full of software that modeled the perfect job. Lift planning is narrower and physics-based, which is why it has survived 20+ years — but adoption, not accuracy, is the failure mode here.
- VR rehearsal (3D Lift Vision) is marketed by the vendor; treat as a real capability with unproven safety ROI.
