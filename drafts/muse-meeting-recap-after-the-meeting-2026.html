Muse Won't Sit In on Your Meeting. Hand It the Recording and It Does the Part Nobody Wants.

By Frank DeLuca · November 25, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

It is 4:47 on a Tuesday and the call just ended. Eleven people were on it. Three decisions got made, two promises got offered, and one client said the words "let's circle back" in a tone that meant the opposite of circling back. Your notebook holds half a sentence and a name you cannot read. Somebody has to turn that hour into emails, calendar invites, and a task list before tomorrow morning, and that somebody is you.

Every meeting tool on the market will sell you the same thing for this moment: a transcript. Otter, Fireflies, Read AI, Fathom, Granola, all of them will hand you a clean record of what was said, with speakers labeled and action items extracted, and all of them will stop exactly there, at the edge of the work that actually eats your afternoon. A transcript is a record. It is not a follow-up.

That distinction is the whole story here, and it starts with an honest limitation that Meta has never tried to hide. Muse does not join your meetings. It sends no bot into the call, it records nothing itself, and no Meta page claims otherwise. An independent capability comparison last checked in August lists "joins and records your meetings" as a gap for Muse, with nothing documented. If you wanted an agent sitting in the room, you bought the wrong product.

What it does instead

Muse works the other end of the meeting. On September 29, 2026, Zoom announced an integration that brings Zoom meeting intelligence into Muse through the Zoom MCP server, so Muse can find relevant conversations and content from Zoom Meetings and My Notes, retrieve summaries and key takeaways, schedule new meetings, and take action on what comes next. Meta's small business launch the same day added Granola alongside Zoom, and Granola's job is the notes themselves: it captures the call on your own device, with no bot in the meeting, and Muse reads what it wrote.

Put the two connectors together and the workflow writes itself, in the exact form a sales setup guide published it: after each Zoom call, read the Granola notes, draft a follow-up with the three things the prospect cared about in their own words and the one next step you agreed, save it as a draft, and ask before sending. That last clause is doing more work than it looks like. Draft before send is the difference between an assistant and a liability, and it is a setting you should check rather than assume.

Once the draft exists, Muse can do the parts the notetakers never touch: put the next meeting on the calendar, file the decision where you will find it, and turn "I'll send the numbers" into a reminder that fires before you forget. One real user, Jenn Strends, who runs two brands and calls her Muse "Nessie," describes the same loop in plainer language: Muse tracks her speaking outreach, who she pitched, when, and when the follow-up is due, and drafts the follow-ups for her approval. She also had it build her 2026 business mileage log from her calendar, a job she had put off to the end of every year until the agent did it in an afternoon.

Three numbers worth doing the math on

First, the price of the transcript itself. Prices below come from the vendors' own pages, rechecked by independent comparisons on November 19 to 21, 2026. Otter Pro is $8.33 a user a month billed annually, with 1,200 transcription minutes a month and a 90-minute cap per meeting. Fireflies Pro is $10 a seat a month billed annually, with unlimited transcription and 8,000 minutes of storage per seat. Read AI Pro is $15 a user a month billed annually, with a free plan that stops at five transcripts a month. Fathom Premium is $16 a month billed annually. Granola Business is $14 a user a month billed annually, and what it sells is not minutes but history: the free plan keeps 30 days of notes, the paid plan keeps all of them.

Second, what the same workload costs on each meter. For 25 hours of meetings a month, for one person, for one year, billed annually, a side-by-side read of the three vendors' pricing pages on November 20, 2026 gives Granola at $168, Fireflies at $228, and Otter at $239.88. Otter looks cheapest on the headline and costs the most on this workload, because its Pro cap is 1,200 minutes, which is 20 hours, which is below the 25-hour workload, and Otter minutes do not roll over, so the workload forces the Business tier at $19.99 a month. Granola charges by history, Otter by transcription minutes, Fireflies by stored minutes. Three tools, three different meters, and the cheapest headline price is not the cheapest bill.

Third, the follow-up budget, which is our arithmetic from stated assumptions rather than a measured study, so read it as an estimate. A worker with 25 hours of meetings a month sits in roughly 30 meetings. If each meeting generates 15 minutes of follow-up admin, the recap, the email, the calendar invite, the task entry, that is 7.5 hours a month, about a full working day, spent on work that starts after the meeting ends. Transcription itself takes zero of those minutes, because it is automated everywhere now. Otter, Fireflies, and Granola sell the transcript. Muse sells the 7.5 hours, and the test of that claim is simple: are the drafts it writes good enough to send with a glance instead of a rewrite?

Muse's marginal price for that workload is zero on top of a subscription you already hold, $0, $20, or $100 a month, because follow-up work is not metered per meeting. Two honest caveats belong on that sentence. It is only zero if you already pay for Muse, and scheduled runs draw on your weekly allowance, so a daily meeting digest costs allowance every time it runs. Nothing in this product is free; some of it is only already paid for.

The part where it can hurt you

Now the counterargument, stated at full strength, because it is stronger than the pitch. A dedicated notetaker is better at the note itself. Granola's bot-free capture and templates, Otter's speaker identification, Fireflies' hundred-plus languages: Muse reads their output, it does not produce it, so if the transcript is wrong, everything downstream is wrong, and Muse adds a second layer of interpretation on top of a first layer that already makes mistakes. Meeting notes apps say so themselves. Their documentation warns that AI-generated notes can miss or misstate things, and that the transcript is the record of exactly what was said, so treat it as the source of truth.

Worse, the follow-up work Muse offers is the part most worth keeping human. A follow-up email in the client's own words, drafted by an agent that was not in the room, can sound right and be wrong in a way a transcript cannot, because a transcript is a record and a draft is a commitment. If Muse mishears who promised what, the error does not sit in a document waiting to be corrected. It leaves the room as an email with your name on it, or a meeting on somebody else's calendar, or a task assigned to the wrong person. An agent that acts on a misheard action item is not a notetaker with a typo. It is a well-meaning colleague who confidently tells the client the wrong thing, and then cannot be asked what it was thinking.

There is also the question of whose account all of this runs on. Muse is one person's agent. There is no admin console, no team plan, no SSO, and no way for an IT department to see what it connects to. It stays on your personal Meta account when you leave the company, and its AI training toggle is switched on by default, which means the meeting notes you hand it can be used for training until you switch the toggle off. Switch it off on day one. Meta also warns, in its own help pages, that it does not review custom connectors or how they use your information, so grant access with caution.

Before you record anything, read this paragraph

California is an all-party consent state. Under Penal Code Section 632, it is illegal to record or eavesdrop on a confidential conversation without the consent of every person taking part, whether in person, by phone, or on a video call, and a separate statute, Section 632.7, covers calls involving a cell phone without any confidentiality requirement. Civil damages run to the greater of $5,000 per violation or three times actual damages, and multiple class actions have already been filed against AI transcription services, including Otter.ai and Google, under California's Invasion of Privacy Act. About nine states require all-party consent, while federal law and most states require only one party's, and the working rule courts apply is that the strictest law among the participants' locations can govern.

Muse's design sidesteps part of this problem by accident: since it does not join the call, there is no bot to announce. But the recording still happens somewhere, Granola on your device or Zoom cloud recording, and the person who decides to record is the person the law holds responsible, not the software. Say at the top of the call that a notetaker is running, explain what it does, and get a clear yes. If someone declines, take notes by hand. Ten seconds of announcement converts a legal analysis into a non-issue, and skipping it converts a productivity habit into a $5,000-per-violation problem.

What to do in the next ten minutes

Five steps, in order, before the first meeting. First, connect Granola or Zoom, and check which one your meetings actually live in, because Muse reads the notes where they already are and cannot invent them. Second, open Settings, find the training toggle, and switch it off. Third, tell Muse the rule in plain language: after each call, read the notes, draft the follow-up, save it, and ask before sending anything. Fourth, run it on one low-stakes internal meeting and read the draft against the transcript before it goes anywhere, because the transcript is the source of truth and the draft is a guess. Fifth, put the consent sentence into your meeting habit: "I'm recording this for notes, is that all right with everyone?" Say it every time, even when you think you already have permission, because permission for last week's call is not permission for this one.

Then watch what it does with the follow-up, the way you would watch any new hire in their first week: check the names, check the numbers, check who promised what to whom. If the drafts need a rewrite every time, the 7.5 hours are not saved, they are moved, and you should say so and stop using it for anything but the recap. If they need a glance and a send, you have bought back a working day a month for the price of a subscription you were already paying, and the meeting that ends at 4:47 stops following you home.

The honest accounting

We did not run a controlled test of Muse's meeting follow-up against Otter, Fireflies, or Granola on the same meeting, so the quality comparison here is structural, what each tool does with the note, rather than measured. Pricing is list pricing from vendor pages as rechecked November 19 to 21, 2026 by the comparison sites cited below, and vendors run promotions, so prices move. The 7.5-hour follow-up estimate is our arithmetic from stated assumptions, 30 meetings and 15 minutes each, not a measured time study. The capability comparison showing Muse does not join meetings was last checked August 21, 2026, and no Meta page we found claims Muse joins calls as of November 25, 2026, but capabilities change fast and this could be out of date by the time you read it. The California law summary is general information from law-firm and legal-guide sources, not legal advice, and recording law turns on the facts of a specific conversation.

None of that changes the decision in front of you. Transcription is solved, and every vendor will sell it to you for ten to twenty dollars a month. What is not solved is the hour after the meeting, when the decisions cool and the promises blur and the person who has to turn talk into work is still you. Muse cannot sit in the room. It can do the paperwork after you leave it, and it can do it wrong in ways a transcript never could. Hand it the recording, read the draft, and keep your name on the send button. That is the deal, and it is worth exactly as much as the hour it saves you.
