The Five Feet Around Your House Is an Underwriting Department Now

By Elena Vasquez · September 19, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Joan Van Kuren of Modesto opened her mail early this year and found a letter from CSAA, the AAA-affiliated insurer that had covered her home for years. Her policy would not be renewed, the letter citing a “substantial increase in hazards” around her property, the evidence arriving as photographs taken from the sky. Van Kuren told CBS Sacramento the images were wrong. They did not show her home as it actually stood. CSAA denied the drone. It confirmed the photos, calling them proprietary aerial imagery. Whatever the camera, the judgment arrived from above.

Van Kuren’s case is the shape of the new underwriting. Carriers have stopped sending inspectors up driveways in fire country and started reading roofs from orbit. Satellites, manned aircraft, and drones now feed machine-vision models that score individual houses for ignition risk: how close the vegetation stands, how flammable the brush reads, what the roof is made of. Axios reported in September that MetLife is working with the Bay Area startup Zesty.ai to score properties structure by structure, folding decades of wildfire data and fire-science research into a single number per address, a number the homeowner never sees until it arrives in the mail as a non-renewal notice or a premium hike.

The Geometry of the Five Feet

Every insurer flying over California is reading the same map. State law, Public Resources Code section 4291, draws three concentric rings around a home in wildfire territory: zero to five feet, five to thirty, thirty to one hundred. Ring one is the ember-resistant zone. Its rule is absolute: Nothing combustible within five feet of the structure. Not mulch. Not juniper. Not the firewood stacked neatly against the garage wall.

Translating that geometry into a working standard, the Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety built its Wildfire Prepared Home standard, the closest thing the industry has to a defensible-space building code, written not by legislators but by the insurers who will eventually have to pay for the houses that ignore it. Inside thirty feet, tree limbs must clear six vertical feet above the ground, crowns must hold ten feet of horizontal spacing, and privacy hedges, those continuous walls of green so beloved by suburban Californians, are not permitted at all. Shrubs must sit two times their own height apart, with a ten-foot cap. Annual grasses get cut below four inches. These are not suggestions; they are the line items on the checklist that decides whether a home earns the designation.

Stand at your front door and walk the perimeter. Within five feet of your walls, the flyover is looking for bark mulch against the foundation, volatile shrubs like juniper and cypress, a wood fence that attaches to the house, plastic trash bins, coir doormats, and patio furniture left against the siding. Fuel, all of it, and every leaf reads as fuel from the air. Replacing bark mulch with gravel, moving the bins to metal containers set farther out, and breaking the fence line where it meets the structure are the cheapest fixes in wildfire hardening, and they are exactly the fixes a machine reader can verify.

Fifty-Five to One

55 : 1 Roughly fifty-five California policies non-renewed by State Farm for every one home that has earned an IBHS Wildfire Prepared Home designation across California and Oregon combined.

State Farm announced in March 2024 that it would not renew about 72,000 California property policies through 2025, citing wildfire risk. Against that, the IBHS has issued just over 1,300 Wildfire Prepared Home designations across California and Oregon combined. Do the division: 72,000 divided by 1,300 is roughly 55. For every home that has earned the industry’s own certification of wildfire readiness, insurers have walked away from fifty-five policies. Certified mitigation covers less than two percent of the scale of withdrawal, which is to say almost none of it. Risk can be scored from orbit at machine speed, yet fixes are still verified at human speed, one $125 application and one exterior inspection at a time.

The Counterargument, Stated Honestly

There is a real case for the flyover, and it deserves its full weight. What it replaced was not a golden age of careful human judgment. In-person inspections were rare, inconsistent, and subjective, and when they failed, insurers fell back on ZIP-code fire maps that punished careful homeowners and careless ones alike. A machine reading the same five-foot zone on every house is more consistent than any inspector, and consistency applied at the scale of millions of properties is a form of fairness that no county fire marshal’s office, however dedicated, could ever deliver. More importantly, granular scoring is what lets insurers keep writing policies in fire country at all. Without aerial underwriting, the alternative is not gentler underwriting. It is no underwriting.

California is also forcing the process into the open. Assembly Bill 1559 would require insurers to disclose the aerial images behind adverse underwriting decisions, and Insurance Commissioner Ricardo Lara has backed it, saying his department’s investigations found “serious flaws” in how companies use the imagery, including photos that contained errors or were no longer current. A homeowner who receives a non-renewal notice accompanied by annotated imagery showing the juniper against the house has something the ZIP-code casualty never did: a punch list, itemized in pixels, that turns an opaque underwriting decision into a weekend of yard work.

What to Actually Do

Start with the five feet. Photograph everything. All four sides of your home, showing the zero-to-five-foot zone. That is what the IBHS evaluator will ask for, and it is what your insurer’s model already has. Clear the combustibles, swap bark for stone, and document the work with dates, because California’s Safer from Wildfires framework ties verified mitigation to premium discounts, which means the photos you take this weekend could be worth real money on your next renewal. Documentation is what turns a weekend of yard work into a credit your carrier must honor, and in a market where carriers are openly looking for reasons to leave, documented mitigation is the difference between a renewal and a FAIR Plan application. A Wildfire Prepared Home application costs $125, the designation lasts three years with annual maintenance reviews, and Colorado just made mitigation credits mandatory for insurers, which is a preview of where California’s rules are going.

If you are buying in fire country, add the flyover to your diligence and ask the seller for the aerial assessment. The same way you would ask for the roof certification. Building? Treat the five-foot zone as a design constraint from the first sketch, not a landscaping afterthought. Noncombustible hardscaping, gravel beds, and metal-edged planters cost no more than bark mulch and a juniper hedge when they are drawn into the plan instead of retrofitted after the non-renewal letter, and the machine reading your five-foot zone will never know the difference between a designed defense and a desperate one, though your premium will. Design the defensible space into the architecture from the first sketch, and the machine reader circling overhead becomes a formality, checking boxes you already checked.

What This Piece Does Not Prove

No published outcome study links AI flyover scoring to actual structure survival. Models predict ignition risk; nobody has shown that a house scoring well in the machine’s eyes burns less often than its neighbor, which means the entire edifice of aerial underwriting rests on correlation and actuarial logic rather than demonstrated survival outcomes. That fifty-five-to-one ratio compares one carrier’s California non-renewals against a two-state certification count, so the true California gap is worse than stated, not better. And the mitigation cost figures cited for Zone 0 work reflect industry ranges, not contractor quotes verified for this piece. Treat the score as a signal, not a verdict. Then go move the firewood.

Van Kuren’s insurer looked at her house from the sky and saw hazards. Whether the camera was right or wrong, the direction is clear. No strip of your property is more scrutinized now than those five feet. Act like it. Design it like it matters. The machine already thinks it does.
