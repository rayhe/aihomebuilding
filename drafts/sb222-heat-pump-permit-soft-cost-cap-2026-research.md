# Research: The $200 Permit Cap Sacramento Just Passed (and What It Means for Your Heat Pump Quote)

**Slug:** `sb222-heat-pump-permit-soft-cost-cap-2026`
**Article number:** 874
**Journalist:** Priya Greenwood (sustainability / utility bills)
**Angle:** California's legislature passed SB 222, the Heat Pump Access Act, in August 2026. It caps heat pump permit fees at $200 ($150 for water heaters), limits cities to one permit per project, bans HOAs from vetoing heat pumps, and mandates instant permitting by 2028. Nobody prices this into a heat pump quote today, but soft costs are the reason a $304/year energy saving never becomes a purchase.

## Kill test
Does this help someone building or buying a home? Yes. A California homeowner pricing a heat pump install can now: (1) refuse a two-permit demand, (2) cite the fee caps at the counter, (3) tell their HOA the veto is illegal, (4) know which provisions are live vs. 2027/2028. A contractor can stop budgeting weeks of plan-check delay into quotes.

Context: 6 million heat pumps by 2030 goal, 7 million climate-ready homes by 2035, 600+ jurisdictions setting their own rules.

## Original contribution (modeled soft-cost stack + the Permit Counter Playbook)
Modeled: a straight gas-furnace-to-heat-pump swap in a 600-jurisdiction state today, before SB 222's caps bite:
- Permit fee: $300-600 (varies by city; plan-check surcharges)
- Second trade permit (panel/feeder for the heat pump circuit): $150-400, plus its own inspection visit
- Contractor plan-check delay: 2-6 weeks, during which the dead furnace stays dead and the quote's "expedite" line item grows
- HOA architectural review: 30-90 days and one retiree's $6,500 in lost incentives (Canary)
Under the final bill: $200 cap (HVAC) / $150 (HPWH), one permit, no duplicative info beyond the CF1R, asynchronous inspection option from 2027, automated permitting for large jurisdictions by July 2028. The model is illustrative, not a survey average; assumptions stated.
The Playbook (five rights with effective dates): one permit max; fee caps; no HOA veto; no site plans for swapouts; no noise rules for inverter units. Plus the one thing the bill does NOT do: jurisdictions that demonstrate higher costs can exceed the caps, and nothing mandates instant permits before July 2028.

## Limitations
- As of Sept 16, 2026, the bill has passed the legislature but the governor has not acted (deadline Sept 30). Article must frame provisions as "if signed."
- Key enforcement dates are 2027 (asynchronous inspections) and July 1, 2028 (automated/instant permitting for larger jurisdictions). A homeowner permitting in fall 2026 gets the fee caps and one-permit rule, not the instant-permit lane.
- Fee caps: $150 HPWH / $200 HVAC per the final Canary summary; an earlier committee analysis had $250 for HVAC. The final enrolled text would resolve this; report the $200 with the caveat.
- Modeled soft-cost stack is illustrative; no California-wide permit-fee survey for heat pumps exists in these sources. State the assumption.
- TECH Clean California savings figures ($304/$97) are cited in the Wiener release; the underlying dataset was not independently pulled.

## Strongest counterargument
The caps have an escape hatch: jurisdictions that demonstrate higher costs can charge more, and small cities (under 5,000) and small counties are exempt from the instant-permitting mandate. The 25C federal credit is dead as of the end of 2025, so the $304/year savings story is running without the federal rebate stack that made early-adopter math work. And the provisions that matter most, instant permitting and async inspections, do not bite until 2027-2028, by which time another legislature could amend them. SB 222 fixes the paperwork; it does not fix the electrician shortage, the panel upgrade, or the fact that a heat pump quote in the Bay Area still starts around $20,000. Soft costs were never the biggest line on the invoice, and the bill's fans sometimes talk like they were.
