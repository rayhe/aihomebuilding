Your Home Leaks $60 a Year for Every Point of Air Leakage. Most Older Homes Have Eight.

By Priya Greenwood · September 26, 2026
← Back to articles

An energy auditor zipped a red nylon shroud into Maria Santos's front door frame on a Tuesday morning, switched on a calibrated fan, and depressurized her 1962 ranch house to the equivalent of a 20-mile-per-hour wind hitting every wall at once. Twenty minutes later he handed her a number: 8.4, not a grade and not a pass or fail. Just 8.4 air changes per hour at 50 pascals, which is the building industry's way of saying her house swaps its entire volume of indoor air with the outdoors more than eight times an hour under test pressure. Santos looked at the number the way you look at a lab result with no reference range. Then she asked the question the industry has somehow never answered in dollars: what does 8.4 cost me?

Nobody at the audit could tell her, and that silence is the story. Blower door testing has been codified since the 2012 energy code, the 2021 code made 3.0 ACH50 the legal ceiling for new homes in most climate zones, and an entire profession now walks around with a diagnostic that produces a precise number and no price tag. Your car gets miles per gallon, your refrigerator gets kilowatt-hours per year on a yellow sticker, and your house gets 8.4, good luck. So I built the missing price tag, using the Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory infiltration model and plain degree-day arithmetic, and the answer turns the cheapest energy retrofit in the house into the one nobody talks about.

A number with no price tag

Start with what the number means. ACH50 counts how many times a home's full air volume leaks out and gets replaced in one hour while a blower door holds the house at 50 pascals of pressure. Real weather never pushes that hard, so building scientists convert test leakage to natural leakage with a ratio called N. Lawrence Berkeley Lab, which invented this math, suggests N equals 20 as a conservative default, and publishes zone-by-zone tables that put a two-story home in Climate Zone 3 right at 20 to 23. Divide ACH50 by 20 and you get the air quietly leaving your house on an ordinary day.

Typical existing American homes test between 5 and 15 ACH50. New construction in a state enforcing the 2021 code must hit 3.0 or better, while Passive House certification demands 0.6. Pause on that spread for a moment, because it means a drafty 1960s ranch can leak twenty times more air than the tightest new builds, and until now nobody had translated that spread into the language every homeowner actually speaks, which is the utility bill.

Doing the math the industry skipped

Here is the full calculation, because a number without its inputs is just another 8.4. Take a 2,400-square-foot home with 8-foot ceilings, a volume of 19,200 cubic feet. One ACH50 point equals 320 cubic feet per minute under test pressure, and divided by the LBNL factor of 20, that becomes 16 cubic feet per minute of natural infiltration, every minute, all year. Heating that infiltrating air is freshman physics: 1.08 times CFM times 24 hours times heating degree days. In a mild California climate with 3,000 heating degree days, one ACH50 point demands 1.24 million BTU a year, which is 365 kilowatt-hours of heat. Run it through a heat pump at COP 3.0 and you burn 122 kilowatt-hours of electricity. At 34 cents a kilowatt-hour, that is $41 a year for heating. Cooling adds roughly $18 more per point at typical air-conditioner efficiency. Total: about $60 a year, per ACH50 point, in a mild climate.

Sixty dollars sounds small, but it is not small. Santos's 8.4, dragged down to the code maximum of 3.0, is 5.4 points times $60, or roughly $320 a year, every year, for air she never asked to heat. In a cold climate with 7,500 heating degree days the heating side roughly doubles, pushing each point toward $80 or $100 a year. In hot-humid country the latent load, the energy spent wringing moisture out of infiltrating Gulf air, piles on top of the sensible number, so $60 is a floor there, not a ceiling. And because this is physics rather than marketing, the math cross-checks against every published figure I could find: the Department of Energy attributes 25 to 40 percent of heating and cooling energy to air leakage, which on an $1,800 annual HVAC bill lands exactly where my model puts an 8-to-12 ACH50 house, and field programs keep confirming it, with Efficiency Maine documenting $175 a year in savings from a $350 air-sealing job, a two-year payback that my arithmetic predicts almost to the dollar.

The hole in your wall

If those numbers still feel abstract, try geometry instead. Building scientists convert blower door readings into something called equivalent leakage area, which answers a vivid question: if you gathered every crack, gap, and unsealed penetration in the house into one opening, how big would it be? The industry's rule-of-thumb conversion says Santos's 8.4 ACH50 ranch has the equivalent of a one-square-foot hole cut straight through its walls, open to the weather, twenty-four hours a day, all year. A 12 ACH50 house, common in pre-1970 stock, carries something closer to a bathroom window left open permanently. You would never tolerate that if you could see it. You tolerate it because it is distributed across a thousand tiny gaps: top plates, recessed can lights, plumbing chases, the attic hatch, the rim joist. Oak Ridge National Laboratory measured individual penetrations and found an unsealed electrical box leaks 21 cubic feet per minute under test pressure while a sealed one leaks 0.24, which means a single lazy electrician's box can matter more than an entire wall of windows.

The code draws a line. States keep moving it.

Credit where it is due: the code has been tightening. The 2012 energy code first required the blower door test with no visual-inspection escape hatch. The 2021 edition set the residential limit at 3.0 ACH50. The 2024 edition pushes toward 2.0 to 2.5, and Illinois, Rhode Island, Nevada, and North Dakota are enforcing it. Then comes the American part. Louisiana amended its requirement to 7 ACH50 in Climate Zone 2. Florida enforces 5 with amendments. Idaho raised its limit to 5. Indiana lets builders substitute a visual inspection, which is the regulatory equivalent of grading your own exam. So the same 2,400-square-foot house is legally twice as leaky in Baton Rouge as in Chicago, and the blower door number that determines your utility bill for the next thirty years depends on which side of a state line the slab was poured. Codes are minimums, and minimums with amendments are suggestions.

The honest objections

Now the part the air-sealing evangelists skip, because sealing a house is not free and I will not pretend otherwise. Tighten below about 3 ACH50 and the house stops breathing on its own, which is when ASHRAE Standard 62.2 steps in and requires mechanical ventilation: for the archetype home in this article, a 2,400-square-foot four-bedroom, the formula demands roughly 62 cubic feet per minute of continuous fresh air. The 2013 edition of the standard deliberately removed the old infiltration credit, meaning the leakier your house, the less fan you need, and the tighter it gets, the more of the job shifts to equipment. A heat-recovery or energy-recovery ventilator runs $2,000 to $3,500 installed, draws fan power around the clock, and eats a real share of the savings the sealing created. That is the correct trade, sealed plus ventilated beats leaky every time, but it is a trade, and anyone quoting sealing savings without the ventilation line item is selling something.

Two more caveats deserve daylight. Tightening changes how a house depressurizes, which matters if you have atmospherically vented combustion appliances; building performance standards require worst-case spillage testing before envelope work for exactly this reason, and skipping it is how carbon monoxide stories start. And diminishing returns are real: a published study of manufactured homes found that a full "house doctoring" retrofit cut leakage only 19.5 percent, because the first points are cheap caulk and the last points are archaeology. The math in this article is kindest to the leakiest houses, which is precisely where the work should start.

Priya's air-sealing playbook: buyers, builders, owners
  1. Buying? Demand the blower door number before close, the way you would demand a Carfax. In 2021-code states new homes must test at 3.0 ACH50 or better; on an existing home, anything above 7 means the seller is handing you a utility bill with the keys.
  2. Building? Seal before drywall, when every top plate, can light, and plumbing chase is still visible. One unsealed electrical box leaks 21 CFM50 against 0.24 sealed. Retrofit sealing costs three to five times more than doing it at rough-in, because access is everything.
  3. Owning? Budget $300 to $500 for the test and $1,000 to $1,500 for professional sealing of a standard home. At 5 points of improvement the payback is three to five years in mild climates and about two in cold ones. DIY materials run $100 to $350 if you start with the attic hatch, can lights, and rim joist.
  4. Going below 3 ACH50? Price the ventilation with the sealing. ASHRAE 62.2 wants about 62 CFM continuous for a 2,400-square-foot four-bedroom, and an ERV or HRV at 75 percent recovery keeps the fresh air from erasing the savings.
  5. Have a gas water heater or furnace in a closet? Get combustion safety testing with the blower door, not after. Tightening without a spillage check is the one way this retrofit gets dangerous.

What this article did not prove

The $60-per-point figure rests on the LBNL N-equals-20 conversion, which is approximate to roughly plus or minus 30 percent depending on shielding, height, and how leaks are distributed around the envelope; the dollar figure inherits that band, so read $60 as $45 to $80. Degree days use the standard 65-degree base and a mild-climate archetype; cold and hot-humid scaling is stated as a range, not modeled to the same precision. Cooling counts sensible load only, which understates hot-humid climates where latent load dominates. Electricity at 34 cents and gas at $1.90 per therm are 2026 California figures; your utility will differ, and the arithmetic is linear, so scale it. Air-sealing costs are market ranges from contractor surveys and efficiency programs, not quotes for your house. Blower door protocol follows RESNET/ICC 380 and ASTM E779/E1827, and none of this overrides your authority having jurisdiction. Until the inspector signs off, the red tag is always right.
