Your Listing Says 2,400 Square Feet. The Permit File Says 2,000. The Missing 400 Could Cost You $50,000.

By Catherine Chen, September 18, 2026, Policy & Regulation

Page three of the escrow paperwork is where the romance dies. Everything before it was granite countertops and morning light in the kitchen. Page three is numbers, and on this particular Tuesday the numbers refused to agree with each other. Listing: 2,400 square feet, four bedrooms. County assessor: 2,000 square feet, three bedrooms. Four hundred square feet and one bedroom had materialized out of thin air, and the seller's agent had a cheerful explanation involving a finished basement and different county conventions, which is the real estate equivalent of "the check is in the mail." Buyers hear this story constantly. Most of them shrug and initial the page; do not, because that is a mistake the municipal code does not forgive.

Here is the part nobody tells you at the open house: when you buy a house, you buy its permit history, and the city holds the current owner responsible for violations, not whoever swung the hammer. Discover an unpermitted addition after closing and the fines land on you, the removal order lands on you, and the retroactive permit process, which involves hiring an architect to draw as-built plans of someone else's construction and then opening walls so inspectors can verify work you did not do, lands on you. A homeowner in New Jersey learned this when the municipality discovered the previous owner had added a second story without permits. Options: legalize it or demolish it. Legalization required structural engineering and code upgrades that ran past $50,000, and he had owned the house for less than a year.

Your lender already knows. You don't.

Over 400 mortgage lenders run permit data on collateral before approving loans, and the GSE guidelines explicitly recommend permitting records as third-party verification of property improvements. That data comes mostly from one place: BuildFax, now part of Verisk, which maintains what it describes as the first automated national database of historical building permits, covering more than 90 million properties across 7,500 jurisdictions, with over 10 billion data points at the permit level. Your bank checks whether the improvements exist in the permit record, and your bank's appraiser checks it too, through appraisal software that pulls BuildFax reports directly into the report. Meanwhile the buyer, the person with the most money at risk and the least institutional protection, walks into the transaction with a Zillow printout. Think about what that means, because the institution lending you $800,000 runs a machine check on whether the bedroom you are paying for legally exists, and you are the only party at the table who has not run the same check, since nobody told you the check existed. Lopsided does not begin to describe it.

Insurance carriers are waking up to the same dataset from the other direction. ZestyAI ran its computer-vision models over aerial imagery for a million U.S. properties and found that 45 percent contained more than one structure, structures the carriers often did not know about, which means policies priced on incomplete information and claims denied when the unknown structure turns out to be the one that burned. That is the insurer's version of the buyer's problem: same blind spot, same technology, same fix, which is to compare what the paperwork says against what actually exists before money changes hands.

The three-document reconciliation

Nobody has published this as a buyer workflow, so I will. It takes an afternoon and three documents. One: the listing, with its square footage and bedroom count; two: the county assessor's record, which is public and free, showing what the tax authority believes exists. Three: a permit-history report covering the gap between the assessor's last update and today. Reconcile them. If the listing claims 2,400 square feet and the assessor says 2,000, those 400 feet must appear as a permitted addition in the permit history. If they do not, you have a documented discrepancy, and the inspection period exists precisely for documented discrepancies. Renegotiate, demand the seller legalize the work, take a credit, or walk. All of those options are available to you before closing. After is too late: none of them survive the signature, which is when the liability transfers and the city's enforcement apparatus starts addressing its correspondence to your name.

Run the economics honestly. A permit-history report costs a rounding error next to a five-figure legalization bill. In Los Angeles, the escalation math on a single non-compliance citation tells the story: the initial fee is $660, and if it goes unpaid for 30 days, a late charge of twice the fee plus a 50 percent collection fee brings the total to $2,310, before interest at one percent per month, before a single dollar of construction, and before the misdemeanor exposure of up to $1,000 and six months per violation. Nobody should read those numbers and conclude that permitting is cheap; read them and conclude that discovering the problem during escrow is cheap, because every one of those costs is negotiable leverage when you hold it, and a personal invoice when you don't.

What the report can't tell you

Now the skepticism, because a database is not a verdict. Permit records are only as good as the jurisdictions that digitize them, and rural counties with paper archives from 1987 are not in anyone's API, which means a clean report proves nothing was recorded, not that nothing was built. Assessors misrecord square footage for innocent reasons: counties count finished basements, enclosed porches, and converted garages differently, and sometimes the assessor is simply wrong in the buyer's favor. My reconciliation method produces discrepancies, not conclusions. Every flag it raises needs a human being, a contractor, an architect, or ten minutes with the building department's permit counter, to resolve. Buyers who treat the report as a lie detector will kill good deals over clerical errors, and in a competitive market, the buyer who demands perfect paperwork on every 1950s ranch will be outbid by the buyer who understands which discrepancies matter.

There is also the uncomfortable fact that enforcement is selective: cities with a thousand unpermitted garage conversions do not have a thousand code officers. Many unpermitted additions sit quietly for decades, harming no one, until a neighbor complains, a fire starts, or an insurance adjuster shows up. Rational buyers in hot markets have looked at that enforcement lottery and decided the risk is worth the square footage. That is a coherent position, but it is just not an informed one unless you have actually priced the downside, which is what the permit file lets you do.

What this doesn't prove

No reliable national statistic exists for how much residential work goes unpermitted, so I will not invent one. The often-cited DIY figures, including a recent insurance-industry survey finding 35 percent of homeowners did additions themselves, describe who did the work, not whether permits were pulled. My $50,000 New Jersey case is one anecdote, not a median, and ZestyAI's 45 percent multi-structure finding measures insurer blind spots, not violations; I use it as evidence the detection technology works, not as a violation rate. Los Angeles fee figures are jurisdiction-specific and other cities differ widely. I could not verify current consumer pricing for permit-history reports, so I state no price. Read the reconciliation method as a screening tool with real blind spots, not a guarantee: a screen, not a verdict.

What to do before your inspection period expires

Four steps, in order. First, pull the assessor's record for the property before you make an offer; it is public, free, and takes ten minutes. Second, compare it against the listing line by line, square footage, bedroom and bath count, and flag every delta. Third, order a permit-history report and check whether each delta appears as a permitted improvement; BuildFax says it serves everyday property buyers and sellers alongside lenders and carriers, and your agent's E&O carrier may even discount their premium for pulling one, which tells you how seriously the industry takes this. Fourth, take any unresolved discrepancy to your inspector and your agent during the contingency period and convert it into leverage: seller legalizes, seller credits, price adjusts, or you walk. The code does not care about your feelings, your timeline, or how much you loved the kitchen; it cares about what is in the file, so read the file before you sign.
