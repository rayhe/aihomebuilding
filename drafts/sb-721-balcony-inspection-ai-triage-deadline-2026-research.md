# Research: SB 721 Balcony Inspections, Ledger Failures, and AI-Assisted Triage
**Slug:** sb-721-balcony-inspection-ai-triage-deadline-2026
**Journalist:** Catherine Chen (policy/regulation beat)
**Date:** September 6, 2026

## The hook
California's SB 721 balcony-inspection deadline passed January 1, 2026, eight months ago. Thousands of apartment buildings are out of compliance right now. The law was written because a Berkeley balcony collapsed in 2015 and killed six people. Meanwhile, inspection firms are selling drone flights and AI photo analysis as a way through the backlog. The question the law actually asks: who is allowed to sign the report, and what can a camera never see?

## Primary sources
1. **CA SB 721 / SB 326 statutory framework** — via Burgelman Homes 2026 guide (https://burgelmanhomes.com/blog/sb-721-sb-326-california-deck-laws): SB 721 applies to rental buildings of 3+ units; exterior elevated elements (balconies, decks, stairways, landings, walkways) 6+ feet above grade with wood/wood-based supports; initial inspection deadline Jan 1, 2026 (extended from Jan 1, 2025 by AB 2579); re-inspection every 6 years. SB 326 (condos/HOAs, Civil Code 5551): initial deadline Jan 1, 2025, every 9 years. Both deadlines have now passed; non-compliant owners face consequences at sale, refinance, disclosure.
2. **Berkeley balcony collapse, June 15, 2015** — Library Gardens apartment, fifth-floor balcony failed during a 21st birthday gathering. Six killed, seven injured (per Bornstein Law: https://bornstein.law/california-balcony-inspection-law-sb-721-before-2026-deadline/). Cause: severe dry rot / water intrusion in concealed wood framing. AAOA concurs (https://american-apartment-owners-association.org/property-management/how-sb-721-sb-326-balcony-inspections-change-insurance-coverage/).
3. **CPSC deck injury data** — via Murphy Bros (https://mbros.com/blog/what-causes-decks-to-fail): ~6,000 people/year injured by structural failure or collapse of decks, porches, railings, staircases. Killino Firm (https://www.killinofirm.com/news/injuries-resulting-from-porch-deck-collapse): CPSC study found nearly 250,000 people needed medical treatment from deck/balcony injuries over 5 years; 20,000+ from partial structural failure or total collapse; 4,600 ER visits tied to deck collapses and 1,900 to porch failures 2001-2016.
4. **InterNACHI deck statistics** — via same: ~60% of the 45 million US decks have safety issues; ~90% of deck collapses trace to ledger-board failure (the connection to the building). NADRA / Decksforlife (https://news.dawnreporter.com/story/546013/...): ~30 million decks in North America have outlived practical service life; 75%+ of people on a collapsing deck are injured or killed; most failures were visible and fixable beforehand.
5. **Inspection cost data** — DrBalcony (https://drbalcony.com/sb-721-inspection-requirements-for-california-apartment-buildings/): 3-10 units $2,000-$4,000; 11-30 units $4,000-$7,000; 31-50 units $7,000-$10,000; 50-100 units $10,000-$18,000; 100+ units $18,000+. Worked example: 20-unit LA building, $3,300 (base $2,500 + $500 wood testing + $300 location surcharge). Coastline Equity (https://coastlineequity.net/insights/california-sb-721-balcony-deck-inspection-mandate): $75-$150/unit visual; $200-$400/unit invasive; 100-unit property $7,500-$15,000 basic. AAOA: basic visual from $550/balcony. Under SB 721, repairs required within 120 days of report; reports retained 12+ years.
6. **AI wood-rot computer vision** — arXiv 2409.20137 "Segmenting Wood Rot using Computer Vision Models" (https://arxiv.org/abs/2409.20137v1): semantic segmentation of rot in wood-log crosscuts, 1,424-image dataset, best model IoU 0.71, near human-annotator agreement. Caveat: trained on log crosscuts in a lab/industrial setting, NOT on in-situ balcony framing. MDPI Woodot (https://www.mdpi.com/2071-1050/17/12/5574): AI-driven robotic defect segmentation on glulam beams, F1 up to 0.686. Drone use: DrBalcony notes high-rise/restricted-access balconies require scaffolding or drones — drones already in the inspection toolkit for access, not diagnosis.
7. **Inspector qualification bottleneck** — Bornstein Law: demand surging for licensed architects, structural/civil engineers, and qualified contractors as deadline approached; many owners waited until the last minute. SB 721 requires inspection by licensed architect, structural engineer, or qualified contractor; SB 326 requires licensed structural engineer or architect only.

## Original contribution (novel analysis)
**The sampling math nobody published:** SB 326 requires a "statistically significant random sample" (commonly interpreted as 95% confidence, ±5% margin). For a complex with N=100 exterior elevated elements, the finite-population sample size is n = N·Z²·p(1−p) / (e²(N−1) + Z²·p(1−p)) with Z=1.96, p=0.5, e=0.05 → n ≈ 80. For N=30 → n ≈ 28. In other words, on a mid-size complex the law's sampling rule means nearly the whole inventory gets hands-on inspection anyway. AI photo triage's real economic value is not reducing the sample count (the statute doesn't let you) but deciding which elements in the sample get the expensive invasive testing (probing, borescope, moisture meters at $200-$400/unit vs $75-$150/unit visual). If triage cuts the invasive subset from 80 to 20 on a 100-unit complex, the owner saves roughly 60 × $175 ≈ $10,500 — comparable to the entire inspection fee. That is the defensible ROI claim; anything claiming AI "replaces" the inspection is selling something the statute does not allow.

**Second original cross-check:** The Berkeley failure mode was concealed rot under waterproofing — invisible to cameras. InterNACHI's 90%-ledger figure and the PSPE field investigations (nail-only ledger attachments, missing flashing) both point to the connection zone, which is precisely where a drone photo shows the least. The article's skepticism core: vision AI is strongest where failures are weakest (surface boards, railings) and weakest where failures concentrate (the ledger, behind flashing).

## Limitations to state in article
- CPSC injury figures are estimates from NEISS sampling, not a census of collapses; the "90% ledger" figure is an InterNACHI estimate, not a peer-reviewed finding.
- arXiv wood-rot model trained on log crosscuts; no published study validates rot segmentation on in-situ balcony/deck framing in real lighting.
- Inspection cost ranges are vendor-published (DrBalcony, Coastline Equity); actual bids vary; repair costs after findings are the real budget risk and are building-specific.
- The sampling calculation uses the conventional 95%/±5% interpretation; the statute says "statistically significant" without fixing parameters, and local jurisdictions may interpret differently.
- SB 721 applies to rentals 3+ units; single-family decks are NOT covered — homeowners must self-inspect (the article must not imply otherwise).

## Strongest counterargument
The inspection industry's position: nothing substitutes for a licensed professional putting a probe into wood. AI photo tools create a moral hazard — owners buy a cheap drone "inspection," feel compliant, and skip the invasive work that would have found the rot. The Berkeley balcony looked fine from the outside too. If AI triage is marketed as inspection, it could make buildings less safe than the pre-AI status quo of doing nothing, because doing nothing at least doesn't generate a false clean bill of health.

## Actionable takeaways (draft)
- Renters/buyers in CA: ask for the SB 721/721-equivalent inspection report in disclosures; "not yet completed" 8 months past deadline is a negotiating fact and a safety flag.
- Small landlords (3-10 units): budget $2,000-$4,000 for inspection; the report is due now, not someday; repairs get 120 days.
- What to demand from any "AI inspection" vendor: (1) licensed architect/SE signs the report, (2) written criteria for which elements get invasive testing, (3) moisture-meter or probe readings at the ledger, not just photos.
- Homeowners (single-family, not covered by the law): the 90%-ledger rule applies to your deck too — annual check of the ledger connection, flashing, and fastener corrosion; lag screws/through-bolts, not nails.
