17,965 Fires a Year Start Inside Your Walls. A $200 Tube of Caulk Is the Fire Department.

By Jake Kowalski · October 8, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

The fire investigator wrote two words on the origin report, "wall cavity," not the kitchen and not the overloaded outlet everyone expected. A fire that started inside a wall, traveled through the hollow bones of the house, and arrived in the attic forty minutes later like it had taken an elevator. By the time anyone smelled smoke, the house was already lost. This happens, on average, forty-nine times a day.

Pull the National Fire Protection Association's area-of-origin tables for one- and two-family homes, add up the four concealed-space categories yourself because nobody at NFPA publishes the sum, and the number lands at 17,965 fires a year: wall assemblies, attic and ceiling-roof assemblies, ceiling and floor assemblies, and crawl spaces. Together they do about $926 million in direct property damage every year and kill roughly 80 people. The attic category alone, 7,551 fires, does $528 million in damage, which makes the space above your ceiling one of the most expensive places in America for a fire to start.

NFPA's own analysts add the detail that matters. Fires in concealed spaces cause a disproportionate share of damage because nobody finds them while they are small. A kitchen fire gets a panicked homeowner and a fire extinguisher inside ninety seconds. A fire inside a wall gets discovered when the paint starts bubbling, which is the wall's way of telling you the studs behind it are already gone.

The code knows, and it is shy about saying so

Fireblocking lives in section R302.11 of the International Residential Code, and it reads like a shy person at a party. The opening paragraph is a performance standard, not a prescription: cut off concealed draft openings, vertical and horizontal, and form an effective fire barrier between stories and between the top story and the roof space. Then it lists some places this matters. The list is not exhaustive, says the code commentary, which is code for "figure it out yourself," and then it goes on to list some places where this matters.

Builder Mike Guertin, writing in Fine Homebuilding, translated the shy code into seven places that actually get flagged: wall plates at ceiling and floor levels, which platform framing gives you for free; furred basement walls, blocked every ten feet horizontally and sealed at the top where the gap meets the floor system; soffits, drop ceilings, and cove ceilings, which connect walls to floors like hallways for flame; tub and shower drain cutouts; every pipe, cable, and duct penetration through plates; chimney and fireplace gaps, which need noncombustible material at least an inch deep; and stair stringers, blocked top and bottom so fire cannot ride the stairs from one floor to the next.

And here is the beautiful part: the materials list is scrap-bin stuff. Two-inch nominal lumber, plywood, half-inch gypsum, mineral wool or fiberglass batts stuffed tight, with no exotic chemistry and no $50,000 system anywhere in sight. The code commentary even blesses caulk, putty, and sealant around penetrations, provided the material resists the free passage of flame and combustion gases. Lumber, batts, and a caulk gun.

Sixty holes in the top plate

Now the part nobody photographs. Every wire, pipe, and duct that passes through a top or bottom plate leaves an annular gap, a ring of empty space around the penetrating object, and every one of those gaps is a chimney in waiting. A 2,500-square-foot two-story house carries roughly sixty cable penetrations through its plates plus another dozen or so for plumbing, ducts, and vents. Each takes about two minutes with a caulk gun.

Do the math the way an estimator would. Six to nine tubes of fire-rated caulk, per a working electrician on the Mike Holt trade forums, at $13 to $24 a tube retail for 3M's Fire Barrier CP 25WB+, plus two to three hours of labor. Materials $80 to $215, labor another $100 to $200 depending on the market. Call it $150 to $400 a house to seal every plate penetration, using a product tested to ASTM E814 that expands to three times its volume above 1,000 degrees. Against $926 million a year in concealed-space fire damage, that is the cheapest life-safety line item in residential construction. It is also the one most likely to be skipped, because nobody will ever see it.

The camera that finally looks

The inspection system for all of this is a person with a flashlight. The framing inspector walks the house before insulation, checks what they can see, and leaves. Anything installed after that walkthrough, a furred shower wall, a relocated soffit, a plumber's afterthought, never gets a second look. Guertin tells the story of furring he installed after the plumbing, electrical, and framing inspections, creating open cavities into the floor system above, and notes the inspectors would never have caught it, because they were already gone.

This is the gap the camera companies walked into. OpenSpace and its competitors strap a 360-degree camera to a hard hat, walk the job, and pin every frame to the floor plan with a timestamp. OpenSpace's ClearSight product runs computer vision over the captures, classifying objects and tracking trade progress, and ENR reports the approach is becoming standard practice on larger jobs. The pitch for fireblocking writes itself: capture the whole house the day before drywall, run the penetration checklist against the imagery, and flag every unsealed top-plate hole while the fix is still a four-dollar squeeze of caulk instead of a sawzall through finished drywall.

Obvious, and mostly theoretical. Nobody sells a fireblocking-specific AI inspector. The capture platforms will happily sell you the walkthrough; the checklist that says "this annular gap at grid C-4 is unsealed" is a workflow you build yourself, or a human reviewer with the code book open. I am not going to pretend a product exists that does not. What exists is the photographic evidence, timestamped and geolocated, which is already more documentation than fireblocking has ever had in the history of residential construction.

Now the part where I argue with myself

The honest objection is that this is gold-plating a solved problem. The framing inspector with the flashlight already exists, already walks every permitted job, and already carries the authority to red-tag missing fireblocking. Production builders, the ones buying 360 cameras, already pass these inspections at high rates. The houses with the worst fireblocking are DIY basement finishes and unpermitted remodels, exactly the jobs where nobody is strapping on a 360 camera or running AI checklists. The technology serves the compliant and misses the noncompliant, which is a strange way to fight a fire problem.

There is a second objection with sharper teeth. Smoke alarms, not caulk, are what save lives in concealed-space fires, because the killer in these fires is late discovery and the alarm is the discovery device. NFPA's data keeps showing that working smoke alarms cut the death rate roughly in half across home fires generally. If a builder has $400 to spend on life safety, the marginal dollar might do more work in interconnected alarms with ten-year batteries than in the ninth tube of red caulk. And in sprinklered homes, the calculus shifts again: where the space below is sprinklered, some fireblocking requirements ease, because the code recognizes that a wet concealed space is a different animal.

Both objections land, and they land hard: the camera does not replace the inspector, and it does not replace the alarm. What it does is narrower and still worth something. It creates a record.

When the insurance adjuster asks whether the penetrations were sealed, when the homeowner sues over a fire that rode an unsealed chase from the basement to the attic, when the builder wants to prove the crew did the work, the timestamped walkthrough answers in a way no one's memory can. Documentation is not prevention, but it is the next best thing when prevention relied on a flashlight and a Friday afternoon.

What this analysis does not prove

The 17,965 figure is my sum of NFPA's published area-of-origin categories, built from national estimates that blend NFIRS incident reports with the NFPA fire experience survey. Area-of-origin coding is a fire investigator's judgment call, and "concealed space" is likely undercounted, because a fire that destroys the evidence of where it started gets coded somewhere vaguer. Treat my total as a floor, not a ceiling.

The per-house cost model leans on a trade-forum anecdote, six to nine tubes and two to three hours, and on retail caulk prices that range from $13 to $36 a tube depending on the supplier. Penetration counts vary with the plan, labor rates vary with the market, and mineral wool for chases and lumber blocking for soffits sit outside the caulk math. Nobody has independently measured what fraction of fireblocking misses an AI-assisted photo review would catch, because the product I described does not exist yet as a product. The capture platforms' deviation-detection claims are vendor claims, cited as such.

The checklist

Before drywall, walk the house with the code's seven locations on a clipboard. Seal every top and bottom plate penetration with fire-rated caulk or packed mineral wool, and remember the annular space has to be filled all the way around the wire, not just dabbed on top. Block furred basement walls every ten feet and seal the top of the gap where it meets the floor system. Close soffits, drop ceilings, and chases at each floor level. Photograph everything, every wall, every chase, every sealed penetration, before the drywall crew arrives, because in six months that photo is the only proof the work was ever done. If you run a 360 capture platform, add the pre-drywall walkthrough to the standard operating procedure and make the penetration checklist a named review step, not a hope. And test the smoke alarms while you are at it, because the caulk slows the fire and the alarm is what wakes you up.
