Your Apartment Building Owed You a Balcony Inspection by January 1. Ask to See It.

By Catherine Chen September 6, 2026

In June 2015, a fifth-floor balcony at the Library Gardens apartment complex in Berkeley gave way during a 21st birthday party, killing six people and injuring seven. Water intrusion had been quietly rotting the wood framing that held the balcony to the building, hidden behind a waterproof membrane that looked perfectly intact from the sidewalk.

Three years later California answered with Senate Bill 721: every apartment building with three or more units must have its exterior elevated elements, balconies, decks, stairways, landings, walkways, inspected by a licensed architect, structural engineer, or qualified contractor. Elements more than six feet above the ground, supported in whole or substantial part by wood. Lawmakers set the initial inspection deadline at January 1, 2026, eight months ago.

If you rent an apartment in California, your building probably owes you a report it does not have.

Selling Into the Backlog

Into this compliance gap has walked a new kind of vendor, selling drone flights, AI photo analysis, and a balcony inspection cost calculator you can run from your phone. Firms like DrBalcony, which reports more than 2,500 completed projects, advertise drones for hard-to-reach balconies and tiered pricing: $2,000 to $4,000 for a small 3-to-10-unit building, $10,000 to $18,000 for a 50-to-100-unit complex, $18,000 and up beyond that. Coastline Equity puts typical visual inspections at $75 to $150 per unit, invasive testing at $200 to $400 per unit.

That pitch is seductive because the bottleneck is real. In the run-up to the deadline, landlord attorneys were warning clients not to wait, because the pool of licensed architects and structural engineers familiar with wood-framed buildings was getting booked months out, and every month of delay pushed owners deeper into a seller's market for a service the law requires them to buy. Demand for qualified inspectors surged, and prices followed. An AI that can pre-screen a hundred balconies from photographs sounds like exactly the pressure valve the system needs.

Here is the part the sales decks leave out. Your drone's findings mean nothing to the statute. It cares whose license is on the report.

What the numbers actually say

Roughly 6,000 people per year are injured by the structural failure or collapse of decks, porches, railings, and staircases, according to U.S. Consumer Product Safety Commission estimates. A CPSC study found nearly a quarter of a million people needed medical treatment for deck or balcony injuries over a five-year period, with more than 20,000 of those tied to partial structural failure or total collapse. According to the International Association of Certified Home Inspectors, 60 percent of America's 45 million decks have safety issues, and 90 percent of deck collapses trace to a single point: the ledger board, the connection where the deck meets the building. NADRA puts the count of North American decks that have outlived their practical service life at roughly 30 million.

Read that again. Ninety percent. The ledger, every time. Nine out of ten collapses start at the ledger. And the ledger is the one component a photograph is worst at evaluating.

Pennsylvania engineer Johann Szautner, who investigates deck failures professionally, keeps finding the same concealed defects: ledger boards attached with a single row of 10d nails instead of lag screws or through-bolts, missing or incorrect flashing at the deck-to-house connection, long-term moisture penetration rotting the structural members underneath, defects that owners never see because they hide by design behind siding and flashing and paint, which is precisely why the Berkeley balcony passed every casual visual check right up until the night it killed six people.

What the AI can do, and what it cannot

The computer vision research is genuinely impressive, and it deserves a fair hearing. A 2024 paper from researchers at Nuremberg Tech trained segmentation models on 1,424 annotated images of wood crosscuts and achieved an average intersection-over-union of 0.71 for detecting and localizing rot, approaching agreement levels of human annotators. A separate system, Woodot, uses a fine-tuned vision model to segment defects on glulam beams for robotic repair, reaching F1 scores near 0.69.

Neither system has ever inspected a balcony: one was trained on log ends in an industrial setting, while Woodot works on factory-fresh engineered beams instead. No published study validates rot segmentation on weathered, in-situ balcony framing photographed in real daylight, and nobody in the research literature claims otherwise. Between "AI can find rot in a controlled image of a log" and "AI can certify the structural connection holding up your neighbor's balcony" lies not a small engineering detail but the entire problem.

Drones, to be fair, solve a genuine access problem. High-rise balconies and restricted courtyards that once required scaffolding can now be photographed in an afternoon, which compresses a week of access logistics into a single site visit and genuinely lowers the cost of the visual portion of an inspection. That is a real efficiency gain in the visual portion of the work, which is unfortunately also the portion least correlated with preventing the next collapse.

Math the Vendors Skip

This is where a careful reading of the law gets interesting, because the statute itself constrains what AI triage can economically accomplish. SB 326, the condo counterpart to SB 721, requires inspectors to examine a "statistically significant random sample" of exterior elevated elements. In practice the industry interprets this as 95 percent confidence with a 5 percent margin of error.

Run the finite-population sample formula with those parameters (Z = 1.96, p = 0.5, e = 0.05). A complex with 100 balconies requires about 80 of them to be inspected hands-on, and a 30-balcony building requires about 28. On a mid-size complex, the sampling rule means nearly the entire inventory gets inspected anyway. No AI, however accurate, reduces that count, because the count comes from the statute rather than from the inspector's discretion, which confines the technology's economic leverage entirely to deciding how the mandated sample gets examined, not whether it gets examined at all.

So where does AI triage actually save money? Not in the sample size, but in the invasive subset.

Visual inspection runs $75 to $150 per unit. Invasive testing, probing wood, borescopes, moisture meters at the ledger, runs $200 to $400. If photo triage can responsibly narrow the invasive subset from, say, 80 elements to 20 on a 100-unit complex, the owner saves roughly 60 units times the midpoint premium (about $300 invasive versus $113 visual, a $187 gap), or about $11,250, which is nearly the entire inspection fee. It is also the only defensible ROI claim in this market. Any vendor promising that AI "replaces" the inspection is selling something the statute does not permit, and any owner who buys that pitch is purchasing a false sense of compliance.

Moral Hazard

Here is the strongest case against the AI-inspection boom, stated at full strength. A cheap drone flyover that produces a glossy AI-generated report creates exactly the wrong incentive: the owner feels inspected, the tenants feel reassured, and the ledger keeps rotting. Doing nothing at least does not manufacture confidence, while a glossy AI report actively manufactures the one thing an uninspected balcony must never possess: the appearance of safety. A false clean bill of health is worse than an acknowledged backlog, because the backlog is at least honest about the risk, whereas the report converts unknown danger into certified safety on the strength of photographs of the wrong components.

That argument from the inspection industry is unglamorous and correct. Nothing substitutes for a licensed professional putting a probe into wood at the connection. SB 721 agrees: the report must come from a licensed architect, structural engineer, or qualified contractor, and SB 326 restricts sign-off to licensed structural engineers or architects only. An algorithm can prioritize, but it cannot certify.

Dry rot does not care about your feelings, and neither does the code.

What to do with all of this

If you rent in a California apartment building with balconies, ask your landlord or property manager for the SB 721 inspection report. Eight months past the deadline, "we haven't gotten around to it" is not a scheduling anecdote. It is a disclosure fact, and it belongs in your lease-renewal calculus.

If you own a small rental building, budget $2,000 to $4,000 for the inspection itself, keep 10 to 20 percent in contingency, and understand that the report is only the down payment, because findings trigger a 120-day repair clock and the repair scope on pre-2000 wood construction is where the real money goes. Reports must be retained for at least two full inspection cycles, twelve years minimum, so whatever the inspector finds becomes a permanent part of the building's record and a future buyer's due-diligence reading list.

If a vendor pitches you AI-assisted inspection, demand three things in writing: the name and license number of the architect or engineer signing the report, the written criteria determining which elements receive invasive testing rather than photographs, and moisture-meter or probe readings at every ledger in the sample. If the proposal cannot produce the third item, you are not buying an inspection at all but a photo album with a letterhead, and your tenants are the ones holding the risk.

And if you own a single-family home with a deck, note that none of this law applies to you and no inspector is coming. Your deck is subject to the same 90-percent ledger rule as an apartment balcony, which means the annual check is yours to perform or yours to hire: the ledger connection, the flashing above it, the fasteners holding it on, which should be lag screws or through-bolts rather than nails. If you find nails, you have found the beginning of a story that ends in a news article.

Limitations

The CPSC injury figures are estimates extrapolated from emergency-room sampling, not a census of collapses, and the "90 percent ledger" figure is an industry estimate from InterNACHI, not a peer-reviewed finding. Cited wood-rot vision models were trained on log crosscuts and glulam beams, not on in-situ balcony framing, and no published study validates their performance in the field, which means every accuracy figure quoted in a sales deck describes a different task than the one your building needs performed. Inspection cost ranges come from vendor-published pricing and vary by region, access, and building condition; repair costs after findings are the dominant budget risk and are entirely building-specific. For the sampling calculation I used the conventional 95-percent-confidence, 5-percent-margin interpretation of "statistically significant," but the statute does not fix those parameters and local enforcement may differ. SB 721 covers rental buildings of three or more units; single-family homes and duplexes are excluded, and condominiums fall under SB 326 with its own deadlines and sign-off rules.
