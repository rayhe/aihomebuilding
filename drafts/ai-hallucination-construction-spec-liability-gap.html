Your Builder Used AI to Write the Material Spec. The Product It Specified Doesn't Exist.

AI is generating construction documents for residential projects right now. Standard contracts don't mention it. Courts say the builder is still liable. And liability insurers are quietly writing exclusions for AI-generated work.

By Catherine Chen

Consider a plausible scenario in 2026 residential construction: a general contractor asks ChatGPT to draft a material specification for a kitchen renovation, and the output calls for "CertainTeed GlasRoc FireGuard Type X, 5/8-inch, for range hood duct enclosure per manufacturer installation guide Rev. 2024-03." Precise language, professional formatting, and a product that does not exist. GlasRoc is a real CertainTeed line, but there is no "FireGuard" variant, no Rev. 2024-03 installation guide, and the model number is a hallucination assembled from fragments of real product data.

What happens next depends on the subcontractor's experience. An experienced drywall sub who knows the GlasRoc line would call the GC for clarification. But a less experienced sub under schedule pressure might substitute whatever Type X board is available without checking whether it matches a specification that was never real. Both outcomes happen daily with human-written specs containing typos or outdated model numbers, but AI-generated specifications differ in volume and confidence: a language model produces fabricated product references at scale, formatted so professionally that they pass the visual credibility test that catches most human errors.

Already in the Pipeline

Construction professionals are not waiting for permission to use generative AI on project documents. ConsensusDocs, the construction industry's leading contract standards body, warned in a 2025 risk advisory that "generative AI tools like ChatGPT are now being used to draft key project documents like scopes of work, RFIs, meeting notes, and change order narratives," and that these tools "will present incorrect information with confidence, making errors difficult to catch." Multi-Housing News confirmed in November 2025 that contractors are using AI "to create proposals, estimates, and blueprints" while engineers generate "renderings, specifications, designs, and plans."

John Priestland, chair of GIRI's technology working group (the Get It Right Initiative, a UK construction quality body), was blunter: "In construction, we cannot afford hallucinations." His organization recommended four safeguards: risk assessments before applying AI to any task, transparent chain-of-thought reasoning, human-in-the-loop accountability, and what GIRI calls "assurative AI," meaning knowledge graphs and automated checks that verify AI-generated content before it reaches the build. When asked how many firms follow those safeguards, Priestland compared the industry's posture to St. Augustine's prayer: "Make me good, but not yet."

The Strongest Case Against Panic

Construction documents have always contained errors, and the industry has developed layered defenses against them: plan review by building departments, professional engineer stamps on structural work, manufacturer technical support lines, field inspection by code officials, and the supply chain itself, where distributors flag nonexistent product numbers before material ships. A GIRI study estimated that errors and rework driven by incorrect information cost the UK construction industry 21% of project value, and much of that cost comes from human-generated documentation mistakes. AI-generated specs are faster and sometimes more complete than what a harried GC produces from memory at 9 PM before a permit submission, and if the per-document error rate is comparable, AI might be a net improvement simply because it produces more consistently formatted output.

But AI hallucinations are structurally different from human typos because they are internally consistent and confidently formatted, making them harder to catch by visual pattern recognition. A human typo in a model number usually looks wrong; an AI-fabricated model number looks right. A distributor will still catch a nonexistent product at the point of order, but the error can survive longer in the documentation pipeline, consuming project management time and creating liability exposure in the gap between document approval and material procurement.

Standard Contracts Say Nothing About AI

HCR Law examined the most widely used construction contract forms in a multi-jurisdictional analysis published in May 2026, including JCT in the UK, NEC4, and by extension AIA's A201 in the United States. Every one of them, the firm found, "was drafted on the assumption that project decisions are made by human professionals exercising judgment," and none of them "address the use of AI systems or allocate risk where project outcomes are influenced by algorithmic tools."

Liability could scatter across the contractor who deployed the AI, the data provider, the AI vendor, or whoever reviewed and approved the output, and "standard forms provide no guidance on liability allocation in these circumstances," HCR Law concluded, "increasing the likelihood of dispute." For residential construction, this gap is wider because a homeowner signing a construction agreement has almost certainly never been told that portions of the project documentation were machine-generated.

Meanwhile, liability insurers have started writing explicit exclusions for AI-generated professional work, with standardized endorsement language now available for carriers to exclude AI-assisted outputs from professional liability coverage. A builder who uses AI to draft specs may not realize that doing so has voided the coverage the homeowner is counting on.

Courts Set a Standard Before Contracts Caught Up

What courts have consistently held is that using AI does not lower the professional standard of care. Suffolk University's Journal of High Technology Law, analyzing emerging caselaw in a 2025 article titled "Prepare Your Forms for Battle," found that courts "currently assign liability to the contractor or design professional exercising a duty of care, unless a contract shifts that risk elsewhere. Using AI does not lower that standard. Courts expect contractors to evaluate their tools before relying on them. Thus, if AI generates a faulty plan, the contractor likely remains liable because they chose to rely on the tool."

Legal professionals learned this first, though the parallel is imperfect: attorneys check citations against case databases, while contractors verify products against physical catalogs and supplier relationships, giving them a practical advantage in catching fabrications. Even so, documented cases of AI-fabricated legal citations have passed 1,400 in the Charlotin tracking database, and in April 2026 Sullivan & Cromwell publicly apologized to a federal judge after submitting fabricated citations generated by AI. The pattern across fields is consistent: professionals overestimate their ability to catch AI-generated errors in high-volume document workflows.

What You Can Do

If you are building a home or doing a major renovation, verify that specified products exist by checking manufacturer websites before signing off on material selections, and ask your builder what process they use to verify that specifications match current manufacturer catalogs and code editions.

For builders: every word in your project documents is your liability regardless of what generated it. If ChatGPT fabricates a product model number and your sub installs whatever they think is equivalent, that rework and legal exposure belong to you because the court standard does not care that an AI made the mistake. GIRI's four safeguards are not regulatory requirements today, but they represent the closest thing to a defensible standard of care for AI-assisted construction documentation that currently exists, and the first lawsuit will make them feel a lot less optional.

What We Could Not Verify

No dataset quantifies how many residential construction documents are AI-generated, and no court has ruled on a case where an AI-hallucinated spec caused a physical defect. GIRI's safeguards are recommendations, not mandates, and the existing inspection and plan review infrastructure catches many errors regardless of origin. But the documents are already being written, the legal and insurance infrastructure has not adapted, and the first case that connects a hallucinated spec to a physical defect will set a precedent that every builder will need to understand.
