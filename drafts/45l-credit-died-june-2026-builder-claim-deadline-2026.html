The $5,000 Federal Credit for Efficient New Homes Died 76 Days Ago. Your Builder Can Still Collect It.

By Catherine "Code" Chen, September 14, 2026, Policy & Regulation

On June 30, 2026, a federal tax credit worth up to $5,000 per new home quietly expired. No press conference, no countdown clock on any builder's website, just a single sentence in the tax code doing what tax code sentences do.

Section 45L was its name: the New Energy Efficient Home Credit. For homes acquired between 2023 and mid-2026, it paid the builder $2,500 for every house certified to the ENERGY STAR program and $5,000 for every house certified to the Department of Energy's Zero Energy Ready Home standard. Per the Department of Energy's own summary, the window was acquired after December 31, 2022, and before July 1, 2026, per the One Big Beautiful Bill Act of 2025, which yanked the termination date forward from 2032. Read the statute yourself, because 26 USC 45L(h) is eleven words long: "This section shall not apply to any qualified new energy efficient home acquired after June 30, 2026."

That is the whole law.

Here is the part that matters to you: the credit went to the builder, not the buyer, and it always did.

The IRS page for the credit puts it bluntly: "Eligible contractors who build or substantially reconstruct qualified new energy-efficient homes may be able to claim tax credits up to $5,000 per home." You, the homeowner, got the lower utility bills, while the builder got the check from the Treasury.

What the money actually was

The IRA-era version of 45L had a clean structure, and it is worth understanding even now, because the same mechanics govern what builders can still claim for pre-deadline closings.

For single-family homes: $2,500 per dwelling unit meeting ENERGY STAR requirements, $5,000 per unit meeting DOE Zero Energy Ready Home requirements. For multifamily, the base amounts were smaller, $500 and $1,000 per unit, but jumped to $2,500 and $5,000 if the builder met prevailing-wage requirements, meaning every laborer and mechanic on the project was paid at least the Department of Labor's local prevailing rate. That wage wrinkle is in 45L(g), and it turned the credit into a two-variable optimization: energy certification times labor compliance.

Claiming it was never automatic: the home had to be certified by an independent rater before or during construction, with blower-door tests, duct leakage tests, and checklist inspections that cannot be backfilled after drywall. Builders claim the credit on Form 8908 for the tax year in which the home was "acquired," which in practice means the year the buyer took title. Miss the certification during construction and the credit is gone forever, because there is no retroactive HERS rating for a finished house.

And "acquired" is doing real work in that termination sentence. A home whose construction finished in May but whose escrow closed July 2 gets nothing. What matters is the acquisition date, not the completion date.

Title, not framing.

Somewhere in America right now there is a builder staring at a closing statement dated July 1 and doing subtraction nobody warned them about.

Running the numbers

The interesting question was never the credit amount. It was the spread: what did certification cost versus what the credit paid? Because if the spread was wide, builders left money on the table for years. If it was narrow, the credit was mostly theater.

Start with certification cost, where the numbers are refreshingly concrete. A basic HERS rating averages about $450 nationally, per RESNET training materials, and a rater variability study put rating-only fees between $350 and $573. Full ENERGY STAR certification costs more, because it adds checklist inspections during construction and program registration; in most markets it lands between $800 and $1,500 per home, so take the midpoint of $1,150 and run with it.

Against a $2,500 ENERGY STAR credit, that leaves roughly $1,350 per home before counting any incremental construction cost. EPA's own cost analysis of the ENERGY STAR checklists is surprisingly friendly here: its Version 3.1 cost and savings summary estimates the HVAC checklist requirements at a net cost of negative $416 to negative $698 depending on climate zone, meaning the required measures pay for themselves in equipment sizing and efficiency. Envelope and efficiency upgrades beyond code vary by market, but for a builder already constructing a tight, well-insulated house, the marginal cost of hitting ENERGY STAR was often a few thousand dollars at most.

Scale it to a 10-home subdivision and the picture sharpens: ten credits at $2,500 total $25,000, ten certifications at roughly $1,150 total $11,500, leaving about $13,500 net before energy savings, before the marketing value of the blue ENERGY STAR label, and before the utility rebates that stacked on top in many territories, which is not a rounding error. That is a truck.

Where the math gets honest is the $5,000 ZERH tier. Zero Energy Ready demands a HERS score around 40 to 55, roughly twice as efficient as code in most climate zones, and the incremental construction cost to get there, better envelope, heat pump water heating, careful air sealing, runs into the low five figures in cold climates, with certification adding another $1,200 to $2,000. It covered a meaningful fraction but rarely the whole premium. Builders chasing the $5,000 were betting that energy savings and buyer premiums would close the gap, and sometimes the bet paid off, though sometimes the house just cost more.

Why some people wanted it dead

Not everyone mourned this credit, and the skeptics deserve their full hearing. Start with the split incentive: the credit rewarded the builder while the energy savings flowed to the buyer, so in competitive markets builders pocketed the credit without cutting prices, and buyers never knew it existed. A subsidy nobody can see is a subsidy nobody competes on, and in markets where every builder certified, the credit became table stakes that buyers paid for without knowing, which is the worst of both worlds: a subsidy that raises prices instead of lowering them.

Then came the compliance burden: certification meant scheduling a rater for multiple site visits during construction, managing checklist paperwork, and risking a failed verification after drywall, when fixes are expensive. For small custom builders doing a handful of homes a year, the administrative overhead could eat half the credit's value. Multifamily developers faced the prevailing-wage documentation regime, which is its own circle of paperwork, and several told industry groups the wage bonus was not worth the audit exposure.

Sharpest of all is the geographic criticism. In California, Title 24 already requires efficiency levels that meet or exceed ENERGY STAR in many measures, which meant the credit was paying Golden State builders for compliance, not for going beyond code. A federal incentive that functions as a windfall in strict-code states and an actual incentive in lax-code states is, at best, poorly targeted. Killing it arguably just stopped paying California builders for obeying California law, while in states still building to the 2009 energy code the same dollars genuinely moved the market, a targeting failure baked into any national efficiency incentive that keys off federal labels instead of local baselines.

A fair point.

All of that is fair, so now weigh it against the alternative, which arrived on schedule: nothing. Meanwhile, the same 2025 legislation that killed 45L also terminated the residential clean energy credit and the energy-efficient home improvement credit for expenditures after December 31, 2025, per the IRS instructions for Form 5695. As of today, there is no federal tax incentive for building an efficient new home, full stop. Whatever you think of 45L's design, its replacement is a blank page.

What you can still do

If you bought a new home that closed before July 1, 2026, ask your builder two questions: was the home third-party certified, and can you see the certificate, and did they claim 45L on it? Those answers tell you whether up to $5,000 of federal money rode along with your purchase, and whether any of it showed up in your price, your upgrades, or your warranty terms, and if the answer to the second question is yes while the answer to the first is no, you have a builder who took federal money for a house they cannot prove was efficient, which is a different conversation entirely. Builders with pre-deadline closings claim the credit on their 2026 returns, filed next year, so the paperwork window is open right now. If your builder certified and never mentioned it, that is worth a conversation.

If you are building now, the federal math is simple.

Zero.

Run the ENERGY STAR decision on energy savings and utility rebates alone. In high-rate territories like California and New England, the bill savings still carry certification comfortably. In cheap-power states, it is a comfort and resale story, not a payback story. Either way, certify during construction or not at all, because no rater can inspect insulation through drywall.

And if you are a builder sitting on pre-deadline closings with certified homes, gather your rater documentation now. The form is 8908, the tax year is 2026, the credit died 76 days ago, and the claims have not.

File accordingly.

What I could not prove

My certification cost figures are ranges, not quotes. Rater fees vary sharply by market, rural areas pay travel premiums, hot markets pay scarcity premiums, and no national survey of 2026 ENERGY STAR certification fees exists, which means every figure in this article is stitched from rater price sheets, a federal training deck, and one very patient variability study, all of which I have linked so you can check my arithmetic. Treat $800 to $1,500 as a working range and get a local quote before repeating my $13,500 subdivision figure at a planning meeting. IRS has not published claim statistics for the 2023-2026 45L regime, so I cannot tell you how many builders actually claimed it or what share of eligible homes went uncertified; the uptake story is anecdotal. State and utility incentives that might replace 45L vary by jurisdiction and change yearly; the DSIRE database is the right reference, not this article. Finally, nothing here is tax advice: the DOE prints that warning on its own 45L page, and I repeat it here, which is to say talk to a tax professional before claiming anything.
