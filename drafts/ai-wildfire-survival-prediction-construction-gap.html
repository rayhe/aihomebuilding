An AI Predicts With 82% Accuracy Whether Your Home Will Survive a Wildfire. Your Builder Didn't Ask.

By Priya Greenwood · July 28, 2026

In January 2025, the Palisades and Eaton fires destroyed more than 16,000 structures across Los Angeles County. After the smoke cleared, ZestyAI ran its Z-FIRE model against the burn perimeter and found that 94 percent of the area consumed by the Palisades fire and 87 percent of the Eaton fire zone had been rated High or Very High Risk by the model before either fire started. Every one of those homes had already been scored. It had measured their roof materials, vegetation density, structural characteristics, and separation distances from neighboring buildings, and it had concluded, property by property, that most of them were vulnerable. Nobody who built those homes had checked.

That is the state of wildfire prediction in residential construction in 2026: the AI exists, it works, and nobody on the building side is using it.

What the Model Sees

A team at UC Berkeley led by Michael Gollner and Maryam Zamanialaei built a machine learning model trained on CAL FIRE's damage inspection database, which contains on-the-ground surveys of every structure damaged or destroyed in major California wildfires since 2013. They combined that data with geospatial sources that defined structure spacing, construction materials, and vegetation density around each building. The resulting model predicts whether a home will survive a wildfire with 82 percent accuracy.

Structure separation distance emerged as the most influential factor. In densely built areas, the gap between one house and the next determines whether fire jumps from building to building through radiant heat. Flame length and construction features also contributed substantially. Clearing vegetation within a five-foot perimeter of a home reduced structure losses by 17 percent. When home hardening and defensible space strategies were combined, the number of homes surviving a fire doubled.

Separately, a joint study by the Insurance Institute for Business and Home Safety and ZestyAI found that mitigating fuel density risk alone nearly doubles a property's wildfire survival rate. Z-FIRE, the commercial model that powers much of the insurance industry's wildfire underwriting, has been trained on more than 1,200 wildfire events across several decades and evaluates property-level factors that legacy risk models ignore entirely: defensible space maintenance, building material composition, and roof pitch.

Who Uses It and Who Doesn't

Insurance companies use these models. The California FAIR Plan, the state's insurer of last resort, adopted Z-FIRE after concluding that its previous tools were inadequate. "We can no longer rely on 30-year-old technology to understand modern climate phenomena," said Anneliese Jivan, president of the FAIR Plan. More than 20 states have now accepted AI-driven wildfire and hail models in insurance rate filings, meaning the risk scores these models produce directly determine what homeowners pay for coverage. In many markets, they determine whether coverage is available at all.

Homebuilders do not use these models. Not a single production builder in the United States markets homes to an AI wildfire survival score. Not a single subdivision in a Wildland-Urban Interface zone advertises the Z-FIRE rating of its lots. Construction decisions that AI models identify as critical to survival, things like noncombustible eave soffits, tempered glass, Class A roofing assemblies, and ember-resistant vents, are treated as optional upgrades rather than as load-bearing inputs to a predictive survival calculation that insurers are already running on the finished product.

The insurer knows what your builder should have built. Your builder never asked.

The Cost of Closing the Gap

An IBHS study published after the LA fires provided detailed construction cost estimates for wildfire-resistant materials meeting three standards: California's Building Code Chapter 7A, the IBHS Wildfire Prepared Home standard, and the enhanced Wildfire Prepared Home Plus designation, which protects against direct flame contact and radiant heat in addition to embers. The study prices out the difference across roofing, eaves, siding, windows, doors, decking, and the critical noncombustible zone within five feet of the structure.

For new construction, IBHS data on the related FORTIFIED program shows that building to the Gold standard, the highest certification level requiring an engineered continuous load path from roof to foundation, adds 1 to 3 percent to total construction cost. On a $400,000 home, that is $4,000 to $12,000. For a re-roofing project on a 2,000-square-foot home, the cost increase for FORTIFIED Roof compliance runs $1,000 to $3,000, depending on local material costs and existing conditions.

Returns on that investment are not hypothetical. IBHS data from independent post-hurricane studies shows that FORTIFIED-designated homes are 35 percent less likely to file an insurance claim and suffer 22 percent less damage than traditionally built homes. In Louisiana, Act 533 legally mandates insurance premium discounts for FORTIFIED-designated homes, with homeowners reporting reductions of 20 to 52 percent on the wind and hail portion of their premiums. Louisiana's Fortify Homes Program provides up to $10,000 in grants for FORTIFIED roof upgrades, entirely exempt from state income tax and paid directly to the contractor.

A Builder's Math Problem Nobody Is Solving

Consider a home in a California WUI zone where annual wildfire insurance runs $8,000 to $15,000 and rising, if coverage is available at all. A builder adds $8,000 in fire-resistant construction upgrades, bringing the home from code-minimum Chapter 7A compliance to a level that AI models would score as significantly lower risk. Even a 25 percent premium reduction, at the conservative end of what FORTIFIED data suggests, saves the homeowner $2,000 to $3,750 per year. The construction premium pays for itself in two to four years. Over a 30-year mortgage, the cumulative savings dwarf the upfront cost by a factor of eight to fourteen.

But that math only captures the insurance line. The deeper calculation involves the probability of total loss. Berkeley's model shows that home hardening combined with defensible space doubles survival probability. If a code-minimum home inside a fire perimeter faces, conservatively, a 40 percent chance of destruction while a hardened home faces 20 percent, the expected loss on a $400,000 structure differs by $80,000. That number never appears on a builder's spec sheet, a buyer's closing documents, or a lender's appraisal.

Z-FIRE currently scores more than 1.5 million structures in California as facing high or very high wildfire risk. That is 1.5 million properties where the insurer's AI model is already evaluating construction decisions the builder made years ago, decisions that now determine premiums, eligibility, and in the worst case, whether the home is still standing twelve months from now.

Why Builders Don't Build to the Score

Builders will make three arguments against designing to an AI wildfire survival score, and each of them has real weight. First, California's Chapter 7A, which becomes Part 7 of Title 24 as the California Wildland-Urban Interface Code on January 1, 2026, already imposes some of the nation's most stringent wildfire construction requirements in designated high-risk zones, and characterizing builders who comply with it as building "to code minimum" understates what that code actually demands: ignition-resistant exterior walls, Class A roof assemblies, ember-resistant vents, and tempered or insulated glazing within 100 feet of combustible vegetation.

Second, an AI model at 82 percent accuracy means 18 percent of its predictions are wrong, and a builder cannot market or warrant that a home will survive a wildfire without creating a legal liability position that no general counsel would accept. Wildfire survival depends on variables no builder controls: wind velocity and direction during the fire, suppression response time, the maintenance habits of the neighbor who planted a Leyland cypress hedge against the property line, the vegetation management practices of the adjacent open space district. A home built to the highest resilience standard on the block can still burn if radiant heat from a collapsing structure next door exceeds the assembly's rated exposure.

Third, every dollar added to construction cost in a California WUI market where median home prices already exceed $700,000 is a dollar that makes homeownership less accessible. Adding $8,000 in fire-resistant upgrades to a home marketed to first-time buyers at the outer edge of affordability is a policy trade-off, not a free improvement.

Each argument holds. None of them erases the fact that the scoring infrastructure now exists, the insurance industry is already using it, and the gap between what a builder designs and what an insurer calculates is quantifiable, growing, and landing on the homeowner's premium statement every year.

What Is Moving

On July 23, 2026, Senator Jeff Merkley introduced the Wildfire Insurance Affordability Act, which would fund grants to states for individual homeowners to create defensible space and harden their homes, followed by vouchers for low-income homeowners who complete the work. Merkley acknowledged the limits: "It won't lower rates to what they were in the past, but it's trying to find a way to help people who might not be able to afford insurance."

In March 2026, IBHS and the American Property Casualty Insurance Association published a joint framework establishing science-based home hardening and defensible space standards, including a noncombustible zone within five feet of structures. The framework is designed to give insurers a consistent, verifiable basis for incorporating mitigation into underwriting and premium pricing, a standard that could, if widely adopted, create the feedback loop between construction decisions and insurance outcomes that does not currently exist.

What remains missing is the builder. The AI knows which construction details predict survival. Insurers use that knowledge to price risk. Legislators are beginning to fund homeowner mitigation. But the entity that actually decides which materials go into the wall assembly, which vents go into the eaves, and how far the landscaping sits from the foundation remains the one party in the chain that operates without reference to the AI score that already determines the home's insurability and, with 82 percent accuracy, its survivability.

What This Analysis Cannot Tell You

The Berkeley ML model was trained exclusively on California wildfire data. Whether its accuracy holds in the different fire regimes of Colorado, Oregon, Washington, or Montana, where fuel types, terrain, and fire behavior differ significantly from California chaparral and WUI conditions, has not been independently validated. ZestyAI's Z-FIRE performance claims, including the 94 and 87 percent correlation with the LA fires, are company-reported; no peer-reviewed academic validation of the commercial model's predictive accuracy has been published. IBHS construction cost data focuses on material costs and does not capture total installed costs, which vary significantly by market, and the FORTIFIED insurance premium reduction data (20 to 52 percent) comes from Louisiana's hurricane and severe storm program, not from wildfire-hardened homes specifically. Premium reductions for wildfire-resistant construction in California remain less well documented, in part because the state's insurance market has been in regulatory flux since the 2020 wildfire seasons. No published study directly connects AI wildfire risk scores to construction quality at the builder level. The 82 percent accuracy figure is an aggregate metric for the entire model; its precision and recall broken out by construction type, building age, and fire intensity have not been reported separately.
