Your Chimney's Fire Costs $381. Your Flue Liner's Failure Costs $77,700. The Camera Knows the Difference.

By Catherine Chen · September 11, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

On the first cold Saturday of November, a couple in Denver lit the fireplace their home listing had described as a "working fireplace." They had never burned a fire before. Neither, as far as anyone could determine, had the previous owner, and neither had anyone before them. By midnight the living room smelled like a campfire. By morning a smoke stain had bloomed on the bedroom wall upstairs, because a chimney fire had burned inside the flue overnight and a crack in the clay liner had let superheated gases into the wall cavity.

This is the usual story, except for one detail. Afterward, the sweep found glazed creosote three-eighths of an inch thick, the kind that does not brush out. Their homeowner policy covered the smoke damage. It did not cover the $18,000 chimney rebuild. Not one cent. The adjuster's letter used the word "neglect."

Two Kinds of Chimney Fire

NFPA's heating-fire tables divide chimney fires into two populations, and they might as well be two different diseases. Between 2016 and 2020, fire departments reported an average of 13,120 confined chimney or flue fires a year, and confined is the operative word: these fires stayed inside the flue, and their total direct damage was $5 million. Divide it out and you get roughly $381 per fire, which is to say a bad evening, a scary story, and a billable service call.

Then there is the other population. An average of 3,010 non-confined fireplace or chimney fires a year, the kind that escape into the structure, caused $234 million in damage and 30 civilian deaths. Same division, different universe: about $77,700 per fire. Luck has nothing to do with it. What matters is whether the fire stays in the flue, and what keeps a fire in the flue is the liner, a clay tile tube usually, sometimes stainless steel, occasionally absent entirely in older construction.

Creosote is the fuel in both populations, and it forms when wood smoke cools on its way up the flue and unburned tars condense on the walls. Stage one is soot that brushes out easily and stage two is tar flakes that a good rotary brush removes in an afternoon, which is why sweeps treat both as routine maintenance and price them that way. Stage three is glaze, hard, shiny, nearly impossible to remove without chemical treatment or a rotary chain, and it burns above 2,000 degrees Fahrenheit. Older NFPA analysis estimated 14,190 creosote fires a year, about 22 percent of all heating fires, named failure to clean the leading ignition factor in home heating fires at 26 percent, and found that two-thirds of chimney-specific fires traced back to nobody sweeping.

The Camera Sees What the Flashlight Cannot

This is where inspection levels matter, and the building codes are surprisingly specific about them. NFPA 211 states that chimneys, fireplaces, and vents shall be inspected at least once a year for soundness, freedom from deposits, and correct clearances. Cleaning, maintenance, and repairs shall be done if necessary, and the standard then defines three inspection levels, with Level 1 as a flashlight and a ladder covering readily accessible areas only. Level 2 adds a video scanner lowered the full length of the flue, and it is required on sale or transfer of a property, after a chimney fire, or when the fuel or appliance changes.

Here is the regulatory gap: your general home inspector, the one you paid $500 to crawl your prospective house, does not perform a Level 2 chimney inspection. Redfin says so plainly: standard home inspections do not cover detailed chimney checks. So the chimney gets a line item, "appears serviceable," and the video scanner, the only tool that can see a cracked flue liner, never goes down the flue. Those Denver buyers had a home inspection report, and it said the fireplace appeared serviceable, which is what inspection reports say about every chimney whose flue nobody actually looked inside.

An honest technology note is owed here, because in residential work the "AI" in chimney inspection is still mostly aspiration. Video scan cameras are standard equipment, and some sweeps annotate stills with software, while genuine computer-vision crack detection, the kind that automatically flags a hairline fracture in a clay tile from a thousand frames of flue footage, still lives in research papers and industrial drones that never visit a suburban roof. A University of Bonn team flew an autonomous drone inside industrial chimneys with 3D laser localization and textured surface reconstruction. Osaka University mounted millimeter-wave radar on a drone to measure lining thickness through the wall of a 150-meter stack, and industrial firms already sell drone inspection services today.

Your local sweep with a practiced eye and a camera on a cable is the residential state of the art, and anyone selling you "AI chimney analysis" for your house should be asked to show their math.

The Adjuster's Favorite Word

Now the part that reaches your wallet: homeowner's insurance covers sudden, accidental fire damage, but not damage from neglect. Square One's plain-language guidance states it outright: if you did not regularly have your chimney swept and a fire was caused by creosote accumulation, the resulting damages would generally not be covered. Fail to declare the fireplace on your application, and the policy itself can be voided, which is a remarkable sentence to read after you have paid premiums for a decade.

There is a documentation angle that makes this a code story rather than a safety pamphlet, and it is the reason this article sits in the policy section instead of next to a brochure about smoke alarms. A Level 2 scan produces video and annotated stills, and a written report with video evidence is what insurers and contractors accept, because an adjuster can argue with your memory of what the sweep said but cannot argue with timestamped footage of a cracked liner. Verbal findings from a Level 1 flashlight walkthrough have no legal standing in a claim dispute, which is the trade press's polite way of saying your sweep's shrug is worthless and his USB stick is everything. Keep the annual report, because it is the difference between "we swept annually" and "prove it."

What to Do With This

If you are buying, order the Level 2 video scan yourself before closing, because two hundred to five hundred dollars buys an hour of a CSIA-certified sweep's time, and that hour is the only inspection in the entire transaction that answers the question that actually matters: is the liner intact? Your home inspector's "appears serviceable" is not an answer; it is a guess from the ground.

If you own, NFPA 211 wants an annual inspection, and the Chimney Safety Institute says sweep at one-eighth inch of sooty buildup. Burn seasoned wood only, since green wood, the stuff your neighbor proudly stacked in October without ever touching a moisture meter, is creosote's raw material. Watch for the red flags that jump you straight to a Level 3 investigation, the kind that can run five figures, because smoke backing into the room, soot falling into the firebox, white efflorescence staining the exterior masonry, and rust around the damper are all the chimney's way of telling you the problem has been compounding for years.

The ROI math, since numbers are my love language, starts from the 2020-2024 top line: 37,365 heating fires a year and $1.2 billion in damage, so the average heating fire costs about $32,100, while a Level 2 scan costs roughly $350. If the scan catches one cracked liner in a hundred, which any sweep will tell you is conservative in 40-year-old housing stock, the expected-value math closes at roughly ninety to one. Insurance against tail risk is cheapest when you buy it before the tail event, which is the whole argument for the $350 scan in a single sentence.

The Case Against

The strongest argument against this thesis is arithmetic, and it deserves a full hearing. Most confined flue fires are $381 non-events that self-limit. A Level 1 inspection bundled with an annual sweep, roughly two hundred dollars, prevents the overwhelming majority of creosote ignitions, which is the actual fire risk. Mandating annual Level 2 video scans for every fireplace owner in America would cost homeowners billions to find a problem that affects a small fraction of flues, and no residential AI chimney product exists with independently verified accuracy claims. Sweeping is the vaccine, and the camera is a diagnostic, an expensive one.

I am not fully persuaded by that argument, for two reasons. First, the $381 average is a lie told by arithmetic, because averages lie most convincingly exactly when the underlying distribution has a fat tail, which this one does. Confined fires are cheap until one isn't, and the ones that aren't average $77,700. Second, the neglect clause means the cost of being wrong is not just the fire. It is the fire plus the denied claim. Both at once. But the skeptics are right about one thing: if you sweep annually and burn seasoned wood, you have handled the 90 percent of chimney risk that is ordinary, predictable, and cheap, and the camera handles the remaining 10 percent, the cracked liners and the glazed creosote in the house you just bought, that can kill you.

Limitations

This article's central calculation divides NFPA's published damage totals (2016-2020 annual averages) by fire counts from the same tables. That $77,700 figure is a mean pulled right by total-loss fires; the median non-confined loss is lower. The 2020-2024 top-line figures (37,365 fires, $1.2 billion) come from a 2026 trade press release citing NFPA, not the full NFPA report, and could not be verified against the original tables. Insurance neglect exclusions vary by carrier and state; "generally not covered" is insurer guidance, not a survey of claim outcomes. Creosote stage descriptions are industry standard but removal costs vary widely by region. I could not verify any residential AI chimney-scan product with published, independently verified accuracy figures. Where this article says "AI," it means industrial drone inspection and computer-vision research, not a product you can buy for your house.
