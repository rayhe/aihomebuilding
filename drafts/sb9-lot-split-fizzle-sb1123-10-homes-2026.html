California Let Homeowners Split Their Lots in 2022. Los Angeles Approved Zero. The Replacement Allows Ten Homes.

By Catherine Chen · September 13, 2026

In 2022, the city of Los Angeles received 28 applications from homeowners who wanted to split their lots under SB 9, California’s headline housing law, and approved zero. That same year, Danville — population 44,000, tucked into the East Bay hills — received 20 and approved 13. Same statute. Same year. Thirteen approvals in a suburb. None in a city of four million people.

SB 9 was supposed to change everything, and effective January 1, 2022, it let any California homeowner split a single-family lot in two and build up to four homes where one stood, with ministerial approval, no public hearing, and no environmental review. YIMBY groups celebrated, opponents predicted the end of the suburbs, and the Terner Center for Housing Innovation at UC Berkeley did something almost nobody else bothered to do over the next four years: it counted, city by city, application by application.

What it found should embarrass everyone who wrote a hot take in 2021. Across 13 surveyed cities, through November 2022, San Diego received 3 lot-split applications and approved none; San Francisco received 4 and approved 2; Berkeley received 1 and approved 0; San Jose received 10 and approved 5; Sacramento received 9 and approved 6. Bakersfield and Santa Maria received zero applications of any kind. California’s two largest cities produced 31 lot-split applications combined, with zero approvals.

Before passage, Terner’s modeling with MapCraft Labs had estimated 714,000 market-feasible new homes statewide, but on only 5.4 percent of California’s 7.5 million single-family lots. It also predicted something the headlines skipped: duplex conversions of existing homes would dominate, with just 10 percent of new units coming from lot splits, a prediction the first-year data confirmed. LA’s 211 two-unit development applications dwarfed its 28 lot-split filings, seven to one.

Three tripwires

Why did the lot-split track stall while the duplex track moved? Start with the owner-occupancy affidavit: split your lot and you sign a sworn statement that you will live in one of the units as your principal residence for three years. Need to sell in year two for a job, a divorce, a death in the family? Your reasons are not the statute’s concern, and Terner estimated this single requirement erased roughly 40,000 otherwise feasible units, about 6 percent of the modeled total.

Then comes the geometry: the smaller resulting lot must be at least 40 percent of the original, and each new lot must clear 1,200 square feet. On a 5,000-square-foot lot — close to the median across much of Los Angeles — a compliant split produces parcels that satisfy the arithmetic and then collide with local setback, parking, and open-space rules. Add the Casita Coalition’s exclusion list: historic districts, very-high fire severity zones, farmland, wetlands, fault zones, floodplains, hazardous waste sites, tenant-occupied properties.

Finally, the anti-assembly rules: you cannot split a lot adjacent to one you already split under SB 9, and a lot created by an SB 9 split can never be split again. That killed the small-developer playbook — buy three adjacent lots, split all three, build twelve homes — dead on arrival. SB 9 was engineered, whether intentionally or not, to permit only the smallest possible version of itself: two lots, four homes, one sworn affidavit, and enough exclusions to keep nearly every contentious parcel in California out of the program.

Ten homes, if you read the fine print

SB 1123 is the do-over. Signed in 2024 and effective July 1, 2025, it amends SB 684’s small-lot subdivision framework to require cities to ministerially approve a parcel map creating up to 10 separate fee-simple lots, with up to 10 homes and no discretionary review, no hearing, and no owner-occupancy affidavit. Read that last item twice. A 2026 practitioner guide from WIRE Associates states the trade plainly: “SB 9 is simpler, cheaper, and faster on the front end... the mom-and-pop play. SB 1123 is more complex but carries far more upside, and unlike SB 9 it has no owner-occupancy intent requirement, so investors can run multiple projects in the same jurisdiction at once.”

Now read the eligibility section of Government Code section 66499.41, because the catch lives there: the lot must be zoned for multifamily use, or be vacant and zoned single-family. Your occupied single-family home on a big lot does not qualify. So the law that fixes SB 9’s three tripwires was written for a different person — the investor holding a vacant infill lot, not the homeowner SB 9 was sold to. Sacramento County’s implementation materials list the remaining fine print: 1.5-acre maximum, substantially surrounded by urban uses, new parcels as small as 600 square feet, CEQA-exempt. Santa Cruz County adds its own layer: average unit size capped at 1,750 square feet of habitable space, minimum density thresholds, inclusionary housing requirements.

A worked example

Take a concrete case: a 7,500-square-foot R-1 lot in Sacramento with a 1,400-square-foot house. Under SB 9, you split 60/40 into 4,500 and 3,000 square feet, keep the house, and build a duplex on the new lot. Three homes total, ministerial approval, and you sign the three-year occupancy affidavit. Under SB 1123, you get nothing — the lot is occupied and single-family zoned. Four years into the experiment, the honest answer for this homeowner is SB 9 or nothing, while the ten-home subdivision belongs to the vacant lot down the street.

The feasibility question now costs nothing

A question that used to require a paid land-use attorney consultation now takes about thirty seconds. In August 2026, the proptech firm Breezy launched Underbuilt Radar, a consumer product that generates a build-potential report for any address covering buildable area, setbacks, height limits, slope analysis, and a proprietary development-potential score, while Acres.com added plain-language AI zoning search in February for the enterprise land teams that buy parcels by the hundred. These tools do not change the underlying math, since Terner already did that math years ago and most lots fail it, but they move the “is my lot even eligible” question from a paid consultation to an instant report. For SB 1123’s investor audience, that speed is the product, and the playbook firms are already running it.

In defense of SB 9

Low numbers do not prove SB 9 failed; they may prove it worked as designed. Terner’s model predicted duplex conversions would dominate lot splits, and they did, seven to one in Los Angeles. Worse for the lot-split boosters: the ministerial two-unit path may have cannibalized the lot-split path, since nobody pays for a survey, a parcel map, and a three-year affidavit when two units require none of it. Then there is the calendar. 2022 was year one, and ADU reform took the better part of a decade to hit its stride. Judging a land-use law by eleven months of applications is like judging a harvest at planting time. Here is the uncomfortable possibility for the law’s critics: the lot-split provision was never the point; the duplex provision was, and it was the headline that got the bill signed.

What this analysis does not prove

No statewide SB 9 application survey newer than Terner’s November 2022 data has been published, which means every claim about the law’s performance since then rests on anecdotes, city staff reports, and practitioner lore rather than systematic measurement. Figures for 2023 through 2026 are anecdotal, drawn from city staff reports and practitioner accounts rather than any statewide survey. SB 1123 has been effective for barely a year, and no jurisdiction has published application counts under the amended law, so the ten-home promise remains a theory with a statute attached rather than a track record anyone can audit. Terner’s 714,000-unit figure is modeled feasibility, not a forecast; it assumes financing exists, and SB 9 construction lending products remain rare. That Sacramento example uses typical R-1 standards, and your city’s objective standards, setbacks, and floor-area rules can erase the modeled outcome entirely, which is why no article, however carefully researched, substitutes for an hour at your city’s planning counter. None of the AI feasibility tools cited here has been independently verified for SB 9 or SB 1123 accuracy.

What to do Monday morning

If you own a California single-family lot of 4,800 square feet or more, start with your city’s SB 9 ordinance. Many cities passed urgency ordinances adding local rules, and those rules control. Run an AI feasibility report before hiring anyone; it costs far less than starting with counsel. If your lot is vacant, or multifamily-zoned, price out the SB 1123 path with a civil engineer who has actually filed one — the ten-parcel map is a different animal from a two-lot split, and the first filers are writing the playbook in real time. Budget honestly: builders routinely report duplex conversions running several hundred thousand dollars and up to 24 months, with possible relocation during construction. And if tenants have occupied the property in the last three years, SB 9’s demolition limits probably end the conversation before it starts. Remember: the code does not care about your reasons. It never does.
