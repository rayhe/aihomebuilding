Your Pipes Lost a $950 Million Lawsuit in 1995. You're Still Drinking Through Them.

By Frank DeLuca, September 15, 2026, Project Management & Operations

At 2:14 on a Tuesday morning, a hot-water supply line lets go behind a washing machine in a 1989 ranch house. Nobody hears it for forty minutes. By the time the homeowner's foot finds the hallway carpet, the water has been running through the wall cavity, down through the subfloor, and into the crawlspace at full municipal pressure. The pipe that failed is dull gray plastic with a stamp on it that reads PB2110. It was installed when the house was built, thirty-seven years ago, and it was already a defendant in a class-action lawsuit before the current owner finished high school.

Polybutylene was the pipe of the future. Builders loved it because it bent around corners without fittings and cost a fraction of copper. Between 1978 and 1995 it went into an estimated six to ten million American homes, as many as one in four houses built during those years. Then the failures started, and they kept starting, until a Tennessee judge approved a $950 million settlement in November 1995, with Shell Oil and Hoechst Celanese funding the repairs. Manufacturing for potable water effectively stopped by mid-1996.

Here is the part that should bother you. That settlement closed decades ago, and those pipes never left.

What chlorine does to the pipe

Polybutylene does not fail the way copper fails. Copper gives you pinholes and green stains and a decade of warning. Polybutylene fails from the inside out, invisibly, because chlorine and chloramines in treated municipal water oxidize the pipe walls. Manufacturers added stabilizing compounds to absorb the chemical attack. Those stabilizers exhaust themselves over the years. Once they are gone, the chlorine goes to work on the wall directly, and the pipe gets brittle and cracks with no external symptom at all.

That inside-out mechanism is why patching polybutylene is theater. You can cut out the burst section and splice in a coupling. Every remaining foot of that run is the same age, drank the same chlorinated water, and is degrading on the same schedule. One repair does not change the risk profile of the system by a single percentage point. I have watched homeowners pay for the same emergency twice in eighteen months and call the second one bad luck. It was not luck. It was chemistry with a schedule.

Your insurer figured this out before you did

Insurance is built to cover the unpredictable. Polybutylene is the opposite of unpredictable: a documented, litigated, nine-figure-settlement failure mode. So carriers treat it as a known defect. Most standard policies will not pay to replace the pipes, and many carriers restrict coverage or decline to write the policy at all. If a line bursts suddenly, the resulting water damage to your floors and drywall may be covered. Replacing the pipe itself is your problem, every time.

Read the fine print on the renewal and you may find worse: a polybutylene endorsement that voids water-damage coverage for any claim originating from those pipes. Some carriers offer conditional coverage through surplus-lines markets, with higher deductibles or hard caps on payouts. And nondisclosure is a trap with teeth. Fail to disclose the piping material on the application, and the carrier can cancel the policy retroactively, even after a claim is filed. You pay premiums for years and discover at the worst possible moment that you were never actually insured.

The newest development is that the underwriting no longer waits for your application to tell it what it already suspects. Insurers now run computer-vision models over aerial imagery, permit histories, and parcel records to score property risk before a human ever looks at the file. ZestyAI's platform cross-validates two decades of imagery against building permits to verify roof age and condition across roughly 97 percent of U.S. properties, and carriers like Augusta Mutual and Southern Oak have adopted it to target inspections and price risk at the parcel level. The models are documented for roofs today. That same machinery scores every visible defect class, and a 1987 build date on the parcel record is all the model needs to infer what is probably inside your walls. Assume the algorithm knows your pipe material before your agent does.

The patch trap, with numbers

Now the project math, because this is a project whether you manage it or not. A whole-house PEX repipe runs about $4.50 per square foot nationally: roughly $6,750 for a 1,500-square-foot house, $9,000 for 2,000 square feet. PEX-specific bids cluster between $4,500 and $9,000 for standard-access homes, and $7,500 to $14,600 for slab foundations where crews reroute through the attic. Angi's national figures put most homeowners at about $7,500 all-in. Copper roughly doubles it.

The four-day critical path

A repipe is one of the most civilized major projects in residential construction, which is not the same as saying it is pleasant. Here is how a competent crew sequences it, and what has to be true for the schedule to hold.

Day 1: Demolition and main runs. Crew maps the system, shuts the main, and opens walls at planned access points. New PEX-A home runs go in first, usually routed through the attic or crawlspace, because PEX snakes through framing without the joints copper needs at every turn. Homeowner job: clear everything from under every sink, pull storage out of closets on plumbing walls, confirm attic access. Water is off roughly 8 to 5 and restored each evening. You sleep at home.

Day 2: Branch lines and fixtures. Every fixture gets tied in: toilets, sinks, dishwasher, washing machine, water heater. This is the day the crew discovers what the last remodel hid. Budget a half-day of float here mentally even if the bid does not show it. If your water heater is over ten years old, replace it now. The access work is already done, and a separate water-heater swap later costs triple for the same plumber to open the same wall.

Day 3: Pressure test and inspection. Crews pressurize the system and hold it while inspectors sign off on the permit pulled on day zero. Any jurisdiction worth living in requires one, and a contractor who suggests skipping the permit is telling you exactly how the rest of the job will go. Failed pressure test means a fitting gets redone before walls close. That is the system working.

Day 4: Patch and texture. Drywall goes back, texture gets matched. Understand what the bid includes here: most repipe bids cover patching and texture matching, not repainting. Paint is on you. Get that in writing before day one, not discovered on day four.

Four days, one permit, water every evening. Compare that against the unmanaged version: a 2 a.m. flood, a week of dehumidifiers, a fight with the adjuster, and then the same four-day project anyway, except now it starts with demolition you did not plan.

The buyer's fifteen-minute pipe check

If you are buying a house built between 1978 and 1995, spend fifteen minutes during the inspection contingency looking at pipes instead of paint colors. Open the water-heater closet first: the main lines are usually visible there. Look under kitchen and bathroom sinks. If there is a crawlspace access, point a flashlight at the supply runs.

You are looking for dull gray flexible plastic, sometimes blue or black on exterior runs, occasionally stamped PB2110 or ASTM D3309 along its length. Gray and flexible with that stamp is polybutylene. Red and blue flexible plastic is PEX, the modern replacement, and that is fine. Rigid white is usually PVC or CPVC. Copper needs no introduction.

If you find it, the inspection contingency is your leverage. Price a repipe credit at $4.50 a square foot of house, adjusted upward for slab foundations, and get a licensed plumber's written bid during the contingency window, not a guess. Sellers in Sun Belt markets have seen this movie before. And call your insurance agent before you close, not after: find out whether your carrier will write the house with polybutylene still in the walls, and at what price, while you can still walk away.

What this analysis does not prove

The $4.50-per-square-foot figure is a national average, and repipes are stubbornly local. Sun Belt slab homes run higher because of the attic reroute. Union labor markets run higher across the board. Your bid is the only number that matters, and it should be in writing from a licensed plumber who walked the house.

The patch-cost scenario uses published slab-leak and emergency repair ranges of $2,000 to $4,000 per event. A simple accessible pinhole patch costs less. That point stands at any price point above trivial: patch spending does not reduce systemic risk.

No public dataset tracks how many polybutylene systems fail per year right now, so the "end of useful life" characterization comes from industry sources, not a measured failure rate. Insurance treatment varies by carrier and state; some carriers still write these homes with endorsements and surcharges rather than declining them.

Finally, the claim that underwriting AI already infers your pipe material is partly inference. Carriers publicly document computer-vision underwriting for roofs, vegetation, and parcel condition. They do not publish the full feature lists of their risk models. But a model that knows your build year, your permit history, and your roof condition does not need to see inside your walls to price the probability correctly.

The case for doing nothing

Here is the strongest argument against everything above, stated fairly. Most polybutylene that was going to fail dramatically already did, back in the nineties and two-thousands. The systems still running have survived thirty-plus years of chlorinated water. A homeowner could reasonably conclude that a pipe with a three-decade track record in their own house is a known quantity, and that $9,000 today is a lot to pay against a small annual chance of a leak. That is not an irrational position. It is a gamble with a known downside and a known price.

But notice what the gamble actually is. You are not betting that the pipe lasts forever. You are betting that when it fails, the failure is small, discovered fast, and covered. The chemistry says failures arrive without warning. The insurance market says coverage is the first thing to go. And the patch trap says the repipe at the end costs the same whether you schedule it or the pipe schedules it for you. Doing nothing is a choice with a price tag. It is just denominated in risk instead of dollars, and risk has a way of converting to dollars at 2 a.m.

What to do this week

If you own a 1978-1995 home and have never checked: spend the fifteen minutes. Water-heater closet, under sinks, crawlspace. Gray flexible pipe with a PB2110 stamp means you have a project. Get two written repipe bids while nothing is leaking, because bids written during an emergency are always worse.

If your carrier asks about plumbing at renewal: answer honestly and get the answer in writing. A retroactive cancellation after a claim is worse than a surcharge you can plan around. If your carrier declines, price the repipe against the surplus-lines premium before assuming you can live with the surcharge.

If you are buying: make the pipe check part of the inspection contingency, price the credit at $4.50 a square foot, and confirm insurability before you remove contingencies. The prettiest kitchen in the showing is worth exactly nothing if the carrier will not write the policy.

If you repipe: pull the permit, replace the aging water heater while the walls are open, confirm in writing that patching and texture are included and paint is not, and keep the permit card and pressure-test record with the house documents. The next buyer's inspector will ask. You will have the answer.

Twenty years of watching projects go sideways teaches you that the expensive failures are never the ones you scheduled. They are the ones you deferred until the schedule picked itself. Polybutylene gave the industry a $950 million lesson in that distinction thirty years ago. The tuition is still being paid, one burst pipe at a time, by people who never got the memo. Now you have it.
