Your 3-0 x 4-0 Bedroom Window Doesn't Meet Egress Code. Nobody Measured It.

By Catherine Chen · September 27, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Nobody measured the bedroom windows at the final inspection. He checked the smoke alarms, tugged the handrail, tested the GFCI in the garage, and the windows got a glance. They opened, they had screens, they looked like windows. That glance is the entire enforcement regime for the one section of the building code written specifically for the night your house catches fire.

Section R310 of the International Residential Code is not long. Every sleeping room and every habitable basement needs at least one emergency escape and rescue opening. R310's numbers are blunt: 5.7 square feet of net clear opening (5.0 square feet for windows at grade level), minimum 24 inches of clear height, minimum 20 inches of clear width, sill no more than 44 inches above the floor, operable from the inside with no keys, no tools, no special knowledge. It even anticipates your cleverness: a step or box placed under the window to fake the 44-inch sill height does not count, because the code has seen you before.

Now the uncomfortable part: take the 3-0 by 4-0 double-hung, the workhorse of tract-housing bedroom windows, three feet wide and four feet tall, and run it against those numbers.

The math the window schedule skipped

"Net clear opening" means the actual airspace a person can get through, measured with the window open. On a double-hung, only the bottom sash opens, and the code counts only that sash, because opening the top one requires what the code drily calls "special knowledge." Start with a builder-grade vinyl unit and reasonable frame deductions: about 3.25 inches of frame and jamb intrusion per side, roughly 2 inches of sash and meeting-rail intrusion on the operable height.

A 36-by-48 double-hung gives you roughly 29.5 inches of clear width and 22 inches of clear height. Multiply: 29.5 times 22, divided by 144, is about 4.5 square feet. That fails the 5.7 requirement, and it also fails the 24-inch minimum height outright, which means the area calculation is almost beside the point. Whatever the window schedule says, a 3050 double-hung cannot legally serve as a bedroom egress window above grade. It fails twice: on area and on the 24-inch minimum height.

Go one size up, to a 3-0 by 5-0 double-hung. Clear width stays 29.5 inches, clear height rises to about 28. It passes, with an area of 5.74 square feet: four hundredths of a square foot to spare. One manufacturer's frame profile revision, one slightly beefier meeting rail, and the verdict flips. Your bedroom's legal escape route currently depends on a rounding error.

Casements have no such drama. A 3-0 by 4-0 casement swings nearly the whole frame open, delivering around 8.5 square feet of clear opening, which is why code-conscious builders default to casements for bedrooms. And there is a second trap hiding in the 5-0 double-hung that does pass: with the sill at the 44-inch maximum, the head of a 60-inch-tall window lands at 104 inches, which is 8 feet 8 inches, above a standard 8-foot ceiling. In a normal-height room, the minimum compliant double-hung often cannot physically fit under the header. Your framer's header height is not the code's problem, so measure twice, order once, or order casements.

Not every 3050 fails in every catalog. But the margin is thin enough that a builder ordering windows by rough-opening habit, rather than by net clear opening, is gambling.

Why this section exists, in numbers

The U.S. Fire Administration's fatality analysis found that bedrooms were the location of 51 percent of civilian fire deaths in residential buildings, that 51 percent of those deaths occurred between 11 p.m. and 7 a.m., and that 31 percent of victims were asleep when they died while another 37 percent were trying to escape. NFPA data puts half of all home fire deaths in the 11 p.m. to 7 a.m. window, with a quarter originating in the bedroom. These are people waking up disoriented, in the dark, with the hallway already untenable. That window is the second way out because the first way is on fire.

And the clock has collapsed. UL's Firefighter Safety Research Institute burned fully furnished homes to prove that occupants once had about 17 minutes to escape after a smoke alarm, and now have three minutes or less, thanks to synthetic furnishings, open floor plans, and lightweight construction that fails fast. Three minutes. In a burning house, at 2 a.m., half awake, you do not have time to discover that your window opens 22 inches high when the code wanted 24.

AI moved the check earlier. The defect stayed in the field.

This is the part where the technology vendors enter, and credit where due. OFA Group launched PlanAId commercially on September 8, an AI blueprint reviewer that checks exit-door counts and widths, travel distances, dead-end corridors, and egress capacity at $9.99 a month, after a beta that started last October. UpCodes shipped its own AI plan review in June. Egress compliance is exactly the kind of tedious cross-referencing AI is good at, and catching a bad window schedule before permit submission beats catching it at framing.

But notice what the AI reviews: drawings. Egress failures that actually kill people live downstream of the drawings: the substitution at the lumberyard when the specified casement is back-ordered and a double-hung goes in instead; the replacement window in a 1978 ranch, installed without a permit by a crew paid by the opening, that shrinks the clear area by an inch on every side; the painted-shut sash; the window AC unit bolted into the only egress opening in July and still there in January; the basement "bedroom" in a flipped house that was never permitted as one, which means no inspector ever looked, which means the buyer inherits a room that cannot legally be called a bedroom and cannot actually be escaped from. AI plan review is a genuine improvement to a genuine process, but it audits paper. The defect is in the field, and the audit tool for the field is still a tape measure.

The cost ladder, from free to five figures

Catching this at the window-schedule stage costs nothing. A builder-grade vinyl casement runs $400 to $700, essentially the price of the double-hung it replaces, and the only cost is someone doing the net-clear math before the order goes in. Miss it, and an above-grade bedroom window swap after construction runs roughly $500 to $1,000 per window by national cost guides.

Basements are where the money gets serious. Cutting a foundation for a proper egress window runs $3,000 to $6,000 all-in in 2026 Midwest pricing, covering the saw cut, lintel, window well, drainage, and trim, with national guides landing in the same band. Go deeper than 44 inches on the well and the code wants a ladder, which adds excavation and another $800 to $1,500. The Michigan contractor who published those numbers offers the obvious advice for new construction: if a basement might ever become a bedroom, put egress in during the pour, when the marginal cost is half the retrofit. He is right, though "might become a bedroom" is doing unpaid work in every basement finish ever sold.

Now the part the industry deserves to hear at full strength

Egress windows are the last line of defense, and last lines get too much credit. Three of every five home fire deaths happen in houses with no smoke alarms or none that work, per NFPA's long-running analysis, and UL's own research says a closed bedroom door buys more survival time than any window ever will, by holding back heat, smoke, and carbon monoxide while occupants wait for rescue. That 5.7 square feet, for the record, is not sized for you. It is sized for a firefighter in full protective gear coming in. A smaller window still lets most occupants out, which means the code is conservative by design, and conservative codes have costs: ground-floor egress windows are also intruder access, which is why security bars exist, which is why the code demands quick-release hardware on those bars, which is another device nobody maintains. A homeowner who spends $5,000 cutting a basement egress window while the smoke alarms have been chirping since March has misallocated the safety budget. Do the alarms first, close the doors, then measure the windows.

There is also an honest question about whether the 24-inch height minimum does what its authors think. A 22-inch-high opening fails inspection and a 24-inch one passes, but no fire researcher will tell you the 23rd inch is where survival begins. Codes draw lines because lines are enforceable, not because the line is where physics changes. Still worth enforcing, because without it the market drifts toward whatever is cheapest, and whatever is cheapest is a fixed picture window.

What this analysis does not prove

The clear-opening math uses assumed frame intrusions for a generic builder-grade vinyl double-hung. Real units vary, and the manufacturer's egress chart for the exact product is the authority, not this table. Cost ranges come from a Michigan contractor's 2026 price book and national aggregator guides; your market will differ, especially on the coasts. There is no national dataset on how often bedroom windows fail egress in the field; the claim that inspectors rarely measure net clear opening rests on inspector-forum discussion and the structural reality of final inspections, not a study. UL FSRI's 17-to-3-minute escape-time collapse is a widely cited campaign figure from full-scale burns, framed by an organization with a public-education mission. None of that weakens the central finding, which is arithmetic: a 3050 double-hung does not clear 5.7 square feet under any plausible frame assumption, and that is checkable with a tape measure today.

What to do Monday morning

If you are building, four moves. First, audit the window schedule for net clear opening, not rough opening, before the order goes in; the schedule should list the clear area per unit, and if it does not, that is the tell. Second, default bedrooms to casements, because the math above is the reason the reps push them and the reps are right. Third, verify the sill height on the framing walk, because a compliant window installed at 46 inches is a noncompliant window and the fix after drywall is a new window. Fourth, for basements that might ever hold a bed, cut the egress during construction; the retrofit costs double and the "might" has a way of becoming a teenager's bedroom within five years.

If you are buying, bring a tape measure to the showing. Open the bedroom window all the way, measure the actual airspace, and do the multiplication. A flipped basement bedroom with a 30-by-30 slider is not a bedroom; it is a liability with carpet. Ask for the permit history. And check the smoke alarms while you are at it, because the code section that saves the most lives is not R310. It is the nine-volt battery nobody replaced.
