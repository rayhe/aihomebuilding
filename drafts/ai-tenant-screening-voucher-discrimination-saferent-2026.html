The Algorithm Priced Her Rent at 140% of Her Income. She Actually Owed 31%.

By Catherine Chen, September 19, 2026, Policy & Regulation

Mary Louis got the denial by email. Not from a landlord. From what the email called "a third-party service," which had reviewed her rental application and decided she was too risky to house. Louis is a Black woman in Massachusetts. She held a federally funded housing voucher, which meant the government had already agreed to pay most of her rent. Behind the email was an algorithm called Registry ScorePLUS, which scored her between 200 and 800 the way it scored everyone: on credit history and court records, the financial paper trail of a life, with the voucher income, the guaranteed money, left out of the math entirely (Associated Press, November 2024).

In November 2024, a federal judge approved the end of that story. SafeRent Solutions, the company behind the score, agreed to pay $2.275 million to settle a class action alleging its algorithm discriminated on the basis of race and income, with no admission of fault, just the standard language about litigation being expensive and the scores complying with all applicable laws. But the settlement terms read like a confession with the interesting parts underlined: for five years, SafeRent may not put a score or an accept/decline recommendation on any report for a voucher holder, and its landlord customers must certify that the applicant is not a voucher recipient before they can even request a score. Read that twice. Its own product is now legally quarantined from the exact population it was mispricing (Bloomberg Law).

What the score could not calculate

This is where I want to show my work, because the mechanism matters more than the outrage. Start with HUD's own published numbers: the average Housing Choice Voucher household earns $17,111 a year and pays $437 a month toward rent, with the voucher covering the rest. Now put that household in front of a $2,000-a-month build-to-rent unit, the kind of product half the Sun Belt is putting up right now. Underwritten on personal income alone, the way a credit-weighted score underwrites them, the applicant faces a rent burden of 140 percent of income, a file no human underwriter would approve and one that fails every 3x-income screen ever written (HUD FY2025 Congressional Justification).

Her actual obligation is $437, thirty-one percent of income, while the federal government wires the other $1,563 every month, as close to guaranteed income as American housing policy produces. Treating that money as zero, the score mispriced this applicant's rent burden by roughly four and a half times, and it did so systematically, for every voucher holder, because the model was trained on financial histories shaped by generations of exclusion and never taught what a voucher is. That is disparate impact with the serial numbers filed off: no protected characteristic in the inputs, wildly unequal outcomes in the outputs. HUD's April 2024 guidance said the quiet part out loud, warning that a screening company can violate the Fair Housing Act "even if the decision for how to screen applicants is made in whole or in part by an automated system, including a system using machine learning or another form of AI" (HUD FHEO guidance, via Maryland DHCD).

Which brings us to the part of this story that reads like satire. HUD has since deleted that guidance from its own website; the Government Accountability Office noticed it was gone in February 2025 and asked why, and HUD offered two explanations, neither of which changes what follows. Guidance is not law. The Fair Housing Act is, the settlement is signed, and the disparate-impact theory the guidance described predates the guidance by decades. Deleting the PDF did not delete the liability. It just deleted the instructions (GAO-25-107196).

Meanwhile, the data was wrong too

Discrimination is only half the enforcement wave. Accuracy is the other half, and it arrived this summer: on July 9, 2026, the FTC announced that RentGrow, a tenant-screening consumer reporting company, will pay $2.25 million to settle allegations that it violated the Fair Credit Reporting Act and the FTC Act. Details from the complaint read like a compliance officer's nightmare, because RentGrow's system sometimes displayed a single eviction proceeding as multiple cases, so one filing looked like three, and it did the same with criminal records. Vendor data could be perfectly accurate and the report still misleading, since a filing, an update, and a disposition for the same case each became a separate row. When consumers asked what sources went into the report, the company allegedly did not disclose all of them, and when they disputed errors, some disputes were labeled "invalid" and dropped. Still pending a judge's approval, the settlement would require procedures to prevent duplicate records going forward (FTC; case summary).

Put the two tracks together and the picture gets expensive: $2.275 million for what the SafeRent score measured, $2.25 million for how the RentGrow report was built, four and a half million dollars of enforcement against tenant-screening automation in under two years, and the two cases do not even overlap. One is about bias in the model, the other about sloppiness in the plumbing, and a landlord relying on a default accept/deny score is standing at the intersection of both.

Colorado tried to write the rulebook, then rewrote it

States are filling the space where federal guidance used to sit. Messily. Colorado passed the country's first comprehensive AI law in 2024, Senate Bill 24-205, which would have forced deployers of high-risk AI, landlords using screening scores very much included, to run risk management programs, conduct impact assessments, and take reasonable care against algorithmic discrimination. Then the law spent two years getting delayed, sued (by xAI, of all plaintiffs), stayed by a federal judge in April 2026, and finally repealed and replaced by SB 26-189, signed May 14, 2026. Its replacement is narrower: if your automated system materially influences a consequential decision like a rental denial, you must tell the consumer AI is involved, explain the system's role in plain language within 30 days of an adverse decision, correct inaccurate data on request, and offer meaningful human review. It takes effect January 1, 2027, enforced only by the attorney general, no private lawsuits. Note what survived the rewrite: the human. Every version of this law, federal guidance included, converges on the same demand, which is that a person has to look at the file (McDermott Will & Schulte; TechTimes).

For the build-to-rent industry, the timing is awkward. Single-family built-for-rent was running around 70,000 starts a year at roughly 7 percent of single-family construction, more than double its historical share, before financing costs cooled it off in 2026. Build-to-rent's entire business model is leasing at scale, which means screening at scale, which means the default settings of whatever screening product the property manager bought three years ago. HUD's deleted guidance had a line for exactly this situation: landlords must evaluate applicants independently "even when using screening reports" and cannot "rely solely on default settings." Those defaults are the product, and the product is now the liability (NAHB via Realtor.com).

In defense of the score

I should steelman the other side, because it is stronger than housing advocates admit. Before the scores, there was the leasing agent's gut: unlogged, unappealable, and in plenty of cases more discriminatory than any model. A score can be audited. A hunch cannot. HUD itself concedes that screening serves a legitimate purpose, predicting whether an applicant will meet tenancy obligations, and the SafeRent settlement does not argue that scoring is illegal, only that this scoring was. There is also a version of the landlord's complaint that deserves a hearing: vouchers come with inspections, rent-reasonableness caps, and public housing authority bureaucracy, and a model that prices in that friction is not obviously the same as a model that prices in race. The risk of overcorrection is real, because if automated screening becomes legally radioactive, small landlords do not stop screening; they go back to informal judgments that no applicant can dispute and no regulator can see. Honest reform is not "no scores." It is scores that count the voucher, which, to be fair, is precisely what five years of injunction now forces SafeRent to build.

What I could not verify

A few honest boundaries. SafeRent's settlement admits nothing, so the "shared liability" reading, vendor and landlord both exposed, rests on settlement terms and lawyers' interpretations, not a litigated holding. RentGrow's settlement was still awaiting a judge's signature when I pulled the FTC's announcement. Colorado's replacement law does not take effect until January 2027, the attorney general's rulemaking is incomplete, and there is no private right of action, so its near-term bite is limited to AG enforcement. My 140-versus-31 calculation uses national HUD averages, not Mary Louis's actual file; it illustrates the mechanism, not her case. FHA penalty figures floating around legal-marketing sites ($23,011 for a first offense and up from there) should be treated as approximate until you check the current Federal Register adjustment.

A compliance checklist

For builders and operators leasing their own communities, the compliance picture is actually clearer than the politics. Turn off the default accept/decline and put a human in the loop, since individualized review is now effectively demanded in every jurisdiction that matters, and Colorado will demand it in writing by 2027. Underwrite the tenant's actual obligation, voucher included, not the gross rent. A $437 obligation is not a $2,000 risk. Get your screening vendor's duplicate-record controls in writing, because RentGrow just established that "the data was accurate when we received it" is not a defense. Publish your screening criteria, hand applicants the report and the specific standard they failed, and give them a real dispute process; it is the cheapest compliance on this list. If you operate in Colorado, calendar January 2027: notice at the point of AI interaction, plain-language adverse-decision explanations within 30 days, correction on request, human reconsideration. For applicants, especially voucher holders: you are entitled to the report, the criteria, and a dispute, and starting in 2027 in Colorado, to a human second look. Guidance got deleted. Rights did not.
