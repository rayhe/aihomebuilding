Lumber Is Up 23% in a Year. Your Framing Package Costs $2,250 More.

By Frank DeLuca · September 10, 2026

Every Thursday morning, the price sheet lands in your inbox, one page, maybe two, every dimension, every species, every grade, and you scan it the way a pilot scans weather, because that is what it is.

On the second Thursday of July, the Random Lengths Framing Lumber Composite printed at $535 per thousand board feet. That was the highest reading since September 2022 and 23 percent above the same week a year earlier, per the Wall Street Journal. If your supplier had given you a 30-day price lock the week before, congratulations: you bought the weather at a discount. If you waited, you paid retail for the storm.

Framing is 16.6 percent of the cost of building a new home, according to the NAHB's 2024 Cost of Construction Survey, the third-largest stage behind interior finishes and rough-ins. On the survey's average $428,215 construction cost, that is about $71,000 riding on wood. And wood, right now, is the most volatile line item in the budget.

This year's move was not demand-driven. Homebuilding is sputtering and renovation spending is soft, yet prices climbed anyway, because the supply side did the pushing, and when duties, mill closures, and wildfire smoke all lean on the same market at the same time, the only direction left is up. Preliminary antidumping and countervailing duties on Canadian softwood came down to a combined 25.9 percent, but the 10 percent Section 232 tariff stays, leaving an effective 35.9 percent on Canadian imports, per NAHB's framing lumber tracker. Sawmills from British Columbia to northern Florida curtailed or closed after last year's low prices. Fastmarkets expects North American softwood capacity to shrink by more than 1.3 billion board feet. Then Canadian wildfires added a supply scare on top. When three different hammers hit the same nail, the nail moves.

Naturally, somebody is selling an AI to predict which way it moves next, and the pitch has two halves that deserve very different levels of trust. LLAIE, the AI lumber estimator, advertises material cost trend tracking and markup recommendations on top of its plan-reading takeoff engine. Academics have been at this longer: a peer-reviewed comparison found neural networks beat classical ARIMA and ETS models at timber price forecasting, especially on outliers and turning points, which is exactly where the money is, while an Oregon State method claims six-month directional reads from observable data and a wood-panel futures paper using a dual-frequency LSTM-transformer fusion reported 96.1 percent trend accuracy over 60 days. The models are getting sharper. The question is whether sharpness buys you anything at the lumberyard.

The Math Nobody Runs on the Framing Package

NAHB cites Home Innovation Research Labs data: the average new single-family home uses roughly 15,000 board feet of framing lumber. From August 2022 through this year, CME physical lumber futures traded between $450.50 and $712.00 per thousand board feet, per Barchart, so run 15 thousand board feet against that range, where the full-range swing is $261.50 per thousand, and multiply.

$3,923
Full-range commodity swing per house on framing lumber, 2022-2026 trading range. Arithmetic on published benchmarks, not a quote from your yard.

That is the theoretical maximum, worst-timed buy against best-timed buy. The realistic 2026 version is smaller but still material. Madison's benchmark SPF 2x4 sat near $450 per thousand in January; summer composite and Southern Yellow Pine readings ran $535 to $595. Call the seasonal swing $100 to $150 per thousand board feet. Times 15 thousand board feet, that is $1,500 to $2,250 per house, and it repeats every spring. A builder framing 20 homes a year leaves $30,000 to $78,000 on the table depending on when the purchase orders go out, which is roughly one superintendent's salary, and it is decided entirely by calendar, by whether the buy order went out in the second week of January or the second week of July, a decision most companies make by habit rather than by data.

Your Yard Price Is Not the Composite

Now the other side, stated at full strength, because the lumber lobby's math is correct and it ruins the simpler version of this story. The U.S. Lumber Coalition, working from West Fraser's numbers, calculates that 2024's $400 average composite times 15 thousand board feet equals $6,000 of softwood in a standard home, about 1.2 percent of the $506,208 average new-home price, a figure drawn from West Fraser's own 2024 investor presentation and deployed specifically to argue that lumber duties are a rounding error in housing affordability, which, on the arithmetic, they are. Add transport and a healthy wholesaler margin and the builder's cost lands near $8,413, or 1.7 percent. Against a half-million-dollar home, a $2,000 lumber swing is noise next to land, labor, and the permit counter.

There are three more problems with the forecasting pitch. First, nobody buys at spot: yards sell on 30- to 60-day price locks, and the bunk price your rep quotes already smooths the weekly spikes the models are so proud of predicting. Second, the institutional hedge does not exist for you: the CME's smaller physical lumber contract carried about 8,077 contracts of open interest in late July, which is a puddle, not a market, and no residential GC is hedging a framing schedule through it. Third, the builders who tried to time the 2021-2022 cycle got destroyed in both directions, buying high into the spike and then sitting on expensive inventory into the crash. Fastmarkets' own analysts warn that forecasting in a policy-driven market is, and I quote, challenging to say the least. Tariffs and duties move faster than any model retrains.

The deepest cut: an AI that predicts the composite with 96 percent trend accuracy still cannot tell you what your yard will charge for your package on your delivery date. Forecasting the commodity is not forecasting the invoice.

What Actually Works

One. Price every framing package against the weekly composite, not last month's invoice. NAHB posts the Madison's index weekly. When your rep's quote drifts more than 5 percent above the composite trend, ask why before you sign.

Two. Time the lock, not the delivery. Lumber futures fell roughly 12 percent from their late-July high as mortgage rates rose and seasonal demand faded, with analysts suspecting the year's highs were in. A falling futures curve into fall is your window to negotiate the lock, even if the truck comes later.

Three. Respect seasonality, but verify it. Demand reliably softens in late fall and winter as construction slows, which is why the composite's lows cluster there. If your slab schedule allows it, forward-buying in November for spring starts has a multi-decade tailwind behind it. But 2026 proved supply shocks override seasonality, so treat the pattern as a prior, not a promise.

Four. Do the storage math before you stockpile. Holding a full framing package costs real money in yard space, handling, and theft risk. If the weekly move is $8 per thousand and storage costs you $60 per thousand per month, the trade needs a bigger swing than the market usually offers. Most small builders are better off with a sharp purchasing calendar than a lumberyard of their own.

Five. Use the AI tools for what they are actually good at. LLAIE's trend tracking is a fine input to a buy decision; its real value is the five-minute takeoff that tells you exactly how many board feet you are pricing, so the 15-thousand-board-foot assumption above becomes your actual number. Precision on quantity beats precision on price, every time.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The Random Lengths composite and Madison's index are wholesale and mill benchmarks, not delivered yard prices. Transport, handling, and yard margins vary by region and were not independently verified here; the Coalition's $75-per-thousand transport and 18 percent margin figures are industry estimates, not audited data. LLAIE's pricing, trend-tracking accuracy, and adoption are vendor claims from its own marketing materials, unverified. The $3,923 and $1,500-to-$2,250 figures are arithmetic on published benchmarks, not observed transaction savings, and no third-party study has measured builder-level savings from forecast-timed lumber buying. The 15-thousand-board-foot figure is a national average; smaller homes and panelized builds use less, and the swing scales linearly. Seasonality is a historical tendency that supply shocks routinely override.

Twenty years of watching jobs go sideways teaches you to respect the boring disciplines. Nobody ever saved a project with a brilliant lumber trade. Plenty of projects bled out through a thousand small procurement shrugs. The price sheet comes every Thursday morning. Read it like weather, lock like you mean it, and never let a 30-day price lock expire unpriced, because the one week you forget to check the sheet is the week the composite jumps forty dollars and your margin goes with it. The market does not care about your schedule. Your schedule had better care about the market.
