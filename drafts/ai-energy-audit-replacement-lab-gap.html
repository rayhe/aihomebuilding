Your $437 Energy Audit Just Lost Its Tax Credit. The AI That Could Replace It Is Stuck in a Lab.

Researchers trained an AI on 536,000 American homes. It identifies the right energy retrofit 98.9% of the time. No homeowner can use it.

Maria Torres paid $485 for a home energy audit on her 1,800-square-foot colonial in Columbus, Ohio, last March. Her auditor spent four hours with a blower door kit, an infrared camera, and a clipboard, crawling through her attic, pressurizing the ductwork, and scanning every exterior wall for thermal bridging. His report recommended insulation in three zones, duct sealing, and a heat pump water heater, and Torres followed the advice. Her gas bill dropped 31%.

She also claimed a $150 federal tax credit for the audit under Section 25C of the tax code, the Energy Efficient Home Improvement Credit, which allowed homeowners to offset part of the cost of a professional assessment alongside up to $2,000 annually for qualified heat pumps and $1,200 for insulation and windows. That credit no longer exists.

The One Big Beautiful Bill Act, signed July 4, 2025, repealed the entire 25C provision after December 31, 2025. According to Tax Foundation estimates, the repeal of all residential clean energy credits, including the companion 25D Residential Clean Energy Credit and the 45L New Energy Efficient Home Credit, is projected to save $267 billion over the next decade, which makes it fiscally significant, though what it costs individual homeowners trying to reduce their energy bills is harder to count and harder to argue about in the abstract.

A professional home energy audit now averages $437 nationally, with a typical range between $212 and $698 for a Level 2 assessment that includes blower door testing and infrared imaging, according to 2026 data from Angi. Comprehensive Level 3 audits with combustion safety analysis and duct leakage quantification can exceed $1,000. Every dollar of that cost now falls on the homeowner.

Whether that tax credit was worth the fiscal cost is a legitimate debate. What is harder to debate is the timing: the credit disappeared just as the technology to approximate some of the audit's diagnostic value began, for the first time, to actually work.

536,000 Homes, Nine Retrofit Categories, One Neural Network

In February 2026, a research team at Michigan State University published a paper that quietly rewrote the math on who needs a human energy expert, though almost no one outside the building-science community noticed it. Lei Shu, Armin Yeganeh, Sinem Mollaoglu, Jiayu Zhou, and Dong Zhao built a domain-specific large language model, fine-tuned with a technique called Low-Rank Adaptation on physics-based energy simulations and techno-economic calculations drawn from 536,416 U.S. residential building prototypes covering nine major retrofit categories, then tested it against the kind of information an ordinary homeowner could provide without any professional equipment.

Those prototypes came from ResStock 2024.2, a dataset maintained by the National Renewable Energy Laboratory that models the American housing stock across climate zones, construction eras, foundation types, insulation levels, HVAC configurations, and dozens of other parameters that collectively determine how a building performs. Rather than merely recommending retrofits, the MSU team asked their model to rank them by carbon reduction and by discounted payback period, using only basic dwelling characteristics: age of the house, type of heating system, approximate square footage, number of stories.

Results: 98.9%.

It identified the optimal retrofit for CO₂ reduction within its top three recommendations in 98.9% of test cases, and for shortest discounted payback period, the figure was 93.3%. Fine-tuning produced an order-of-magnitude reduction in CO₂ prediction error relative to generic language models, alongside multi-fold reductions in energy use and retrofit cost prediction errors, and the model held its accuracy even when inputs were incomplete, when the homeowner could not specify the insulation R-value or the exact vintage of the furnace. No homeowner can use it, because the model exists exclusively in a research paper, with no public release, no API, no consumer product, and no announced timeline for any of these.

What Happens When You Ask ChatGPT Instead

In a companion study published in MDPI's Buildings journal, the same Michigan State team tested what happens when homeowners turn to the AI tools they can actually access today. They put six commercially available large language models through the same retrofit decision-making tasks without any domain-specific fine-tuning: ChatGPT o3, DeepSeek R1, Grok 3, Gemini 2.0, Llama 3.2, and Claude 3.7.

On the broad strokes, the generic models performed respectably. They understood that insulation matters, that heat pumps outperform resistance heating, and that air sealing is usually the cheapest first step, which made them, in the Insulation Institute's characterization of the research, "effective" at producing retrofit options that belonged on a reasonable list.

They were substantially worse at the question that matters when you are holding a checkbook: which retrofit delivers the best result most quickly and at the lowest cost? All six struggled with ranking, local cost sensitivity, and the building-physics interactions that make one measure dramatically more effective than another depending on the specific house. A heat pump upgrade in a poorly insulated home wastes capacity heating air that leaks through the envelope all winter, while air sealing in a tight modern home may save so little energy that the payback stretches past two decades, and the sequence of interventions matters as much as the selection. None of the generic models reliably got the sequence right, and the researchers concluded, in measured but pointed language, that the models "must be improved before they can be relied upon to make building retrofit decisions."

The 48-Minute Audit

A separate research effort published in iScience by a team at the University of Cambridge attacked the problem from the professional side rather than the consumer side. Their framework, Data2BEM, uses a multi-agent LLM system that parses architectural drawings, building specifications, and sensor data, then automatically generates and calibrates building energy simulations meeting ASHRAE Guideline 14 accuracy benchmarks, the same standard professional human auditors are held to.

Modeling time dropped from between 8 and 32 hours of professional work to 48 minutes with minimal human input, and when applied to a test building, the system found that switching from gas heating to an air-source heat pump would cut annual energy costs by 44% and reduce the building's carbon tax exposure roughly fivefold.

Two caveats constrain the celebration. Data2BEM was tested on a commercial office building, not a single-family home, and residential buildings are smaller, more varied in construction quality, and far less likely to have the architectural drawings and sensor data that the system ingests, which means translating the framework to the housing stock will require significant further development that has not yet been done. That 90% time reduction is measured against professional-grade energy modeling, which is work that most homeowners never commission and cannot afford, so the tool accelerates the practice of specialists rather than the decisions of ordinary owners.

The Physical Gap AI Cannot Close

There is a strong case that none of this replaces an actual person standing in your attic with a flashlight and an infrared gun.

A blower door test, the $350 diagnostic at the core of every serious professional energy audit, depressurizes your house to 50 pascals and measures exactly how much air leaks through the thermal envelope. It finds the section of vapor barrier behind the knee wall that rodents chewed through sometime in the late 1990s, and it quantifies the disconnected duct boot in the crawlspace that has been pumping conditioned air into the ground for six consecutive winters, neither of which will ever appear in any statistical model of houses like yours, regardless of how many prototypes the training dataset contains.

MSU's 98.9% accuracy figure is measured against physics simulations of idealized building prototypes, not against actual homes built by contractors who occasionally cut corners during the Ford administration. ResStock archetypes capture the statistical distribution of American housing: the framing materials, insulation levels, window types, and HVAC vintages that characterize each construction era and climate zone across the country. They do not capture the bathroom exhaust fan venting into the attic instead of through the roof, or the previous owner's unpermitted garage conversion that added square footage but not insulation, or the ductwork leaking into an unconditioned plenum above the drop ceiling in the basement. Between a prototype and a house lies the same gap as between epidemiology and a doctor's visit, and both disciplines are valuable precisely because they answer fundamentally different questions.

A responsible deployment of this technology would position the AI model as a triage layer that tells every homeowner which category of retrofit is most likely to pay for itself first, then routes owners with complex, ambiguous, or historically unusual building conditions toward a professional audit where the blower door and the infrared camera do the work that no training dataset can simulate. That deployment does not exist.

The Policy Collision

What makes this moment unusual is the convergence of two trends that have nothing to do with each other. The federal government pulled the financial support for professional energy audits at the same time that researchers demonstrated AI could approximate some of their diagnostic value, and neither side was responding to the other, because the OBBBA's authors were targeting IRA spending totals while the MSU team was publishing a computer science paper about fine-tuning techniques.

That overlap is accidental. For homeowners, it is also unhelpful.

What remains is fragmented. WAP, the Weatherization Assistance Program, still provides free audits and retrofits for income-qualifying households, with an average value between $6,500 and $8,000 per home, and some state and utility programs continue to offer rebated or free basic audits that homeowners can locate through dsireusa.org. DOE's Genesis initiative has announced plans to partner with machine learning and agentic AI platform developers to accelerate building energy analysis and permitting, though its specific residential impact timeline is undefined.

Meanwhile, H.R. 4758, which passed the House in February 2026, would repeal the $4.5 billion High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebate Program entirely, eliminating the IRA provision that covered part of the upfront cost of heat pumps, insulation, and air sealing for low- and moderate-income households. About a dozen states had already opened their programs under that funding. Others were still waiting for DOE approval of their detailed plans when the House voted to eliminate the program's authorization. ACEEE's Jennifer Layke called the repeal "a blow to affordability."

What This Means for You

If you own a home built before 2000 and have never had a professional energy audit, the probability is high that you are losing money through the building envelope every month. ACEEE, the American Council for an Energy-Efficient Economy, estimates that targeted retrofits, primarily insulation, air sealing, and HVAC upgrades, can reduce residential energy consumption by 20 to 40% in older homes, which translates to roughly $400 to $1,200 per year at current national average electricity rates of $0.17 per kilowatt-hour and natural gas prices around $1.60 per therm.

You can ask any general-purpose AI what to do about your energy bills. It will give you a reasonable list. It will not reliably tell you which item on that list to do first, or what the payback period looks like for your particular house in your particular climate zone, because the fine-tuned model trained on 536,416 American residential prototypes that can answer those questions with 98.9% accuracy exists in an academic paper at Michigan State University and has not been turned into a product that any consumer can access, and no one involved is on a public timeline to change that.

A professional energy audit, now $437 out of pocket with no federal credit to offset any portion of the expense, remains the most reliable path to a prioritized, building-specific retrofit plan. If your utility offers a discounted version, take it before the program expires. If you qualify for WAP assistance, apply, because the program's own long-term funding stability is not guaranteed. If neither is available, the $437 typically pays for itself within one heating season through the targeted improvements it identifies, which is the same return it delivered when the government covered $150 of it, only now the entire bet is yours.

Technology that could democratize this expertise is real. Policy infrastructure that subsidized the existing alternative just expired. Between those two facts is where 130 million American homeowners currently live.

Limitations of This Analysis

The MSU fine-tuned model's 98.9% accuracy is validated against physics-simulation baselines from ResStock 2024.2, not field-tested against actual audit outcomes in occupied homes, and the gap between prototype performance and real-world building conditions is acknowledged in the paper but remains unquantified. Data2BEM's 90% time reduction was demonstrated on a commercial building, and residential applicability requires further validation that has not been published. State and utility incentive programs vary significantly by location, so the $437 average audit cost may be partially or fully offset in some jurisdictions, and this article does not evaluate every state program. Energy savings estimates use national average utility rates and will differ by region, building size, occupancy patterns, and local fuel mix.
