Your Cool Roof Shingles Were Rated in a Lab. Three Years of Dust Ate 45% of the Payback.

By Priya Greenwood · October 6, 2026 · 7 min read

Your cool shingles' ENERGY STAR label promises a solar reflectance of 0.25. Fine print allows 0.15 after three years of weather. A decade of exposure-farm data says the dust always collects its share, and the difference lands on your utility bill.

Your roof has a number, and an algorithm already knows it. Researchers mapping 78 cities with satellite imagery and machine learning found that the median urban roof reflects just 21 percent of the sunlight hitting it, half what the cool-roof brochures promise. Most of the gap between the brochure and your roof is made of dust.

Reflectance is the whole product: a dark roof in summer sun can run 50 to 90 degrees Fahrenheit hotter than the air around it, according to Lawrence Berkeley National Laboratory, while a reflective roof stays within 10 to 20 degrees of ambient. That temperature gap becomes your attic temperature, your air conditioner's workload, and a visible line on your summer electric bill.

Berkeley Lab proved it in the field, not just in a model. In Fresno, researchers monitored two identical new homes side by side for nearly a year, same floor plan, same weather, same blazing Central Valley sun. Only the hat differed: one house wore dark asphalt shingles, the other a reflective concrete tile roof with an air gap beneath it. On a summer day the cool roof ran 13.8 degrees Celsius cooler, cut cooling electricity 26 percent, and shaved 37 percent off peak demand.

With numbers like that the pitch writes itself: swap the dark shingles for cool-rated ones, pocket the savings, maybe collect a rebate. A federal bill proposed paying homeowners 25 to 75 cents per square foot for steep-slope cool products, with the payout keyed to aged reflectance. California's Title 24 already requires cool roofs on most re-roofs in the state's hot zones.

Read the label closely, though, because ENERGY STAR's steep-slope requirement is an initial solar reflectance of 0.25, while the maintenance requirement, measured three years after installation, is 0.15. A product can lose 40 percent of its promised reflectivity and keep the blue label. That is not a loophole; it is the standard, written by people who know exactly what weather does.

The label is fresh; your roof is not.

Nobody at the Cool Roof Rating Council guesses about aging. It runs exposure farms in Florida, Arizona, and Ohio, racks of roofing samples left out in the weather for three years, then publishes both numbers: fresh and weathered. Berkeley Lab's Ronnen Levinson and colleagues analyzed the CRRC and ENERGY STAR databases and found the pattern nobody puts in the brochure. High initial reflectance is what gets lost, while products that started dark sometimes gain a little, because ordinary grime is lighter than asphalt.

How much you lose is decided by geography. In the site-resolved CRRC data, absolute reflectance losses for medium-to-high initial reflectance products ran two to three times greater in Florida, hot and humid, than in Arizona, hot and dry, with Ohio, temperate but polluted, landing in between. Humidity grows things on roofs while aridity mostly dusts them.

Climate is only half the story; material matters as much. Largest aged losses showed up in field-applied coatings, modified bitumen, and single-ply membranes, while the smallest showed up in factory-applied coatings and metal. Asphalt shingles sit in the middle of the pack: not the worst, nowhere near the best. A cool shingle in Tampa and the same cool shingle in Tucson are different products by year three, and only one of them paid for itself.

The soiling tax, in dollars

Here is the math the brochure skips. Take a cool asphalt shingle marketed at 0.30 initial reflectance against a conventional dark shingle at 0.08. The brochure promises a 0.22 advantage, which shrinks to 0.12 once the shingle ages to 0.20, still legal under every standard that matters. Forty-five percent of the cooling benefit is gone, collected by dust.

Berkeley Lab's cool-colored-roof research gives us the exchange rate. Raising a roof's reflectance from 0.10 to 0.40 saves roughly 250 kilowatt-hours a year in mild climates and more than 1,000 in very hot ones, per 100 square meters of roof. That works out to about 83 to 333 kilowatt-hours per tenth of a point of reflectance per thousand square feet. Losing a tenth of a point to soiling on a 2,000-square-foot roof erases 155 to 620 kilowatt-hours a year. At twenty cents a kilowatt-hour, the dust costs you 30 to 125 dollars every single year.

EPA's numbers land in the same neighborhood. Its Heat Island Compendium models cool-roof conversions across California's 16 climate zones and finds average savings of 297 kilowatt-hours and 372 dollars a year per 1,000 square feet of roof, against a small winter heating penalty. Haircut that conversion by 45 percent and a 2,000-square-foot roof leaks about 335 dollars a year in promised savings. Your exact figure depends on climate zone, insulation, and utility rate; the direction does not.

None of this means the cool shingle was worthless. An aged 0.20 still doubles or triples the reflectance of a charcoal architectural shingle. It means you bought the fresh number and you live with the dusty one, and the payback math in the sales pitch used the fresh number.

Florida dust is not Arizona dust

The practical split is humidity. In hot-dry climates, soiling is mostly mineral dust, and the losses run about a third of what humid climates see. A cool shingle in Phoenix keeps most of its advantage. In Houston or Orlando, the same shingle grows a thin biological film that rain never fully removes, and the reflectance slides toward the ENERGY STAR floor.

There is a maintenance lever, with caveats: Berkeley Lab's weathering studies found that washing samples with detergent restored most of them to within 90 percent of their unweathered reflectance, though some needed an algaecide to get there. That work was done on single-ply membranes, not asphalt shingles, so treat it as direction rather than prescription. Pressure-washing an asphalt shingle roof can strip granules and void the warranty. If you wash, soft-wash, and check the manufacturer's guidance first.

Buy the aged number

The CRRC's Rated Products Directory publishes both numbers for more than 3,000 products, independently verified, so shop it by aged reflectance, not initial. A shingle sitting near the 0.15 ENERGY STAR floor on its aged rating is a dark roof with a blue sticker by year four in a humid climate. The directory is free; the brochure is advertising.

Material choice is the bigger lever, because factory-applied cool coatings and cool metal hold their reflectance far better than cool asphalt shingles through the same three years of weather. If you are re-roofing in a humid hot zone and the budget stretches, that is where the extra money earns its keep. That premium buys durability of performance, not just durability of material.

California homeowners get a code shortcut worth knowing. Title 24's prescriptive cool-roof requirement for steep-slope re-roofs, aged reflectance 0.20, vanishes entirely if you have R-38 in the attic, a radiant barrier, or R-2 of insulation above the deck. Replacing half the roof or less also exempts you. Know the exceptions before you pay the cool-shingle premium for compliance you did not need.

The other side

Here is the honest counterargument: cool shingles are still strictly better than the dark alternative. Even aged down to 0.15 or 0.20, they reflect double or triple what a charcoal architectural shingle does, and they usually price inside the normal architectural shingle range rather than far above it. Skeptics cite a winter heating penalty, and it is real but small: EPA's California modeling puts it under five therms a year per 1,000 square feet. In arid climates the soiling tax is modest enough that the brochure math nearly holds. Nothing here says cool roofs are a scam. It says the label number is the fresh number, and fresh numbers fade.

What this analysis does not prove

The 45 percent figure assumes savings scale linearly with reflectance and follows one specific aging path, 0.30 to 0.20. Real products vary by formulation, color, and microclimate; the CRRC directory carries product-level aged values, and this article generalizes across them. EPA's compendium baseline is a dark-to-white conversion, so interpolating down to shingle-scale deltas is approximate. Published data isolating the price premium of "cool" over standard architectural shingles is thin; cool lines price as premium lines, and no dataset cleanly separates the two. This piece's soiling database dates to 2011, though later Berkeley Lab work confirms the humid-beats-arid pattern. And the wash-restoration finding comes from membrane samples, not shingles. Treat the maintenance advice as informed extrapolation, flagged as such.

Your move

If you are re-roofing, look up the shingle's aged solar reflectance in the CRRC directory before you sign, not the initial number on the data sheet. An aged rating near 0.15 buys you three good years and twenty mediocre ones.

In hot-humid climate zones, price factory-coated metal or cool tile against the cool shingle and compare aged numbers, not brochure numbers. That post-weathering reflectance gap between the materials is the real premium you are paying for.

If you already own a cool roof, a gentle detergent wash can recover most of the lost reflectance. Soft-wash only on asphalt shingles, and confirm with the manufacturer that it will not void your warranty.

In California, check the Title 24 exceptions first. R-38 attic insulation, a radiant barrier, or a partial re-roof can exempt you from the cool-roof requirement entirely, which changes the value math on the premium shingle.

Your roof's number is already known to the satellite. Soon your roofer's quoting software will know it too, because albedo you can measure from orbit is albedo you can price, and when that happens the fresh-number game ends. Buy the aged number now and you will already be ahead of it.
