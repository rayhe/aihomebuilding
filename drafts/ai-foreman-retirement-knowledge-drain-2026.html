28% of Construction Foremen Are 55 or Older. Nobody Wrote Down What They Know.

By Marcus Washington • October 5, 2026

On his last Friday, the lead framer cleaned out a gang box he had owned longer than some of his crew had been alive. Forty-odd years of shims, story poles, and a tape measure worn smooth at the 16-inch marks. Somebody brought a sheet cake, and somebody else took a photo. Monday morning, a 24-year-old with a good attitude and six months of experience inherited the crew, the schedule, and every judgment call the old man used to make before coffee.

Nobody filmed him explaining why he always doubled the header over the garage door. Nobody asked.

This scene is playing out on residential job sites across the country, and the numbers behind it are stark enough to stop treating it as a personnel problem; it is a structural one.

The crew chief is 46, and the clock is ticking

Start with the federal data, because it does not care about anyone's pitch deck. The Bureau of Labor Statistics' Current Population Survey for 2025 breaks employed people down by detailed occupation and age, and the construction rows read like a retirement schedule:

27.8%
of front-line construction supervisors are 55 or older (median age 46.4)
30.8%
of cost estimators are 55 or older (median age 45.5)
20.5%
of carpenters are 55 or older (median age 41.5)
18.0%
of electricians are 55 or older (median age 39.6)

Read that first number again. More than one in four of the people who actually run crews, the foremen and lead supers who decide the sequence of every day, are at or past the age when retirement starts looking like a plan. Their median age, 46.4, is the oldest of any field trade in the survey. Older still are the people who estimate the jobs. Their replacements, in large part, are not in the pipeline: CPWR, the construction industry's research arm, has tracked the 55-and-older share of the workforce climbing from 17 percent in 2011 to roughly 22 percent today, while workers under 25 make up barely a tenth of the industry.

Associated Builders and Contractors put the 2026 hiring need at 349,000 net new workers, rising to 456,000 in 2027. Here is the sentence that matters most in ABC chief economist Anirban Basu's release: a majority of the 2026 demand comes from replacing retiring workers, not from growth, not from the data center boom, but from retirements. NCCER has long estimated that roughly 41 percent of the current construction workforce will retire by 2031.

So the industry does not just need bodies. It needs the specific bodies that are leaving, because those bodies carry something no hiring bonus can buy.

What walks out the door at 3:30 on Friday

A few years back, the BIM manager at Hawaiian Dredging Construction Company in Honolulu watched his best estimators approach retirement age and realized something uncomfortable. As he told New Civil Engineer, his veteran estimators could look at a set of plans and tell you the rough cost and duration of a high-rise in a few hours. All of it lived in their heads, and none of it was documented. His rule of thumb for how long that judgment takes to build: "You probably have to build about 10 high rise buildings before you can really say that you know what is going on."

Translate that to residential. Nobody publishes the equivalent number for a framing lead or a trim carpenter, but every GC reading this has one in their head. It is measured in dozens of houses, hundreds of inspections, thousands of small disasters averted because somebody recognized the smell of a problem before it became one. Think of the veteran foreman as the crew's answer key for the thousands of micro-decisions that never appear in the drawings: which sub to call first when the slab is wet, how to sequence around a late truss delivery, when concrete is ready by look and feel rather than by the clock.

That is tacit knowledge, and it has a property that should worry everyone selling a software fix for it. Philosopher Michael Polanyi put it this way: we know more than we can tell. No foreman who just knows the concrete is ready can dictate that knowing into a standard operating procedure, though he can try. What comes out will be true and incomplete, like a recipe that says "cook until done."

The industry that wants to download him

Into this gap has walked a wave of AI vendors with essentially the same pitch: let us capture what your veterans know before they leave. Hawaiian Dredging used a platform from Alice Technologies that turned scheduling knowledge into what the company called "recipes," reusable chunks of expert judgment. Trunk Tools, which raised serious money to build AI for construction document work, launched its Cortex agent platform in June with language about making "every team run like your best one." ENR has described sensor-driven AI platforms as a form of "institutional memory" that learns why delays happened and what distinguishes a normal idle period from a red flag.

A December 2025 survey by Dodge Construction Network and CMiC, cited in the Trunk Tools launch, found that 87 percent of contractors expect AI to meaningfully reshape construction, while only 19 percent have actually adapted their workflows to it. That 68-point gap between belief and behavior is the whole story. Everybody agrees the knowledge drain is real, and almost nobody has changed how they work.

Some of that is the tools' fault: most knowledge-capture products capture documents, not judgment. They ingest the drawings, the RFIs, the daily logs, and produce a searchable pile of what was already written down. What was never written down, the reason the pile exists in a particular order, stays in the foreman's head, because getting it out requires the foreman to stop building and start narrating, which is the one thing a good foreman will not do during working hours.

About $1,000 a year to capture, $15,000-plus to replace

Here is a calculation I have not seen anyone in the trade press actually run. Take one veteran lead and run the numbers: once a week, Friday afternoon, fifteen minutes, he talks through the week's three hardest calls into his phone while the concrete cures or the crew cleans up. An AI transcription service turns it into text at roughly twenty dollars a month. Somebody, an office manager or a junior PM, files the transcripts by job phase and tags the recurring topics.

Start with the time cost: thirteen hours a year of a lead's time, and assume a fully loaded cost of sixty dollars an hour, which is a judgment call but a conservative one for a working foreman in most residential markets. That is $780 a year in time plus about $240 in tooling. Call it an even thousand dollars per lead per year to build a narrated record of how your best people think.

On the other side of the ledger, a long-cited U.S. Department of Labor estimate puts the cost of a bad hire at up to 30 percent of first-year pay. A construction recruiting firm's 2026 analysis puts replacing a skilled tradesperson at more than $15,000 once you count lost output, retraining, and re-recruiting, and replacing a construction leader at $50,000 to $75,000. SHRM's broader research lands between half and twice annual salary depending on the role.

So the capture program costs roughly seven percent of a single skilled-trade replacement. Even if you assume, pessimistically, that the captured knowledge is only a fifth as useful as having the person standing on the slab, the expected value clears the cost three times over. And that is before you price the thing contractors actually fear: the six weeks a green crew spends relearning, through rework, what the veteran knew for free.

Urgency comes from the AGC and NCCER 2026 workforce survey. Eighty-three percent of firms reported at least some turnover among new field employees in their first ninety days, driven mostly by the gap between what the job was sold as and what it is. A narrated record of real job-site judgment does not just preserve knowledge. It gives new hires an honest preview of the work, which is the cheapest retention tool in the industry.

Why the veterans will not talk, and why the rookies cannot listen

Now the part the pitch decks skip. A sharp ConstructionExec analysis from this summer named the two-sided adoption trap, and it is worth quoting in spirit: veteran estimators who have spent decades inside legacy workflows see no reason to adopt new AI tools, so their expertise stays locked in methods nobody else can see. Meanwhile, handing a junior an AI tool that answers nearly any question risks producing someone who can get answers without developing judgment, which means the gap does not close; it gets outsourced.

There is a harder truth underneath that, about why the veterans stay quiet. Veterans have rational reasons to keep what they know to themselves. This is an industry that laid off a generation of craftsmen in 2008 and then acted surprised when the apprenticeship pipeline did not refill itself. In that world, being the only person who knows how the thing works is not selfishness; it is job security. If the captured knowledge gets used to justify paying the replacement less, or to deskill the role into following an app's instructions, the veterans are right not to cooperate, and no Friday voice memo program will fix it.

My family is union, so I was raised to respect the craft and to distrust anyone who shows up with a plan to extract value from workers while calling it empowerment. Let me say the uncomfortable part plainly: a big share of this crisis is self-inflicted. For decades, the industry treated skilled labor as a commodity input, squeezed training budgets, fought prevailing wage standards, and let the apprenticeship system wither. Now the bill is due, and the proposed payment method is software that records the knowledge of the workers the industry undervalued, so it can be used after they are gone. If that sounds extractive, it is because it can be, depending entirely on who benefits.

Here is the honest version of the AI knowledge-capture story: software will not save the industry. It can help, at the margins, if and only if the humans are treated as partners: paid for their knowledge, credited for it, and given a reason to believe the record they create will make the next generation better rather than cheaper.

What to actually do Monday morning

If you run a residential GC or a trade crew, here is the program. It costs almost nothing, which is the point.

Name your single points of failure by writing down the one lead per crew whose departure would break your schedule. Most owners can do this from memory in ninety seconds. That list is your capture priority, in order.

Start Friday debriefs: fifteen minutes, phone voice memo, three hardest calls of the week, transcribed with any twenty-dollar-a-month AI tool and filed by job phase. Do not aim for comprehensive; aim for the five tasks your crews rework most.

Pair every green hire with a veteran for the first ninety days. This attacks the 83 percent early-turnover number directly. Paired work is the oldest knowledge-transfer technology humans have: the apprentice model built the cathedrals, and it still works.

Build the video library for rework, not for everything. Have your best carpenter narrate one window install, one stair layout, one roof tie-in on a phone camera. Three videos that prevent your three most expensive callbacks are worth more than a hundred generic SOPs nobody opens.

Pilot the enterprise tools on one crew, with buy-in in writing. If you evaluate platforms like Trunk Tools' Cortex or the recipe-style capture tools, run them with a single crew for a quarter and get the veterans' explicit agreement on how the captured knowledge gets used. Captured knowledge used against the people who created it kills cooperation permanently.

If you are buying or building a home: ask your GC who runs the crew day to day, how long that person has been with the firm, and what happens to your schedule if they leave. Then ask what the firm does to train the person standing next to them. Those answers will tell you more about your project's odds than the bid price will. A contractor with a deliberate answer to the retirement question is a contractor who plans further ahead than next Friday.

Limitations of this analysis

The BLS age breakdowns are computed from the Current Population Survey's 2025 Table 11b; BLS notes the 2025 annual figures are eleven-month averages because October data was not collected during the federal government shutdown, so treat year-over-year comparisons cautiously. BLS publishes age data at the industry level, not residential-only; residential skews are inferred from the overall construction figures, not measured directly. Replacement-cost figures come from a recruiting firm's published guide citing Department of Labor estimates, not a peer-reviewed study, and SHRM's 50-to-200-percent range is wide enough to drive a truck through. Vendor-cited in a product launch, the Dodge/CMiC 87-percent-versus-19-percent figures come without a methodology I could review. Note the dates: the Hawaiian Dredging case is from 2022, and Alice Technologies' current product direction is unverified, so treat it as proof of concept rather than a current endorsement. My capture-versus-replace model assumes a $60-per-hour loaded wage and 20 percent knowledge-transfer effectiveness; both are judgment calls, shown here so you can substitute your own numbers. Immigration-enforcement labor effects come from industry self-reported surveys. Nothing in this article replaces a workforce consultant's assessment of your specific market, trade mix, and crew.

The foreman's gang box is empty now, and the question is whether anyone thought to ask what was in his head before the cake was cut. A thousand dollars a year and fifteen minutes on Fridays is not a moonshot. It is the cheapest insurance a contractor can buy against the most predictable risk in the business: the day the person who knows how everything works stops coming in.
