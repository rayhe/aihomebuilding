Your Contractor's AI Took Notes at Every Meeting. Now Opposing Counsel Has the Transcript.

By Catherine Chen

The progress meeting lasted forty-two minutes. A general contractor's project manager had Otter.ai running on his laptop, the way he always did. It captured everything. It captured the structural engineer saying the settlement reading was "probably within tolerance." It captured the owner asking whether the waterproofing was done and the PM saying "we're on it." It captured the architect noting, off-handedly, that the window header looked undersized but that it was "probably fine for now."

Eighteen months later, the foundation wall cracked. When the owner filed suit, the contractor's attorney discovered that "probably within tolerance," "we're on it," and "probably fine for now" were no longer informal reassurances exchanged between professionals who understood their context. They were timestamped, searchable, discoverable evidence.

All along, the AI had been taking notes, and it remembered everything.

The documentation arms race nobody planned

Construction has always been a documentation business, and the party with better records wins the dispute — a principle that drove decades of CYA culture: careful meeting minutes, selectively worded emails, RFIs with audit trails. An experienced project manager knew what to write down and, just as importantly, what to leave out, because the purpose of meeting minutes was never to create a verbatim record but to establish an agreed-upon account of decisions made and commitments given.

AI meeting transcription tools obliterate that distinction entirely. They record everything. All of it. Every hedged commitment, every candid aside, every moment of professional uncertainty that a human note-taker would have smoothed into anodyne language or simply left out. What emerges is a new category of evidence that construction law has barely begun to absorb, and that most contractors are creating without understanding what they are building against themselves.

One in four construction projects produces a formal claim, according to an Independent Project Analysis study cited by the National Academies. HKA's CRUX Insight Report analyzed 662 projects across the Americas with an average capital expenditure of $583 million and found that claims and disputes added 31.7 percent to budgeted project costs while extending schedules by 57 percent. Resolution takes an average of 16.2 months in North America, which means the AI transcript of a progress meeting held in January 2026 could be sitting in a discovery production request by the spring of 2027.

Litigation is not an outlier in this industry. It is a cost center. Every AI-generated transcript sitting in a vendor's cloud is potential ammunition, waiting for a subpoena to pull it into the light.

What the courts have already decided

Courts are moving fast — faster than the industry. In Heppner, a Canadian court ruled that uploading documents to a publicly accessible AI platform constituted a waiver of confidentiality and legal privilege. No lawyer-client relationship could exist with an AI platform, the court reasoned; communications processed through a public model could not be considered confidential, and sharing was equivalent to disclosing to a third party.

In September 2025, the American Bar Association's GPSolo division warned that AI notetakers "may inadvertently destroy privilege or expose privileged communications," noting that these tools typically process audio in the cloud, granting a third party access to what would otherwise be protected conversations. Morrison Foerster documented how plaintiffs' attorneys are already testing wiretapping theories against AI notetakers, citing In re Otter.AI Privacy Litigation (N.D. Cal., Case No. 5:25-cv-06911), in which plaintiffs allege the service recorded meeting participants without consent and used their voices to train its AI models.

In Dixon v Royal Live Oaks Academy, a federal court in South Carolina declined to consider auto-generated transcripts at summary judgment because the transcripts contained errors. That cuts both ways: an AI transcript can be challenged as unreliable when it helps you, cited as damning when it hurts you, and introduced selectively by opposing counsel who read all 4,200 words while your team never reviewed them once.

White & Case, analyzing the governance risks, identified a specific vulnerability: when AI-generated transcripts differ from formal meeting minutes, adverse parties and regulators may rely on the more detailed AI version. Your curated record, the one you spent thirty minutes drafting, loses to the raw transcript nobody reviewed.

Construction's particular exposure

Construction's exposure runs deeper than most industries, for three reasons that compound on each other.

First, construction meetings are where uncertainty gets expressed out loud, where professionals who know what they are doing talk through problems in real time using the kind of shorthand and hedging language that sounds reasonable in context and reckless on paper. A structural engineer says a measurement is "close enough" while mentally planning to verify later. An architect voices a concern about a detail that turns out to be fine. A contractor hedges on a schedule because he does not yet have the subcontractor committed. These are normal professional conversations, the kind of hedged, contextual exchanges that happen on every job site and in every progress meeting in the country. In a transcript, stripped of tone, body language, and the shared understanding that everyone in the room brought to the table, they read like admissions of negligence. Damning ones.

Second, construction operates across jurisdictions with different recording consent requirements, and a project that spans a video call between a California architect, a Nevada-based builder, and an Arizona subcontractor can trigger three different legal frameworks in a single forty-minute meeting. California, where a significant share of US construction spending occurs, is a two-party consent state under Penal Code Section 632. An AI notetaker that joins a Zoom progress meeting typically obtains consent from only the account holder. Every other participant may be recorded without adequate notice or consent. Nobody asks. Nobody discloses. Nobody even thinks about it until the lawsuit arrives. Otter.ai reached $100 million in annual revenue while, according to the class-action complaint, its software recorded non-account-holding participants who never agreed to anything.

Third, construction professionals are adopting these tools as productivity aids without any legal review, without consulting the project attorney, without checking the terms of service, without reading the vendor's data retention policies, and without considering for even a moment that the meeting they are about to record could become the centerpiece of a lawsuit they have not yet imagined. An HCR Law analysis observed that "the gold standard of construction evidence has traditionally been the contemporaneous record, including meeting minutes and site diaries," and that as these records become AI-generated, "their reliability will inevitably be challenged in litigation or arbitration." Construction attorney Michael Vardaro, who has spent thirty years representing contractors in disputes where meeting minutes are the first thing both sides reach for, has called AI meeting minutes "the hidden danger" that could make a contractor's legal position worse, not better.

What an AI transcript does that human minutes do not

A human minute-taker performs an editorial function, and a crucial one at that. They compress forty-two minutes of discussion into a page and a half of decisions, action items, and agreed-upon next steps. When the PM said "the footing looks a little off but we'll deal with it," that exchange vanishes from the official record. When the owner said "I don't care about the cost, just get it done" and then refused to pay the change order three weeks later, nobody wrote that down either.

An AI transcript preserves all of it. Every word. Every statement is indexed, every speaker identified, every timestamp precise to the second. When a dispute arises eighteen months or three years down the line, the transcript sitting on that vendor's server is not a summary of what happened. It is an almost-verbatim record of what people said, minus the facial expressions, gestures, and vocal inflections that made the meaning clear to everyone in the room.

Context vanishes. That is where the danger lives. "We'll figure it out" between colleagues who have worked together for eight years means something entirely different from "we'll figure it out" read aloud by opposing counsel in a deposition. Neither the AI nor the jury will know the difference.

A counterargument deserves a hearing

Better documentation can, in theory, prevent disputes. If every commitment is recorded, every scope discussion is captured, and every agreement has a timestamp, the "he said, she said" that drives many construction claims disappears. Some dispute prevention specialists argue that comprehensive records reduce ambiguity and therefore reduce litigation, period.

That argument has force. Arcadis's Global Construction Disputes Report identified "failure to properly administer the contract" as the most common cause of disputes worldwide. AI meeting notes could constitute better contract administration, if they were reviewed, corrected, and incorporated into project documentation. Almost nobody does that.

Operative word: "if." Nobody does. Contractors adopting AI notetakers are not treating the transcripts as formal project documentation subject to review. They are treating them as a convenience, a way to avoid writing meeting minutes by hand. Transcripts get generated, stored in a vendor's cloud, and forgotten until a lawyer subpoenas them.

What construction professionals should do now

AIA Trust, the American Bar Association, and multiple law firms have converged on similar recommendations, and the advice is blunt. Adopt a written AI governance policy that defines permissible uses and prohibits AI recording in any meeting where legal counsel is present or privileged matters may be discussed. Route all legal questions through counsel rather than through AI platforms, because a single query about potential liability typed into an AI tool in the middle of a meeting can create discoverable evidence that no attorney would have allowed if consulted first. Evaluate every enterprise AI tool for data retention periods, vendor access policies, confidentiality provisions, training-data commitments, and whether the vendor's terms of service grant it ownership of or access to the transcripts generated from your meetings.

For construction specifically, three additional steps matter. First, check the recording consent laws in every jurisdiction where the project operates. An AI notetaker that is legal in a one-party consent state may create criminal liability in a two-party consent state, and a contractor running projects in both types of jurisdictions, or hosting meetings with participants calling in from both, may not even realize the legal line they are crossing every Tuesday at ten o'clock. Second, establish a retention and review protocol for AI-generated transcripts. A transcript that is never reviewed is a liability. Period. A transcript that is reviewed, corrected, and adopted as part of the formal project record is documentation. Third, include AI-specific provisions in contracts and subcontracts, addressing who may use AI recording tools, what happens to the transcripts, and whether they constitute project records subject to retention and discovery obligations. Start there.

None of this is standard practice yet. CCDC 41, the standard Canadian construction contract recently updated for modern technology, remains "largely silent on the specific risks created by autonomous software and algorithmic decision making," according to HCR Law. Standard AIA and ConsensusDocs forms do not address AI meeting transcription at all. Between what these tools are doing and what the contracts contemplate lies a widening gap, and that gap is where exposure accumulates.

Limitations

Published data on AI notetaker adoption rates specifically within construction does not exist, though anecdotal reports from construction attorneys and the marketing materials of AI transcription companies suggest adoption is accelerating fastest among mid-size firms without in-house counsel. Claim frequency and cost data from HKA and IPA reflect projects large enough to engage claims consultants, likely overstating dispute intensity for typical residential work. Heppner is a Canadian ruling and may not bind US courts. Otter.ai's class action has not reached judgment. Most AI transcript case law is emerging, not settled, and construction-specific holdings are essentially nonexistent. This analysis projects from adjacent precedent, not from a body of construction AI case law that does not yet exist.
