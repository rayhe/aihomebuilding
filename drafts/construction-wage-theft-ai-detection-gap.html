The Workers Building Your Home Lose $50 Billion a Year to Stolen Wages. The AI That Catches It Runs on a Phone. Nobody Showed Them.

A framing crew in suburban Dallas works six days a week through July. By noon it reads 98 degrees. A site supervisor logs their hours on a clipboard. At the end of the pay period, each framer gets a check for 40 hours at $18 an hour. Nobody gets overtime. Nobody asks about the extra 12 hours. The crew, mostly Spanish-speaking, mostly working under 1099s they never requested, cashes the checks at a payday lender down the road and comes back Monday.

This is not an unusual week. It is the single most common labor violation in American construction.

Across the United States, wage theft costs workers roughly $50 billion a year, according to estimates from the Economic Policy Institute. That is not a typo. It is more money than Americans lose annually to all robberies, burglaries, and motor vehicle thefts combined. And construction is the industry where it happens most.

The federal government has 611 investigators to police it. That is one investigator for every 278,000 workers. It is the lowest staffing level in 52 years.

Meanwhile, an open-source AI model fine-tuned on 365,393 real Department of Labor enforcement cases can now read a photo of a paystub, spot overtime violations, flag illegal deductions, and explain the results in eleven languages. It runs on a phone. It is free. Almost nobody in construction knows it exists.

The arithmetic of stolen labor

The numbers are hard to absorb because they are enormous and because no single agency tracks them comprehensively.

EPI's seminal study, examining low-wage workers in New York, Chicago, and Los Angeles, found that two out of three experienced at least one pay-related violation in any given week. Average annual loss per worker: $2,634, out of total earnings of $17,616. That is 15 cents on every dollar, gone.

Extrapolated to the nation's 30 million low-wage workers, the annual total reaches $50 billion. Minimum wage violations alone account for $15 billion of that, documented across the ten most populous states. These figures do not include unpaid overtime, stolen tips, illegal deductions, or misclassification losses.

Construction gets hit hardest. A study by The Century Foundation found that up to 2.1 million construction workers are illegally misclassified as independent contractors, costing those workers more than $12 billion a year in lost wages and benefits. In Massachusetts, targeted audits by the state's Department of Unemployment Assistance found that half of construction employers examined were committing illegal labor practices. Not a quarter. Half.

The University of Massachusetts Labor Center, which analyzed those audits, noted that the real rate is almost certainly higher. Small construction employers, the ones most likely to cheat, are also the hardest to audit. Some cannot be located. Others stall investigators until the company quietly dissolves and reopens under a different name next county over.

The enforcement collapse

In 1978, the Department of Labor's Wage and Hour Division employed 1,232 investigators. Sixty million Americans were in the workforce. One investigator for every 49,000 workers.

As of May 2025, according to data analyzed by Rutgers University's Workplace Justice Lab, WHD had 611 investigators protecting 165 million workers. One investigator for every 278,000 workers and 20,000 establishments. Additional resignations accepted through the current administration's deferred resignation program have likely pushed the real number even lower, though no official figure has been released.

In 2019, the DOL cited roughly 8,500 employers for taking about $287 million from workers. Repeat offenders rarely face penalties. Total recovery through all channels that year, including private lawsuits, came to under $1 billion. Against $50 billion in estimated annual theft, that is a recovery rate below two percent.

For perspective: if the IRS collected taxes at that rate, the federal government would shut down in a week.

What AI can already do

PaySnap, an open-source project built by developer Aadarsh Praveen, fine-tuned Google's Gemma 4 language model on the full corpus of 365,393 Department of Labor enforcement cases. What emerged is a multimodal AI that can read a photograph of a paystub, extract the relevant numbers, compare them against federal and state labor law, and tell the worker, in plain language, whether they have been cheated.

A Texas construction worker enters 52 hours at $15 an hour with no overtime shown. PaySnap identifies 12 unpaid overtime hours and calculates $90 owed under FLSA Section 207(a)(1). A New York restaurant worker uploads a paystub in Hindi showing deductions for uniform costs and breakage. PaySnap flags both deductions as illegal and calculates $149 owed.

The model runs in 3.4 gigabytes of memory. It fits on a phone. It works in Spanish, Hindi, Chinese, and eight other languages, which matters enormously in an industry where 30 percent of the workforce was born outside the United States and many workers communicate primarily in a language other than English.

A separate tool called Reclamo, built by the Justicia Lab, helps undocumented construction workers document wage theft and generate legal complaint letters. It walks a non-lawyer advocate through a series of questions, calculates the stolen wages, and produces a formal letter citing state and federal statute. According to the developers, a complaint letter sent to the employer is often enough to recover the missing wages without a full state investigation, which can take a year or longer.

Who actually gets the AI

Insurance companies.

Nomad Data's Doc Chat platform offers AI-powered anomaly detection specifically for construction payroll audits. It can identify payroll-splitting schemes, detect networks of subcontractors sharing addresses and bank accounts, cross-reference certified payroll against actual payments, and flag workers appearing on projects where they are not listed in auditable payroll. Every alert comes with page-level citations from the source documents.

It is sophisticated, well-funded, and built for Special Investigations Unit teams at insurance carriers. It exists to protect insurers from fraudulent premium claims. It does not exist to protect the workers whose wages were stolen to generate those fraudulent premiums.

This asymmetry is not accidental. Insurance companies pay for software. Construction workers, particularly misclassified ones receiving 1099 forms they never agreed to, do not. Economic logic is straightforward and brutal: whoever has the money gets the technology. Whoever is losing the money gets a phone number for the Department of Labor.

That phone number connects to an agency with 611 investigators.

Why this reaches your front door

If you are building a home, renovating a kitchen, or hiring a contractor for a deck, the workforce question is not abstract. It is in your driveway every morning at 7 a.m.

When a contractor misclassifies workers as independent contractors, the cost savings, typically 20 to 30 percent on labor, flow directly into a lower bid. Homeowners see a competitive price. What they do not see: those workers carry no workers' compensation insurance, no unemployment coverage, and often no OSHA protections. If a worker falls from a ladder on your property and has no workers' comp, the homeowner's liability exposure can be significant.

The quality implications are harder to measure but no less real. Underpaid workers with no job security or benefits have little incentive to flag a problem they notice on-site. A joist that looks wrong, a foundation form that shifted, a vapor barrier left unsealed. Reporting it means slowing down. Slowing down means fewer hours. Fewer hours, at a wage that is already being stolen from, means the difference between rent and eviction.

ABC, representing over 23,000 members, has called wage theft "a $50 billion problem" for the $2.7 trillion U.S. construction industry. Their concern is partly about workers and partly about honest contractors. A company that pays its employees legally, provides benefits, carries insurance, and pays payroll taxes cannot compete on price with a company that does none of those things. A race to the bottom does not just harm workers. It drives quality contractors out of residential work entirely.

The DOL's own AI problem

The Department of Labor has also issued guidance warning that employers' own AI tools can create new forms of wage theft. AI-powered timekeeping software that monitors keystrokes, eye movements, or browsing activity to distinguish "active" from "idle" time can incorrectly categorize compensable work as unpaid downtime.

The DOL's bulletin is explicit: an AI program that "incorrectly categorizes time as non-compensable work hours based on its analysis of employee activity, productivity, or performance could result in a failure to pay wages for all hours worked." The agency further noted that productivity monitoring systems are "not determinative of whether an employee is performing 'hours worked' under the FLSA."

In other words, AI in the workplace is being deployed in two directions simultaneously. One direction catches wage theft. One creates it. Construction, where timekeeping is already informal and enforcement is already negligible, sits at the intersection of both.

What would change

The technical components exist. PaySnap's model, trained on every DOL enforcement action in the database, runs locally and works in the languages construction workers actually speak. Reclamo generates the legal documents needed to file a claim. Insurance-grade AI can map entire networks of payroll fraud from a stack of subcontractor documents.

What does not exist is distribution. No state labor department has integrated AI paystub analysis into its intake process. No contractor licensing board requires payroll transparency as a condition of licensure. No home-building platform, from Angi to Houzz to the local building department's permit desk, asks whether the workers on a permitted project are classified correctly and paid legally.

Everything needed to protect workers is free, open-source, and fits on a phone. Workers it could reach earn less than $18,000 a year and cannot afford to lose $2,634 of it. What separates what exists from what is deployed is not technical. It is political, economic, and, if we are being honest about it, moral.

What you can do

If you are a homeowner hiring a contractor for any project over $5,000, ask for a certificate of workers' compensation insurance. Not a promise. A certificate, issued by the insurer, with your project address listed. If the contractor says their workers are all independent subcontractors, ask how many work exclusively for that contractor. Under most state laws, if a worker performs labor exclusively for one company, on that company's schedule, with that company's tools, they are an employee, regardless of what form they signed.

If you are a construction worker and you believe your employer is stealing your wages, the DOL Wage and Hour Division hotline is 1-866-487-9243. It is free and confidential regardless of immigration status. PaySnap, available at paysnap.vercel.app, can analyze your paystub against federal and state law. Reclamo, the Justicia Lab's wage theft documentation tool, can generate the legal complaint letter needed to file a claim.

If you are a general contractor who pays workers legally and carries proper insurance, you already know that you are competing against companies that do none of this. Every industry group that lobbies for deregulation while ignoring the enforcement collapse is making your business harder to sustain. That $12 billion misclassifying employers steal from workers each year doubles as $12 billion in competitive advantage that honest contractors subsidize with thinner margins.

Six hundred and eleven investigators. For 165 million workers. An AI trained on every case they have ever filed runs on a phone. The question is not whether the technology works. The question is who gets to use it.
