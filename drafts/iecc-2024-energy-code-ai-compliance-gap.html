Your Builder Has to Pick From 51 Efficiency Options to Pass Code. The AI That Could Help Hasn't Left the Lab.

By Jake Kowalski | August 4, 2026

Open Section R408 of the 2024 International Energy Conservation Code and start counting. Fifty-one options. Heat pump water heaters, ENERGY STAR windows, smart thermostats, ERV ventilation, solar-ready conduit. Each option earns a different number of points depending on your climate zone. Your builder must pick at least two of them and score a minimum of ten points. Building a house over 5,000 square feet? That minimum jumps to fifteen.

This is just one section of one compliance path in the most complex residential energy code the United States has ever published.

Five states have adopted it. Oklahoma is still building to a code written in 2006.

Three Paths, One Code, Zero Margin for Error

Builders get three ways to prove a home meets the 2024 IECC's energy requirements. A prescriptive path lays out specific R-values, U-factors, and SHGC ratings for every component of the building envelope, then bolts on Section R408's points menu. A simulated performance path runs the entire home through energy modeling software and compares it against a reference design. For the first time since 2009, that simulation can include equipment efficiency trade-offs. An Energy Rating Index path uses HERS scoring against a normalized reference home.

Each path has different rules about what can be traded off, what stays mandatory, and what documentation the building department needs to sign off on. Two of the three paths require hiring a certified HERS rater.

There are roughly 3,000 RESNET-certified raters and auditors serving all of North America. The country had 1.4 million housing starts in 2025.

What $400 Buys You

A HERS rating runs $400 to $800 for a typical production home, according to Stephen Mogowski of Desert Skies Energy in Phoenix. Mogowski works with more than 200 custom home builders in the Phoenix and Scottsdale market. He told Builder Magazine he finds problems with ductwork or insulation in about one of every two homes he tests.

"You cannot believe the problems in modern construction," Mogowski said. "Everything can look like it's installed great, but if you're not doing due diligence, things will get missed."

That inspection involves two site visits: one before drywall goes up, one at final. Raters model the home's energy performance in accredited software pre-construction, recommend modifications based on the builder's targets, then verify as-built reality with blower-door and duct-leakage tests. In Massachusetts, 80 percent of new homes get rated. In Arizona, 63 percent. In Oklahoma, testing is not required at all.

DOE Built an AI. It Looked Up R-Values.

Pacific Northwest National Laboratory received Department of Energy funding through the Building Energy Codes Program to build an AI tool for energy code compliance verification. Led by researcher Hanlong Wan, the project uses GPT-4o and Meta's Llama 3 with a retrieval-augmented generation architecture. Building plans go in. Code compliance checks come out. That is the pitch.

PNNL presented a demo in late 2024 that located R-value requirements for Washington State from ASHRAE 90.1-2019. It is a chatbot. You type a question about a code section. It retrieves the relevant passage from a vector database and generates an answer.

PNNL's own documentation identifies the central challenge: "accurately extracting important data from tables and figures within building energy codes." Energy codes are heavy on tables. R-value tables. U-factor tables. Climate zone lookup tables. Points tables with row-by-column dependencies. Section R408 alone is a matrix of options, point values, and climate zone modifiers that changes meaning depending on which row you read.

PNNL built a second AI tool called BEM-AI. This one is more ambitious. It uses agentic AI with multiple specialized sub-agents for walls, roofs, lighting, and energy modeling software. A "planner" agent breaks down the user's request. An "orchestrator" assigns subtasks. Specialized agents gather data and run simulations through DOE's EnergyPlus software.

BEM-AI successfully modeled energy savings from reducing the window-to-wall ratio on a three-story, 53,000-square-foot commercial office building in Florida.

It works on commercial buildings. In one state. Residential is not on the menu.

What the Market Offers Today

InQI, an AI-native proptech platform, launched its Codes.IQ product in May 2026. Its pitch: a "plan-checker aware of jurisdiction-specific building codes and project zoning." InQI uses what it calls a "multi-LLM consensus architecture" that cross-references outputs from multiple AI models and flags discrepancies between them.

No accuracy data for Codes.IQ has been published. No independent evaluation exists. InQI is live and accepting users, but no peer-reviewed study has tested whether its code interpretations hold up against a certified plans examiner, a HERS rater, or a building department rejection letter.

This is the state of AI in residential energy code compliance: one DOE-funded research prototype that reads tables from ASHRAE 90.1, one agentic bot for commercial buildings in Florida, and one commercial product with no published accuracy numbers. Every builder navigating Section R408's 51-option matrix is doing it with a spreadsheet, a HERS rater, and experience.

Why This Matters to You

If you are building a home in a state that has adopted the 2024 IECC, here is what compliance actually looks like on the ground.

Prescriptive path: Your builder picks from R-value and U-factor tables for your climate zone, meets mandatory air sealing and duct leakage thresholds, then selects at least two measures from Section R408 to hit ten points. Points span nine categories. Some options are worth one point in Climate Zone 3 and four points in Climate Zone 7. Your builder has to read the matrix, not just the measure.

Performance path: An energy modeler runs your design through software like REM/Rate or EnergyGauge and proves the proposed home uses less energy than the code's standard reference design. Since 2024, the model can now factor in HVAC equipment efficiency, which the code had excluded from this path since 2009. A HERS rater verifies results in the field.

ERI path: Your home gets a HERS Index score. Lower is better. A reference home scores 100. A HERS rater runs the numbers and the field tests. This path has allowed equipment efficiency trade-offs since 2015.

All three paths share a set of mandatory requirements that cannot be traded off: air barrier installation, duct sealing, mechanical ventilation, and lighting efficiency among them.

Optimization Without a Solver

Looking up an R-value is not the hard part. Any chatbot can do that now. Here is the hard part: a builder in Climate Zone 4 wants to install R-13 cavity insulation with R-5 continuous insulation instead of R-20 cavity. Prescriptive tables say no. A UA alternative calculation says maybe, if total building envelope heat loss stays below the code threshold. But that calculation depends on window area, door count, foundation type, and whether the attic has a ventilated or unventilated assembly. On top of that, Section R408 points are needed, and the optimal combination depends on which equipment is already spec'd, which climate zone rows apply, and whether the truss manufacturer can accommodate raised-heel detailing for the ceiling insulation depth.

Builders who get it wrong do not just fail inspection. They either over-spec and eat the margin, or under-spec and delay the certificate of occupancy while scrambling for a fix.

An AI that could solve this would need to read code tables correctly, model building envelope physics, price trade-offs against local material and labor costs, and generate a compliance report that a building department would accept. Nobody has built that tool. PNNL's chatbot handles step one. BEM-AI handles step three, for commercial buildings in Florida. Steps two and four do not exist in any AI product available to a residential builder today.

What a Builder Should Do Right Now

Do not wait for AI to catch up to the code.

If your jurisdiction has adopted or is adopting the 2024 IECC, start with the ERI path if you are running more than twenty homes a year. It gives the most trade-off flexibility and your HERS rater handles the compliance documentation. Budget $400 to $800 per home for the rating. If your rater is finding defects in half the homes they test, that $400 is catching problems that would cost ten times as much in callbacks.

If you are a custom builder running fewer than ten homes a year, the prescriptive path is simpler to document, but Section R408 is new territory. Read the points table for your climate zone before you finalize the mechanical spec. A heat pump water heater is worth four points in most zones. That is nearly half the threshold from one equipment swap.

If a vendor tells you their AI can check your plans against the 2024 IECC, ask for the accuracy data. Ask which compliance path it supports. Ask if it generates a report your building department will accept. If those answers are not specific, the tool is not ready.

Right now, the 2024 energy code is real. AI to navigate it is not. Your HERS rater is still the best technology available.
