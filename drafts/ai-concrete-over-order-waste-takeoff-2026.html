Your Foundation Order Includes $1,116 of Concrete Nobody Needs

By Jake Kowalski · October 8, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Pour day. Three trucks lined up on the street, chutes down, the crew moving like they've done this a thousand times because they have.

Your forms hold 62 yards of concrete. You ordered 68. That extra six yards cost you over a thousand dollars, and it bought you something invisible: insurance against running short, which is the oldest product in construction, older than the nail gun, older than the transit, older than the building code itself. Everyone in the business does it. Whether the rest still need to is an open question.

Nevada Ready Mix, citing NRMCA guidance, tells contractors to order an allowance of 4 to 10 percent over plan dimensions for waste, over-excavation, and other causes. That is not a rule of thumb from some foreman. That is the industry's trade association saying, buy more than you need, on purpose, every time, and the waste is not theoretical. Per the NRMCA, roughly 5 to 10 percent of ready-mix deliveries get returned to the plant because of overages or quality issues. Millions of yards of concrete, mixed at a plant, driven across town, then driven back to the same plant to be dumped, which is the most expensive possible outcome for a material that was perfect when it left the gate. Pure waste.

Now look at what the measuring machines can do, because the numbers coming out of this corner are getting hard to argue with, unless you sell fear by the yard, in which case you should probably stop reading. A 2026 benchmark from Structures Insider ran an AI takeoff platform against verified manual measurements on 50 civil drawings and found a gross error of 0.18 percent, with the software reviewing a full plan set in about a minute. Independent research from the University of Kansas civil engineering department, reported by Construction Business Owner, found AI-assisted takeoffs completed in 37 minutes what took a human two hours and 35 minutes, a 76 percent time cut, while holding within a 5 percent error margin with an estimator reviewing the output.

Read that again. The plan takeoff, a job that used to eat an estimator's entire afternoon and still came back as one person's best guess, is now the most accurate part of the pour, which should embarrass an industry that still prices its insurance against the least accurate step. The 4-to-10-percent fudge factor is wider than the measurement error of the tool that creates the number the fudge factor gets applied to. Something in that stack is obsolete. It is the fudge factor.

The Math on a Real Foundation

Take a standard basement: 30 by 50 feet, eight-foot walls eight inches thick, a four-inch slab, footings two feet wide and a foot deep. Run the geometry and you get 31.6 yards of walls, 11.9 yards of footings, 18.5 yards of slab, for a theoretical total of 62 yards.

At 2026 delivered pricing of roughly $180 a yard, that is $11,160 of material. A 10 percent allowance adds 6.2 yards and $1,116 to the ticket. A 4 percent allowance adds 2.5 yards and $447. The difference between the top and bottom of the industry's own recommended range is $669 on a single pour, and nobody on the crew knows which end of the range is correct for your dirt. Nobody measured it. They measured the drawings.

This is where the drone earns its keep, because the one thing the takeoff cannot measure is the ground itself, and the ground is where the concrete actually goes. Subgrade is the biggest liar in the concrete equation: soft spots, over-excavation, a base course that was never quite at grade, all of it drinks concrete the drawings never showed. BarnardHQ's field work shows a drone flying a stockpile or excavation in 22 minutes and returning volumes accurate to 1 to 3 percent, against eyeball estimates that were off by 400 yards, about $8,000 at delivered prices. A subgrade scan before the pour tells you exactly how much extra concrete the ground will swallow, which turns the least honest number on the ticket into the most defensible one, and suddenly the foreman who wanted 10 percent has to argue against a point cloud. Then the allowance is not a guess anymore: it is a number with a picture behind it, a survey-grade point cloud instead of a foreman's shrug, and nobody has ever successfully argued with a point cloud, which is the real reason the drone matters: not the accuracy, but the end of the argument.

On a 20-home subdivision, the per-pour savings of tightening from 10 percent to 4 percent with verified measurements runs about $13,000 across the project, which is not life-changing money until you remember it costs one drone flight and one takeoff run to unlock, the cheapest margin improvement most residential GCs will find all year. That is real money for the price of a drone flight and a takeoff run.

Why the Habit Survives

The allowance is not pure waste, and anyone who says otherwise has never watched a crew stand idle at full day-rate while the plant scrambles a cleanup truck to cover the last three yards of a wall. Running short on a foundation pour is a nightmare scenario: the truck leaves, the crew stands around at full day-rate, a cold joint forms in your wall, and in the worst case the monolithic pour you specified gets rejected. Nevada Ready Mix is not being generous when it recommends 4 to 10 percent; it is pricing fear, and the fear is legitimate. Subgrade conditions vary by the hour, and rain softens the base overnight, so forms that measured true on Monday can lie by Wednesday. Forms deflect. The fudge factor covers a dozen small dishonesties between the drawing and the hole in the ground.

There is also the plant's side of the ledger. Returned concrete is the producer's problem: disposal, washing out drums, the diesel burned hauling your overage back to the yard, which means the plant quietly shares your incentive to get the order right, a fact worth remembering the next time you negotiate what a returned yard actually costs. Volumetric mixers, which batch on site and produce only what the forms take, meter within plus or minus 1 percent and eliminate the return trip entirely. But you do not rent one for a single-family foundation, and the economics still favor contractors big enough to keep a volumetric truck busy, which means the best technology for eliminating concrete waste is priced out of the exact projects that waste the most concrete per yard.

Objections, at Full Strength

Now the steelman. A skeptic can fairly say the takeoff is not the problem. AI takeoff measures drawings, and drawings are already the accurate part, the one thing on the whole job that sits still long enough to be measured perfectly. The concrete disappears into subgrade variation, form deflection, and spillage, none of which a takeoff algorithm can see. The Structures Insider 0.18 percent figure is on drainage quantities from plans, not on as-built concrete placed in a muddy excavation. Extrapolating plan-measurement accuracy to poured-concrete savings is the kind of category error a vendor white paper makes, and I just did the arithmetic on top of it, so hold that against me.

And there is a second, harder objection, because the allowance also covers human imperfection at the plant and on the crew, and no software fixes either. Batch weights vary within ASTM C94 tolerances, a driver adds water on site and changes the yield, and neither of those is a takeoff problem. The 4-to-10 percent band is not just measurement insurance; it is schedule insurance, and the cost of a short pour dwarfs the cost of six returned yards, and anyone who has paid for both in the same month will tell you the comparison is not close. Any GC who trims the allowance to the bone and gets burned once will order 10 percent for the rest of his career, which is how an industry ends up with a fudge factor that outlives the measurement problem it was invented to cover. The habit has a memory. That memory is a cold joint.

What This Analysis Does Not Prove

The 62-yard scenario is a calculated example, not a measured project. Real over-pour depends on soil, crew discipline, and formwork quality, and I have no independent study quantifying how much of the allowance AI takeoff actually removes on residential foundations; the $669 savings figure is an estimate built from the industry's own range, disclosed as such. Concrete pricing is regional: the $180 average is national for 2026, while a Denver supplier quotes $150 a yard for 4,000 psi mix, so get local quotes, then run this same math with your own numbers, because the whole argument survives contact with any zip code in the country. The NRMCA 5-to-10 percent returned figure mixes quality rejections with pure overages. And the University of Kansas study tested an estimator working with AI, not AI running alone; remove the human and the accuracy claims have no evidence behind them.

What to Do on Your Next Pour

Do not order exactly 62 yards. That is the one lesson this article must not teach. But you can stop guessing across a $669 band. Here is the new playbook, priced in minutes instead of yards, because the measurements are cheap now and the concrete is not.

Shoot the subgrade with a rotary laser or a drone scan before you call in the order, because ten minutes of measurement on Tuesday beats an argument with the batch plant about who pays for the returned truck on Friday. That one step converts half the allowance from fear to measurement, and fear was always the most expensive line item on the ticket, the one nobody itemizes and everybody pays. Re-measure the forms the morning of the pour, not the week before. Lumber moves. For the last two trucks, have someone do the remaining-volume math while concrete is still in the drum, the Nevada Ready Mix guidance says exactly this, and adjust the order mid-pour, because the guidance is older than most of the crew and it still works, which tells you something about how little the fundamentals have changed while everything else got software. If your volume is big enough, price a volumetric mixer against the ready-mix ticket and see which number is smaller, because if the volumetric wins, you have just deleted the entire return-waste problem instead of arguing about its size. And ask the plant what a returned yard actually costs you versus what a short load costs you, because most contractors have never priced both sides of that trade, and the answer determines whether your allowance should sit at 4 percent or 10.

The allowance is not going away, but it used to be the only tool you had. Now it is a choice, priced to the yard, and the people still ordering at the top of the range are paying for a measurement they could get for the price of a drone flight.
