Your Induction Range Will Trip the Breaker the Code Requires. Massachusetts Says Take It Out.

By Catherine Chen · September 26, 2026
← Back to articles

A Seattle-area electrician wired a kitchen this spring, put a 50-amp GFCI breaker on the new Miele induction range as the code demanded, and watched the breaker trip. He called Miele support. Miele told him the range is not compatible with a GFCI breaker. He asked a local appliance installer what everyone else does about it. Everyone removes the GFCI breaker after the final inspection, the installer told him. That is the entire 2023 National Electrical Code GFCI expansion, rendered in one kitchen: the code requires a device, the manufacturer says the device cannot work with the product, and the trade has settled on a workaround that is a code violation.

I should be precise about who is mad at whom, because the chain of blame here is unusually legible. In the 2023 edition of NFPA 70, Section 210.8 did three things at once. It expanded GFCI protection to all 125-volt through 250-volt receptacles in a dozen dwelling locations, which means every receptacle in a kitchen, not just the ones serving countertops: the fridge outlet, the disposal, the microwave, the island. It pulled the appliance list from Section 422.5 into 210.8(D), so electric ranges, wall ovens, cooktops, clothes dryers, and microwave ovens now need GFCI on their branch circuits or outlets whether they are cord-and-plug or hardwired. And in new Section 210.8(F), it required GFCI on all outdoor outlets supplied by single-phase branch circuits of 50 amps or less, which reaches hardwired outdoor equipment, with one exception: listed HVAC equipment got a carve-out that expires September 1, 2026. That was twenty-five days before this article published. Read that again and notice the calendar.

Physics the panel never voted on

Here is the part the code panel did not adjudicate, because it was never submitted to them in a form they could adjudicate. Per the Association of Home Appliance Manufacturers, the GFCI was engineered around 60-hertz current. When the device entered the code in the 1970s, residential loads ran on 60-hertz sine waves, and the GFCI's trip logic measures imbalance at that frequency. Modern appliances do not.

LED drivers, switched-mode power supplies, electronically commutated motors, and variable-frequency drives all put current on the circuit at frequencies other than 60 hertz, partly because federal and state energy-efficiency mandates pushed manufacturers toward exactly these components. No requirements for above-60-hertz behavior exist in the GFCI product standard, UL 943. So a range can leak a small, entirely safe amount of high-frequency current and the breaker trips anyway, because the breaker was never taught to tell the difference. AHAM's phrasing is blunt: even minimal, safe levels of high-frequency leakage current will trip a GFCI and disable critical appliances. And the code now requires this device on the most power-electronics-dense appliance in the house, with compatibility testing that never happened, because the code proposal never went to the appliance safety standard it affects. UL 858 governs electric ranges. That proposal never went near it.

There is a UL 943 task group working on modernizing the standard. That sentence should comfort you less than it sounds. Standards work moves on a multi-year clock, and the code is already adopted, enforceable, and expiring its own exceptions.

Counting the premium

Let us price it, because the trade never trusts a code article that does not come with a number. In 2019, the National Association of Home Builders priced the 2023 GFCI expansion at $272 for a home with two 240-volt appliances, an electric range and an electric dryer, using 2019 device pricing, and NAHB's own document notes that GFCI device costs rose substantially after that calculation. A two-pole 30- to 50-amp GFCI breaker currently retails around $90 to $130 against $8 to $12 for a standard breaker of the same frame.

Run the archetype: a new 2,400-square-foot all-electric home in a 2023 jurisdiction needs GFCI breakers for the range circuit and the dryer circuit, dual-function protection where AFCI also applies, plus GFCI protection on every remaining kitchen receptacle, the laundry, the bathrooms, the garage, and the outdoor outlets, and under 210.8(F) the outdoor heat pump or condenser now joins the list as of this month. That puts the device premium somewhere between $400 and $700 per home at retail, before the callbacks. Each nuisance-trip service call runs $150 to $250, and on the Mike Holt trade forums the standard advice for a tripping range is now to test the breaker, confirm it is the GFCI and not a ground fault, and then have the removal conversation with the customer in low tones. Multiply the device premium across housing starts: roughly 40 percent of annual U.S. starts land in the 19 states that have adopted the 2023 NEC in some form, which puts the national premium in the $250 million to $400 million per year range, concentrated almost entirely on circuits where the pre-vote compatibility evidence consisted of an AHAM white paper the panel did not read. That is the cost of a rule written for 1970s electricity and enforced on 2026 appliances.

Patchwork as policy

No national code is ever really national, and this one is fracturing on contact with the field. Massachusetts finalized code language that allows a GFCI to be removed when nuisance tripping occurs on the new 210.8 requirements, a fact AHAM reported directly to the Consumer Product Safety Commission in a March 2023 meeting where CPSC staff supported a UL proposal for a GFCI interoperability test. Ohio went the other way and neutered the section before it could bite: the state dropped the 250-volt receptacle requirement, lets garage door openers sit on a non-GFCI single receptacle, and allows sump pumps on non-GFCI circuits so long as a GFCI receptacle sits within six inches. Similar carve-outs have surfaced in Iowa, Oregon, South Dakota, and Utah, where NAHB reports amendments trimming the appliance requirements. Meanwhile the District of Columbia is still enforcing the 2014 NEC, which means the same range that trips a breaker in Boston passes inspection in Washington with a standard breaker and no argument. As of August 2026, nineteen states had adopted the 2023 edition in some form, six had already moved to the 2026 edition, and the rest sat on 2017, 2020, or local adoption, which means the largest appliance manufacturers in the world are designing products for a protection requirement that applies in roughly a third of the country and is being amended into irrelevance in several of the states that adopted it. Call it a suggestion with enforcement.

And then there is the September 1 hinge. Time-limited from the start, the 210.8(F) HVAC exception was a truce between the code panel and the equipment manufacturers while compatibility work continued. It expired three and a half weeks ago. Every outdoor condenser and heat pump installed in a 2023 jurisdiction from this month forward legally requires GFCI protection, and variable-frequency-drive heat pumps are among the worst documented nuisance-trip offenders in AHAM's field data. Electricians on the trade forums are already pricing the warranty implications: a GFCI disconnect feeding a condenser for the life of a five-year equipment warranty, in weather, outdoors. Some jurisdictions will amend around it the way Ohio did. Jurisdictions that do not amend will learn what the range installers already know.

Steel-manning the code

None of this changes what the GFCI has accomplished, and the record deserves its full weight. It entered the code in the 1970s, and since then residential electrocutions have fallen 83 percent according to the Electrical Safety Foundation International. CPSC's own fact sheet says GFCI protection could prevent more than two-thirds of the roughly 300 electrocutions still occurring in and around American homes each year. That is the most successful life-safety record of any residential electrical device ever mandated, and every expansion of the requirement has followed the same script: the industry predicts cost and chaos, the devices go in, and the deaths happen on the circuits that were left unprotected. That logic now extends, in the 2023 edition, to the circuits where people actually touch appliances with wet hands, which is the entire kitchen, and the nuisance-tripping problem is arguably a standards failure rather than a code failure. If UL 943 is updated to handle high-frequency leakage the way the task group intends, the tripping stops and the protection stays, which is the outcome everyone claims to want. Removing GFCIs after inspection, the trade's current workaround, voids the permit's protection, transfers the liability to the homeowner, and ensures the next buyer inherits an unprotected kitchen with no way to know. Massachusetts's removal allowance has the same flaw in formal dress: once the device comes off, nobody puts it back on.

Catherine's compliance checklist for anyone building, buying, or remodeling in a 2023 state
  1. Before you buy the range, ask the manufacturer one question in writing: is this model compatible with a Class A GFCI breaker? Get the answer in the quote. A $10,000 induction range that the maker will not certify for GFCI is a $10,000 argument with your inspector.
  2. Price the GFCI premium into the electrical bid, not the contingency. Two 240-volt GFCI breakers plus the kitchen, laundry, garage, and outdoor receptacles run $400 to $700 in device cost alone at current retail, and the bid should show it as its own line.
  3. Check whether your jurisdiction amended 210.8 before you argue with the inspector. Ohio, Massachusetts, Iowa, Oregon, South Dakota, and Utah have all carved pieces out. Inspectors enforce the amended code, not the model code.
  4. If you are on the 2020 or 2017 NEC, know that the heat-pump and range requirements are coming for you on the next adoption cycle. Already published, the 2026 NEC has six adopting states. Budget and specify accordingly.
  5. Never remove a GFCI breaker after final inspection to stop nuisance tripping. It is a code violation, it shifts liability onto you, and it voids the protection the permit was supposed to buy. Document the trip, confirm it is nuisance and not a ground fault, and push the manufacturer and the AHJ for the amended path your state actually offers.
  6. Test every GFCI monthly. Your kitchen's protector is a mechanical device with a finite life, and the nuisance-tripping debate has trained everyone to think of it as the enemy instead of the thing standing between a wet hand and 240 volts.

What this article did not prove

NAHB's 2019 figure of $272 for two 240-volt appliances anchors the cost arithmetic, adjusted with September 2026 retail pricing for two-pole GFCI breakers from major manufacturers; prices move by brand and distributor, and the national $250 million to $400 million estimate assumes the 19-state adoption footprint reported by EC&M and NFPA in August 2026, which counts partial and local adoption in ways that shift the denominator. Nuisance-trip rates by appliance model are anecdotal: forum reports, AHAM's white paper, and the CPSC meeting log. No controlled study of trip rates across range brands exists in the public record, and Miele's incompatibility statement comes from a customer-service call relayed on a trade forum, not a published technical bulletin. Adoption status is a moving target; the IAEI table I used was current as of late September 2026, and any single state's status should be verified against NFPA's official adoption map before you rely on it. AHAM's is the claim that the 210.8 proposal was never submitted to UL 858, reported through NAHB's amendments document and AHAM's own nuisance-tripping page; the code panel's substantiation record is public and readers can judge it. Finally, nothing here is legal advice and none of it overrides your authority having jurisdiction. Until the appeals board says otherwise, the inspector with the red tag is always right.
