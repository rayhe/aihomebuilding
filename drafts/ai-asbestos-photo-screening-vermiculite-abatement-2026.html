Your Flip's Attic Is Full of Gray Pebbles. Pulling Them Out Costs $12,000, and an AI Spotted Them From a Photo.

By Jake Kowalski, September 2, 2026, Construction Technology

Demo day, 7:15 a.m. Your guy pops the attic hatch on the 1962 ranch you just closed on, shines his light across the joists, and goes quiet. Between every joist lies a six-inch bed of gray pebbles. They look like kitty litter, and they glitter a little where the beam hits them.

He climbs down, and the word he uses is not insulation.

Vermiculite.

What follows is the most expensive silence in residential renovation, because the pebbles are not a material problem. They are a math problem wearing a material costume, and the math only gets worse the longer nobody photographed them.

The pebbles that eat your contingency

Vermiculite is a mineral that puffs up like popcorn when heated, and from the 1940s through the 1990s, mountains of it were poured into American attics as insulation under the brand name Zonolite. Most of it came from a mine in Libby, Montana, where the vermiculite shared the ground with asbestos, so the insulation that landed in millions of attics carried asbestos fibers with it. EPA guidance on the stuff is blunt: assume vermiculite contains asbestos, and do not disturb it. It is friable, meaning it crumbles into airborne fibers with almost no provocation, which is exactly what a demolition crew provides.

Here is the part that ruins budgets: ripping out ordinary attic insulation costs $1 to $2 per square foot, so a 1,000-square-foot attic runs $1,000 to $2,000 and your flip budget absorbs it without a flinch, according to 2026 pricing data from Angi. Vermiculite abatement, with containment, negative air, licensed crews, and hazardous disposal, typically runs $7,000 to $12,000, and complex attics push $10,000 to $15,000. Same attic. Same square footage. Yet one costs $1,500 and the other costs $12,000: an 8x multiple that announces itself on the morning your crew is already mobilized, which is when the schedule dies too, because nobody demos around a containment zone.

Somewhere on page 14 of the 40-page inspection report, the inspector noted "vermiculite-like material," everyone skimmed past it toward the foundation section, and now the number is real.

What the camera sees

This is where the machines come in, and the honest version of the story is better than the hyped one. Researchers at the Universitat Oberta de Catalunya trained convolutional neural networks on 2,244 aerial photographs of rooftops, half with confirmed asbestos-cement roofing and half without, and the best model classified new roofs at 92 percent accuracy using nothing but ordinary RGB images, no hyperspectral gear, with heatmaps showing which roof regions drove each call. Published in Remote Sensing in 2024, the work spun out into a company called DetectA that sells the screening to municipalities facing asbestos survey deadlines. Separately, a Griffith University team in Australia hit 94 percent precision detecting asbestos-cement roofs on houses with a Mask R-CNN model, and framed the tool exactly right: a way to build the register of suspect buildings so removal can be planned instead of discovered.

Now the brake pedal, because I am not going to sell you a phone app that does not exist yet. Those studies classify roofs from the air, and nobody has published a peer-reviewed study validating a consumer app pointed at attic insulation, and no photo of anything has ever confirmed asbestos, because confirmation means a lab, polarized light microscopy, a technician, and a chain of custody. Anyone selling you certainty from a JPEG is selling something else.

But the triage logic holds, and it holds specifically for vermiculite because vermiculite is the easiest visual target in the entire asbestos catalog. Asbestos pipe wrap looks like a dirty cast. Nine-inch floor tile looks like nine-inch floor tile. Vermiculite looks like glittering kitty litter poured between joists, and there is almost nothing else in a house that looks like that. A classifier does not need to beat a lab. It needs to beat the flipper who never opened the hatch, and that bar is on the floor.

The math nobody does before demo day

Run the decision tree with real numbers. A lab test runs $250 to $850 and the Trust tests free, against a photo screen that costs effectively zero, so forget whether the photo replaces the lab and ask instead whether a free thirty-second screen gets you to spend the $250 test before demo day instead of discovering the $12,000 problem while the crew is already mobilized.

Then the money almost nobody claims: funded by the WR Grace class-action settlement, the Zonolite Attic Insulation Trust reimburses 55 percent of removal and reinsulation costs up to a CPI-adjusted cap currently set at $5,397.48, and it has been paying since 2014 with at least a 20-year lifespan. Do the arithmetic on the $12,000 nightmare: 55 percent is $6,600, the cap binds, and your net is about $6,603. On an $8,000 job the cap does not bind, 55 percent is $4,400, and your net is $3,600. That is the difference between a project-killer and a bad week, and it is available only if the vermiculite is actually the Zonolite brand, which is exactly what the free test establishes, and only if you open the claim before the abatement, because the Trust wants before-and-after photos, the contractor invoice, and proof of payment, in that order.

Nobody calculated this stack for you: free photo screen, then free brand test, then a claim worth up to $5,397, against a surprise that otherwise lands at $12,000 with a stopped schedule. Note the AI never saved the $12,000. Sequencing did. That photo just started the sequence early enough to matter.

The part where I pump the brakes

Strongest case against everything above, stated straight. A 92 percent accurate roof classifier still misses 8 percent, and a false negative on friable asbestos is a lung event, not a rounding error, which is why every legitimate use of this technology ends at a lab, not at a phone screen. An experienced inspector's eye is already free and already in your attic during the inspection contingency, so the marginal value of the algorithm is not beating the pro on one house, it is screening fifty houses in a portfolio, or giving a buyer the nudge to read page 14 of the report. Federal asbestos demolition rules exclude houses with four or fewer units, so there is no federal inspector coming to save you on a single-family flip; the pressure is state rules, lender requirements, and the disclosure form you sign at sale, where "vermiculite-like material" becomes the buyer's $12,000 negotiating club. And the Trust pays only for Zonolite-brand material with proof, which means off-brand vermiculite gets you zero, a fact worth knowing before you budget the reimbursement.

One for the graveyard file this publication keeps: construction tech is littered with tools that worked in the paper and died on the job site, and Katerra burned $2 billion proving it. Nobody has shipped a validated consumer asbestos-photo product, and the first one that ships will face the liability question of what happens when it misses. Bet on triage workflows run by professionals, not on an app store miracle.

What this analysis did not prove

The cost figures are national ranges from trade press, not binding quotes, and abatement in high-cost metros runs above these numbers. Those 92 and 94 percent accuracy figures come from roof-classification studies on aerial imagery, and I extrapolated the triage argument to phone photos of attics, which no published study has validated. That $5,397.48 cap is CPI-adjusted and the trustee can reduce payment percentages during cash-flow shortfalls, so treat the cap as current, not guaranteed. I did not verify per-state disclosure rules, which vary, or per-carrier insurance treatment of vermiculite, which most homeowner policies exclude as a maintenance issue rather than sudden damage.

Should you do this

If you buy, flip, or renovate houses built before 1990: photograph the attic insulation during the inspection contingency, every time, and run it past any available classifier or just a knowledgeable inspector before the contingency expires. If anything looks like gray glittering pebbles, stop and get the lab test, which costs $250 to $850, or send a sample to the Zonolite Trust, which tests for brand identification free. If it is Zonolite, open the Trust claim before anyone touches the attic, then hire the licensed abatement crew, then submit the before-and-after photos and invoices for up to $5,397 back.

If you are a GC: put "attic insulation photo" on the pre-demo checklist next to the asbestos survey line item, because the $12,000 surprise has never once improved a client relationship, and the thirty-second photo is the cheapest insurance on the job. Those pebbles are not going anywhere; your contingency, on the other hand, is.
