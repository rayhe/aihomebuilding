Your Builder Bid $125,000 an Acre. The Data Center Bid $4.4 Million.

By Frank DeLuca, September 13, 2026, Project Management & Operations

Somewhere in Loudoun County, Virginia, a developer recently offered $4.4 million for a single acre of land. Loudoun County's median land price in 2025 was $125,000 an acre. That is 35 times the going rate, and the buyer was not building homes.

Twenty years of watching land deals go sideways has taught me that every market has a moment when the numbers stop being about the product and start being about something else entirely, and when a single buyer can pay nine times what your entire industry can pay, the market is no longer pricing land at all, it is pricing proximity to power, water, and fiber, inputs no homebuyer ever asked to compete for. This is that moment for residential land in America. Behind that $4.4 million check stood a buyer assembling a site for a data center, and the homebuilder who might have put forty starter homes on that ground never had a chance to compete.

Land purchases for future data centers hit about $6 billion in the first half of 2026, up 79 percent from the year before, according to commercial real estate firm Avison Young via Inman, and that figure, which counts only the fee-simple acquisitions and reported option agreements captured in six months, already ranked data centers ahead of every traditional commercial category except apartments. That made data centers the second-largest buyers of US development land, trailing only apartment developers and outranking industrial, office, retail, and mixed-use combined. The National Association of Home Builders, which does not traffic in understatement, put it this way in July: "Homebuilders cannot bid in that market, because a builder's land budget is capped by what homebuyers can afford. A data center operator faces no such constraint. The result is not more expensive homes on that parcel. It is no homes at all." Nobody outbids that.

The arithmetic of zero

Let me walk the Loudoun numbers the way a land acquisition manager would, because the NAHB quote sounds like rhetoric until you run it.

A production builder targeting a $600,000 home typically needs the finished lot to land around 20 percent of the delivered price, because vertical construction cost, financing carry, the developer's required margin, and the buyer's mortgage qualification all stack on top of the dirt, leaving land as the one input that cannot stretch when the bid runs hot. That is $120,000 per lot, which means that at four homes to the acre, a standard suburban greenfield density, the builder can pay roughly $480,000 an acre for entitled land and still make the pro forma work: four hundred eighty thousand dollars, and not a dollar more. That $4.4 million bid was more than nine times the maximum a builder can pay.

At that bid, the per-home-site land cost works out to $1.1 million per potential front door. Nobody finances that, nobody appraises that, and the residential yield on the parcel is zero. That is a calculation, not an opinion, so do the division yourself, because I already did.

To scale it, consider North Las Vegas, where VanTrust Real Estate sold nearly 205 acres in the Apex Industrial Park to Novva Data Centers for $181 million in 2025, more than $880,000 an acre, per the NAHB's July brief. At five homes to the acre, that single transaction represents roughly 1,025 homes that will never be built on that ground. A thousand front doors, gone, and you can count every one of them. Along US Route 67 in Texas, land that sold for $20,000 to $40,000 an acre a few years ago was trading above $350,000 an acre by 2026. A local residential land developer told HousingWire the plain truth: "There's no possible way you can make those numbers work." He is right.

The appraisal problem nobody is pricing

Here is the part that worries me more than the headline bids, because it poisons deals the data centers never touch.

In Texas, HousingWire reports a data center buyer paid $500,000 a year for a four-year option on a property, just to keep the land off the market while it decided. Another tract drew a $16 million cash offer that local brokers say would have been $3 million without the data center in the picture. Those outlier sales enter the county comparable databases that appraisers use to value every nearby parcel, including the ones zoned residential that no server farm wants.

Appraisals are backward-looking machines. Feed them $360,000-an-acre comps in a market where agricultural land traded at $25,000, and the residential parcel next door appraises at a number no builder's pro forma can support. Deals like that die in underwriting. Nobody in the trade press has connected the option-payment practice to residential appraisal failure, but every land man I know has a story about a comp that made no sense until someone explained the data center down the road.

First-order damage is the parcel the data center buys. Second-order damage is every parcel around it that becomes unbuildable because the comps went insane. Contagion, not competition, as any appraiser will tell you.

It is not just the land

The same campuses then tax the infrastructure the homes would have used. In Virginia, the State Corporation Commission approved Dominion Energy base rate increases of $11.24 a month for the average residential customer in January 2026, part of a grid buildout that data center demand is widely seen as driving, per Inman, while the utilities' own load forecasts, filed in dockets most homebuyers will never read, attribute the bulk of the coming decade's capacity additions to large-load customers that barely existed in the planning models five years ago. Virginia's legislature passed SB 253, a cost-shift provision the SCC estimated would have cut residential bills by about $5.52 a month, and it was signed in modified form. Read that number twice, because it tells you how much of the grid expansion homeowners were subsidizing.

New Jersey went further, enacting a law requiring large data centers to cover at least 85 percent of their projected power costs for a decade. Senator Mark Warner of Virginia is co-sponsoring federal legislation tying data center tax breaks to energy and water disclosure. These are not anti-growth gestures; they are the first attempts to make the heaviest users of the grid pay for the grid, and it is about time.

The backlash is now a line item

None of this is happening quietly. Investment bank Mizuho reported on September 1 that nine states have pending moratoriums on new data center development. Governor Hochul issued a one-year statewide moratorium on new hyperscale facilities in New York in July, the first of its kind in the nation. Arizona's budget imposed a three-year moratorium on new data center tax incentives effective July 1. Illinois and Ohio both paused new incentive agreements administratively. In Cass County, Nebraska, officials approved a 12-month moratorium affecting a 1,300-plus-acre site. In Saline Township, Michigan, local officials received death threats over a multibillion-dollar Oracle and OpenAI project called "The Barn," and several resigned.

Wells Fargo's analysts called the politics manageable for now and warned that a shift to statewide scale could pose a material risk to future growth, which is the kind of sentence that sounds reassuring until you notice that statewide scale is exactly where the nine pending moratoriums, the New York precedent, and the Arizona budget language are all pointing. Translation: the industry knows the permitting environment is the binding constraint now, not the technology.

For a builder, that sentence cuts both ways. Moratoriums slow the land grab in your market. They also signal that entitled, grid-connected land is about to get scarcer and more political everywhere, which is the opposite of a buying opportunity.

The other side of the ledger

Now the strongest case against everything I just wrote, stated at full strength, because a land man who only hears one side of a deal gets eaten.

Data centers are close to the ideal commercial neighbor from a county budget office's perspective. They pay enormous property taxes and demand almost nothing in return: no schoolchildren, minimal traffic, few emergency calls, no parkland dedication. A lot of the ground changing hands was farmland, not entitled residential, and it was never going to become starter homes without rezoning, water, and sewer extensions the county could not finance. Blaming the server farm lets three decades of exclusionary zoning off the hook. First and always, the housing shortage is an entitlement failure. And moratoriums are theater at the state line. Pause Loudoun and the campus goes to Frederick County, or to Ohio, or to Texas, taking the tax base with it.

Price-effect research is genuinely mixed: a University of Rochester study found data center development has little measurable effect on nearby home prices, while a George Mason University-led study found new centers slowed local home-price growth. Both cannot be the whole story, which usually means the truth is local and nobody has measured it properly yet, though somebody should.

Fair points, all of them, though they do not change the arithmetic in the first section. A builder who cannot pay nine times his maximum land budget does not lose a philosophical debate; he loses the parcel, end of story, almost.

What this analysis did not prove

Honest accounting before the close. That $4.4 million Loudoun figure is a reported offer, not a recorded sale; treat it as directional, though the direction is consistent with every other data point. Avison Young's $6 billion covers all data center land purchases in H1 2026, and the share that was residentially-zoned or residentially-suitable cannot be determined from public data, so the true homes-not-built figure is smaller than the gross acreage implies and still large. My density assumptions of four to five homes per acre are standard suburban greenfield math; actual entitled density varies by jurisdiction and would move the per-parcel numbers. My appraisal-contamination mechanism is reasoned from the HousingWire option-payment reporting, not from a statistical study of comp databases; consider it analysis, flagged as such, not a finding. The mechanism is real even if the measurement is mine. And I have not verified CBRE's $8 million-per-acre figure for grid-ready Northern Virginia sites against primary documents.

The call

If you are a builder or developer working markets anywhere near the data center corridors, the move is unglamorous and urgent. Audit your land pipeline against comp integrity, not just price: pull the last twelve months of comparable sales in every submarket where you hold options and flag any comp tied to a data center buyer or option payment, because your appraiser will not flag it for you. Underwrite utility cost escalation into your HOA and homeowner disclosures now; the Dominion and New Jersey precedents say ratepayer protection is coming unevenly and late. And track the moratorium states as deal risk, not just headlines: a 12-month pause in Cass County is a 12-month pause on your entitlement timeline if your parcel sits in the blast radius.

If you are buying a home near a data center market, ask your builder where their next three communities are entitled, not just the one you are touring. A builder with no answer is a builder with no land pipeline, and you will feel that in delays and price increases long before anyone mentions a server farm.

A new buyer has entered the land market with no budget constraint and no interest in your product. Plan accordingly. That is the entire memo. It took $6 billion in six months to make it obvious. Sleep on that number, then check your land pipeline.
