Your Buried Oil Tank Is Exempt From Federal Law. Your $50,000 Cleanup Isn't.

By Catherine Chen, September 13, 2026, Policy & Regulation

A New Jersey environmental firm ran a ground-penetrating radar unit over the yard of a house built in 1978. It had sold in 2016 with no tank sweep, no disclosure, no paperwork suggesting anything was buried, and the radar found a tank anyway.

This happens constantly: one firm with 26 years in the business reports that roughly 75 percent of its tank sweeps turn up evidence of prior oil heat. Natural gas did not become the default until the 1970s, which means most houses built before then burned oil, and when they converted, the tank often stayed exactly where it was: underground, unmapped, quietly corroding.

Federal regulators know about these tanks and chose not to regulate them.

The 1988 carve-out

When Congress added Subtitle I to the Resource Conservation and Recovery Act in 1984 and the EPA wrote the underground storage tank rules in 1988, the agency drew a bright line in 40 CFR 280.12: a tank storing heating oil for consumptive use on the premises where stored is not, legally speaking, an underground storage tank at all. Its own FAQ states it plainly: excluded from federal UST regulations, so check with your state.

That last sentence did a lot of work, creating a patchwork where the same steel tank is a regulated facility in one state and a private surprise in another, and the surprise is priced accordingly.

Three states, three liability regimes

New York declined the federal invitation to look away. Its petroleum bulk storage rules start at 110 gallons underground, recognize none of the federal exemptions, and cover aboveground tanks too, per the state DEC. A 275-gallon basement tank that is invisible to Washington is fully visible to Albany.

New Jersey took a different route: strict liability plus a fund. Under the state Spill Compensation and Control Act, the residential exemption evaporates the moment a buyer is permitted to test the tank, and a positive test must be reported to the state on pain of penalties, as the CPA Journal's legal analysis lays out. New Jersey runs a Petroleum UST Remediation Fund for unregulated tanks with published flat rates: $2,000 to pull a 550-gallon tank, $2,500 for a 1,000-gallon one, per the NJDEP cost guide. It pays for removal, not for the six-figure remediation that follows a bad leak.

As for how many bad leaks turn up, New Jersey's own performance reports count residential unregulated heating oil tank cases closed with No Further Action letters: 272 in fiscal 2019, 188 in fiscal 2024, per NJDEP's published KPIs. Those are completions, not discoveries; nobody counts the tanks still in the ground.

Massachusetts shows what the uninsured version looks like. About 110 homeowners a year report oil leaks to the state DEP, out of roughly 650,000 oil-heated homes, and cleanup of a simple release runs $20,000 to $50,000 while a complex one, oil under the foundation and into groundwater, can exceed $500,000, according to environmental attorney Susan Crane interviewed by MassLive. One of her clients, a man in his eighties on a fixed income, had a hundred-gallon basement spill, spent $60,000, and was nowhere near finished. Its state Senate passed a bill in 2022 that would have forced insurers to automatically include fuel-oil spill coverage in homeowner policies. Most buyers discover their policy excludes contamination only after the leak.

Pennsylvania's DEP puts the top end at $50,000 for a spill cleanup and warns it may not be covered by homeowner's insurance at all, in a state where a quarter of homes heat with oil (PA DEP advisory). Oregon's DEQ figures run $1,000 to $2,000 to decommission a tank that never leaked, up to $10,000 once soil is contaminated, past $30,000 once groundwater is involved, with more than 40,000 tanks decommissioned since the late 1980s (Oregon DEQ via the Register-Guard).

Notice the shape of the cost curve: finding the tank is cheap, removing an intact tank is cheap, and everything after a leak is expensive, with the price jumping an order of magnitude at each stage, soil, then groundwater, then foundation. Each stage multiplies the bill.

The $400 scan and the $30,000 antenna

Answering the regulatory gap is the tank sweep, and it is worth understanding what you are actually buying. A basic sweep walks the property with a metal detector. A serious one uses ground-penetrating radar, a unit that costs tens of thousands of dollars and renders an actual image of what is buried, then uses the metal detector only to confirm the anomaly is metallic. This distinction matters because a yard is full of metal that is not a tank: pipes, rebar, old fencing, iron sand, buried trash. One firm's case files include a tank a metal-detector-only sweep mislocated under a concrete driveway; the mislocation cost the client $1,200 in unnecessary concrete restoration (Curren Environmental).

Buyers pay for the sweep; it is textbook due diligence. And the industry's open secret, per Curren's buyer's guide: most sellers never order one, because they do not want to find a tank.

You can understand the impulse. It is still a terrible strategy.

AI enters this picture only at the edges so far. Interpreting a GPR B-scan, picking the hyperbolic signature of a buried cylinder out of soil noise, is currently expert human work. But it is exactly the kind of pattern recognition deep learning eats for breakfast, and the literature is moving fast: Faster R-CNN adapted to GPR B-scans for buried-object detection (Pham and Lefevre, IGARSS 2018), second-order deep networks classifying buried objects from radar thumbnails with strong results on small training sets (arXiv 2410.07117, 2024), and a 2025 review in MDPI Applied Sciences cataloguing YOLO, SSD, and R-CNN variants adapted to subsurface imaging, with pipeline-classification studies hitting 92 to 98 percent accuracy. Honest status: research-grade, validated on utility and pipeline data, not on residential tank surveys. No commercial "AI tank finder" for homebuyers exists today, so do not wait for one.

The myth that costs people deals

Ask around any transaction involving an old house and someone will offer the reassuring line: the tank was abandoned properly. Sometimes that means it was filled with sand or foam. A New Jersey real estate attorney's blunt guidance is worth quoting in full spirit: a sand-filled tank still corrodes, still counts, and is not an acceptable scenario for any prospective buyer. Insurers have denied coverage over the mere existence of a UST, and lenders have balked at mortgages. What actually protects you is not a verbal assurance but removal, soil sampling, and a state-issued No Further Action letter. Paperwork beats promises.

This is also where the math gets clarifying. A GPR sweep costs a few hundred dollars against a simple cleanup's $20,000 to $50,000, which makes the sweep roughly one percent of the problem it screens for and one of the cheapest risk-adjusted purchases in the entire homebuying process. Industry veterans figured this out; buyers who skip the sweep are usually the ones who never heard of it.

The strongest case against worrying

Fairness requires the other side at full strength. Most sweeps find either no tank or an intact one, and an intact tank is a $2,000 removal, not a catastrophe. Massachusetts's 110 reported leaks a year against 650,000 oil-heated homes is a reported rate around 0.017 percent annually, though underreporting is certain since nobody is required to look. That 75-percent sweep hit rate is one firm's experience in the densest oil-heat market in the country, not a national figure; in much of the South and West the prior probability is near zero. And the AI angle, to repeat, is prospective. What finds your tank today is a technician with an expensive antenna and twenty years of reading hyperbolas, not a model.

None of that changes the expected-value arithmetic for a buyer in oil country. It just means the right response is a purchase order, not panic.

What we did not prove

An honest accounting of what this article did not prove: that 75-percent figure comes from a single New Jersey firm's published experience and should not be treated as a national statistic; oil heat prevalence ranges from roughly a quarter of homes in Pennsylvania and Massachusetts to negligible across the Sun Belt. Cleanup cost bands are state-specific and turn on whether groundwater is impacted; the "simple versus complex" framing is one attorney's taxonomy, not a regulatory definition. New Jersey's closed-case counts measure remediations finished, not tanks leaking undetected, which are uncountable by definition. Machine-learning results for GPR interpretation come from pipeline, utility, and archaeology datasets; no published study validates them specifically on residential heating-oil tank surveys. Insurance and disclosure rules vary by state; this article documents New Jersey, New York, Massachusetts, Pennsylvania, and Oregon as examples, not as fifty-state advice.

The call

If you are buying a house built before roughly 1975 in Pennsylvania, New Jersey, New York, Connecticut, Massachusetts, Maine, or Oregon: put a GPR tank sweep in your inspection contingency, pay the few hundred dollars yourself, and treat it as non-optional as the home inspection. If the sweep finds a tank, make the seller's obligations concrete before closing: removal, soil sampling, and a state No Further Action letter, written into the contract. A verbal "abandoned properly" is worth the paper it is printed on.

If you are selling a house of that vintage with a known tank, remove it before listing. A $1,000 to $2,000 proactive decommissioning beats a buyer's radar finding that kills the deal at the eleventh hour, and in New Jersey the UST Fund exists precisely for this. Check your homeowner's policy for pollution and contamination exclusions now, while nothing is leaking, and ask your agent specifically about heating-oil endorsements.

EPA exempted these tanks in 1988 and never looked back. States, insurers, and courts did not get the memo, or rather they got it and each wrote their own. Your job is narrower: know which regime your address sits in, spend the $400, and never buy a buried mystery you could have mapped.
