A $499 Agent on Your Face: What Muse's Accessibility Play Really Costs

By Priya Greenwood · October 29, 2026
← Back to AI Home Building

Stand in front of a grocery shelf and ask, out loud, "What am I looking at?"

That one sentence is doing an enormous amount of work. For a sighted person, it is a convenience. For a blind or low-vision person, it is independence: no flagging down a stranger, no squinting at a label, no app that only reads the barcode if you hold the phone at exactly the right angle. And according to the Paschall Power newsletter, an accessibility publication that covers technology and independence, this exact scenario is why Meta's announcement at Connect 2026 may be "one of the most exciting accessibility developments of the year."

Meta said on September 23 that Muse, its personal AI agent, is coming to AI glasses "in the coming months." You will be able to wake the agent by saying its name, and it will be able to act on what you are looking at: a product on a shelf, a flyer on a wall, a school-supply list on the counter. Voice mode will keep a conversation open while Muse works in the background. Separately, Meta got a hearing enhancement feature for its glasses cleared by the FDA: a $149.99 software add-on that turns compatible frames into an over-the-counter hearing aid for adults with perceived mild-to-moderate hearing loss.

So here is the question that matters to the people who need these tools: what can you buy this week, what can you actually do with it, and what is still a keynote slide? I ran the availability ledger and the cost math, because nobody else assembled them in one place.

What ships, what waits: the availability ledger

Start with what is real right now. Ray-Ban Meta Audio, the camera-free glasses, shipped October 13 at $349. Ray-Ban Meta Gen 3, with a camera, starts at $449. Entry-level Meta Glasses start at $249. Hearing enhancement, the FDA-cleared feature, is launching in the United States "later this year," first on select Meta Glasses, not the Ray-Ban models, with a 30-day free trial and a $149.99 one-time cost afterward, or bundled with a Meta One subscription. Setup happens at home: a short hearing test in the Meta app tunes the amplification, per ear, with no clinic visit and no prescription.

Now what is not real yet. Muse on glasses has no date beyond "the coming months," no published list of supported glasses, and no published detail on which countries get it when. Muse Charm, the keychain gadget for talking to the agent without glasses, ships in December with no price announced. Realtime Avatar, the video face for your agent, has no release date at all. Every accessibility scenario that involves the agent seeing your shelf, reading your flyer, or working in the background while you walk is prospective. Glasses on shelves today come with the older Meta AI assistant, not with Muse.

This distinction matters because the most exciting sentence in the announcement, the grocery-shelf moment, requires the unshipped part. Meta AI on current glasses can already identify objects and translate text, which is genuinely useful. But the persistent agent that knows your shopping list, checks it against the shelf, and orders what is missing while you keep walking, that is the Muse pitch, and it is not on sale.

Running the cost math nobody published

Methodology first, because numbers without inputs are advertising. All prices are the ones Meta or the reporting named; the comparison anchors are the published anchor prices, not cherry-picked deals.

Adding it up: glasses at $349 (camera-free Audio) plus Hearing Enhancement at $149.99 equals $498.99, call it $499. Cheapest path in: the $249 entry frames plus the $149.99 add-on, about $399, though those frames are not first in line for the hearing feature. With the camera-equipped Gen 3 at $449 plus the add-on, the total is about $599, and per the reporting the Ray-Ban models are not expected to support hearing enhancement at launch.

Compare. Apple's AirPods Pro 2, with its own FDA-cleared hearing aid feature, is the predicate device in Meta's own FDA filing. Traditional prescription hearing aids start around $800 per pair and run commonly into $1,000 to $4,000 per ear. On paper, Meta's $399 to $499 stack is dramatically cheaper than a clinic-fitted pair. But the comparison has a hole: AirPods Pro 2 exist, with the hearing feature shipping today, at $249 retail, and Meta's version does not. Cheap and late: the affordable stack is the one that has not arrived.

And there is a subscription shadow on the math. Hearing enhancement costs $149.99 once, or comes with Meta One. Muse itself is free with usage limits, $20 a month for Power, $100 a month for Maximum. Background work, long conversations, and seeing what you see: the agent scenarios the accessibility story depends on will presumably land on the paid tiers first, since Meta already said the Wonder model rolls out to subscribers first. Nobody has published what an accessibility-grade Muse experience costs per month, because the experience does not exist yet.

Why the vision case is genuinely different

It would be a disservice to end on the fine print, because the underlying idea has real force. A smartphone is a terrible accessibility device in practice. It requires holding, aiming, tapping, and reading a screen, every one of which is a task that vision loss makes harder. Glasses sit on the face, leave the hands free, and listen. A wearable agent that answers to a name and acts on the visual scene removes the interface entirely. That is not a feature improvement over phone apps. It is a category change in how assistive technology fits into a day.

Hearing has a subtler strength. Open-ear audio, the glasses' speakers delivering amplified voice-focused sound without blocking the ear canal, means the wearer still hears the room. For someone with mild loss who needs a boost in conversation, not isolation from the world, that is arguably the right trade-off. Meta's clinical study of 142 adults at three US sites found self-fitting performed comparably to fitting by a licensed professional, which is exactly the claim that makes an at-home, no-prescription product credible for its narrow audience.

Narrow is the operative word. Adults 18 and older with perceived mild-to-moderate loss. Not severe, not uneven between ears, not anyone who needs a professional's judgment. That FDA clearance is a 510(k): substantial equivalence to an existing device, not an approval after novel testing, and "cleared" is the right verb. Meta names Apple's AirPods Pro 2 hearing aid feature as the predicate device in its filing, and the FDA record, number K254044, decided July 13, 2026, shows no third-party reviewer on the decision.

Steelmanning the other side

Here is the honest counterargument, stated at full strength. An accessibility promise tied to a $349 device, a $150 add-on, and a still-unshipped agent is a paywall in front of people who need help now, not later. Disability does not wait for product roadmaps. People who most need the grocery-shelf moment cannot schedule their needs around Meta's rollout cadence.

Then there is the camera on the face. Current Muse community notes from late October include users disabling the "Hey Meta" wake word in public and using the touchpad instead, precisely because bystanders assume they are being recorded. That anxiety is rational. An accessibility device that makes the people around you uncomfortable is an accessibility device that does not get worn. Meta's camera-free Audio model is the honest answer to that problem, but it is also the model that cannot do any visually-aware AI tricks, per the hands-on reporting, which means it cannot do the grocery-shelf moment at all. The device that sees is the device that worries people. The device that does not worry people cannot see.

Finally, the agent itself is unproven in the role being advertised. One market-watch analysis of the announcement is blunt: Meta supplied no evidence of completion rates or adoption for the glasses integration. An agent that misreads a shelf, mishears an address, or silently fails a background task is not an accessibility tool; it is an accessibility liability. Every assistant launch since 2014 sits in a graveyard of demos that worked on stage and stuttered in a kitchen.

What to actually do this week

If you or someone in your family is weighing this, here is the honest playbook.

For hearing needs today: AirPods Pro 2, with its hearing aid feature already shipping, is the available product. Meta's hearing enhancement is worth a 30-day free trial when it lands, if the loss is genuinely mild, if the glasses fit comfortably for all-day wear, and if a phone-based hearing test feels trustworthy for your situation. Do not buy glasses now on the promise that the hearing feature will arrive for your model; the launch list is not published.

For vision help today: the current glasses run Meta AI, not Muse, and reviewers confirm it can identify objects and read signs hands-free. That is real and useful. But if the pitch that sold you was an agent that knows your context, works in the background, and acts on what it sees, wait. That product has not shipped, has no date, and the price tier it lands on is not announced.

For everyone: check the hearing enhancement HSA and FSA eligibility if you use those accounts, because $149.99 pre-tax is a different number than $149.99 after-tax. And if you buy the camera glasses for an accessibility purpose, learn the touchpad activation now, because the social friction of a wake word in public is the difference between a device that gets worn and one that stays in the case.

What this article did not prove

Limits in the open. This analysis is built on Meta's Connect announcements as reported against the Meta newsroom, the FDA 510(k) record K254044 as summarized by audiology reporting, the Paschall Power accessibility newsletter, CNET's Connect hands-on, and community usage notes from late October 2026. We did not test Muse on glasses, because it has not shipped. We did not test hearing enhancement, because it has not launched. That 142-person clinical study is Meta's reported result from its own FDA summary, and independent evaluation is thin. If Meta publishes a glasses-integration date, a supported-models list, or Muse pricing for the accessibility scenarios, the math and the ledger in this article change, and we will update both.

Accessibility technology should be judged by one standard: does it work for the person who needs it, today, at a price they can reach? By that standard, Meta has assembled an impressive stack of almosts. That grocery-shelf moment is coming. It just has not arrived.
