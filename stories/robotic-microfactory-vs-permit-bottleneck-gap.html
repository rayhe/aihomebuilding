Construction Technology August 4, 2026

Sixteen Thousand Homes Burned. One Year Later, Seven Have Been Rebuilt. The Robot Isn't the Bottleneck.

A robotic microfactory in Pacific Palisades can fabricate a house in 12 weeks. Los Angeles County takes 95 business days to issue the permit. Billions went to building houses faster while the paperwork pipeline that controls whether you can start building operates at 1970s speed.

By Jake Kowalski

Sue Labella lived in Pacific Palisades for forty-eight years before fire consumed her home on January 7, 2025, along with roughly 16,000 other structures across Los Angeles in what became California's most destructive wildfire event in recorded history. Her builder, Cover, secured a rebuild permit on May 23, manufactured all-steel non-combustible panels at its Gardena factory, assembled them on-site without a crane, and delivered a certificate of occupancy on January 7, 2026, exactly one year after everything burned down. Cover promoted that timeline as a triumph of modern construction, and within the context of Pacific Palisades, it was: Labella's home ranked among the fastest rebuilds in the entire fire zone, a showcase of what pre-engineered, factory-fabricated steel panelization delivers when a team is laser-focused on compressing every phase of the construction calendar.

As of that same one-year anniversary, Los Angeles County reported that seven homes had been completed across all unincorporated fire areas.

Seven out of sixteen thousand.

Three miles from Labella's finished lot, a tent sits on a vacant parcel in Pacific Palisades, and inside it, an ABB IRB 6710 robotic arm bolts two-by-six lumber into structural wall panels while two workers feed materials at one end and pull finished panels off at the other. Cosmic Buildings, a San Francisco-based construction technology company organized as a Public Benefit Corporation, operates this mobile microfactory with a simple promise: a panel every twenty minutes, a full house frame in ten days, and a move-in-ready home twelve weeks after ground-breaking at $550 to $700 per square foot, roughly 30 percent below what traditional builders charge in the same zip code.

Twelve weeks to build. Ninety-five business days for Los Angeles County to grant you permission to start.

How a Rebuild Timeline Actually Works

Here is what city, county, and federal data reveals about the full journey from fire to front door for a Pacific Palisades homeowner, reconstructed as of mid-2026, with each phase measured not in the rosy projections of press conferences but in the averages that bureaucratic dashboards reluctantly disclose.

Debris removal consumed three to six months. Governor Newsom's office declared phase one "months ahead of schedule" by mid-2025, with nearly 10,000 home sites cleared, and this step, mercifully, moved fastest because heavy equipment and dump trucks do not require discretionary review from seven overlapping jurisdictions before a bucket can scoop ash off a foundation slab.

Insurance and financing remain ongoing and unresolved for thousands of families. According to SBA data, the agency approved over 12,000 disaster loans totaling $3.2 billion for Los Angeles wildfire survivors, but only 22 percent of that funding had been disbursed by January 2026 because homeowners could not draw down loans without permits, and permits required completion of a review process that, for most applicants, had not yet concluded, creating a Kafkaesque cycle in which money exists, the lot is cleared, the builder is ready, and the homeowner waits.

Permitting: 95 business days on average for new residential construction in LA County, translating to roughly nineteen calendar weeks or four and a half months. Under a post-fire "expedited" process, processing had averaged 51 days by July 2025, at which point only 90 of 1,207 requests had been authorized. By January 2026, LA had received 3,075 applications for 1,400 unique Palisades addresses and issued 1,448 permits covering 689 of those addresses, leaving more than half the applicants waiting, many of them still living in temporary housing, rental units they cannot afford long-term, or with relatives whose generosity has an expiration date. In Altadena's Eaton Fire zone, 44 permits had been issued out of 890 applications. Dallas, by contrast, processes a residential permit in a median of eight days.

Construction (traditional): twelve to eighteen months for a custom single-family home, running $800 to $1,000-plus per square foot in Pacific Palisades, where even a modest three-bedroom on a hillside lot routinely exceeds 2,500 square feet and where tariffs on imported building materials and federal immigration enforcement actions that have thinned construction crews are compounding costs in ways that did not exist when these homes were first built.

Construction (robotic microfactory): twelve weeks at $550 to $700 per square foot, per Cosmic's published figures and press materials verified through ABB, Dezeen, and Engineering.com reporting.

Add those phases sequentially, which is how they work in practice because you cannot build before the permit clears and you cannot permit before debris is removed, and a traditional rebuild runs twenty-two to thirty months from lot clearance to certificate of occupancy, with actual stick-and-nail construction consuming roughly half that timeline. Swap in Cosmic's microfactory for the build phase and the total compresses to ten to thirteen months, a genuine and meaningful reduction that could save a displaced family an entire winter in temporary housing, but notice where the compression happens: entirely in the construction phase, which was already not the longest phase. Permit review, often the longest phase, remains untouched at four and a half months minimum. Even if you could teleport a finished house onto the lot instantaneously, reducing construction time to zero, the regulatory pipeline alone guarantees no home is completed in fewer than nineteen weeks after application.

Brilliant Engineering Aimed at Twenty Percent of a Problem

In isolation, Cosmic's technology is remarkable and genuinely novel in ways that most construction-robotics press releases are not. ABB's RobotStudio digital twin software integrates with Cosmic's AI-driven BIM platform so that every home is simulated, collision-checked, and optimized in a virtual environment before a single board gets cut, and once the microfactory deploys on-site, computer vision systems run alongside the robotic arm making real-time quality decisions, detecting sub-millimeter misalignments, and enforcing dimensional accuracy that even experienced framers cannot consistently match across a full production run. Homes produced by this system incorporate non-combustible cladding, integrated solar arrays with battery backup, and greywater recycling systems that exceed California's already-aggressive wildfire and energy efficiency codes. Cosmic operates a 1:10 philanthropic model, donating one home for every ten sold to families recovering from disaster.

None of that engineering touches the regulatory pipeline preceding construction: the overlapping jurisdictional reviews and discretionary planning approvals and special geographic ordinances and infrastructure sign-offs that exist independently of how fast anyone can put up walls, and that, in the specific case of these fires, have locked $2.5 billion in approved SBA loans inside accounts that survivors cannot access because a piece of paper authorizing them to pick up a hammer has not yet arrived. "It's not individual departments that are delaying the process," urban planning researcher Myung Kim told Grist. "It's the entirety of the network of reviews that needs to happen."

Consider scale. Cosmic is currently supporting 35 families and targeting 100 homes by 2027, which would address 0.6 percent of structures destroyed. At its current throughput of roughly 35 homes per year of operation, a complete rebuild of the fire zone by this one company alone would require approximately 457 years, and while nobody at Cosmic, ABB, or anywhere else claims a single mobile microfactory was designed to solve a metropolitan housing crisis, the technology is explicitly intended for replication, so even imagining ten or a hundred microfactories operating simultaneously, the permit queue remains the gating function because construction cannot begin until a reviewer signs off, and reviewers are processing applications at a pace that constitutes a humanitarian bottleneck in the aftermath of a disaster that displaced tens of thousands of people and left 78 percent of $3.2 billion in approved federal disaster loans sitting in accounts that no one can touch.

What Happened When LA Built an AI Permitting Tool

Officials have not entirely ignored the permitting crisis, and to their credit, the interventions have been both numerous and, on paper, aggressive. Newsom issued executive orders suspending certain California Environmental Quality Act requirements for fire rebuilds. Bass waived non-safety building mandates and deferred permit and plan-check fees. State and local officials launched what they described as an AI-assisted permitting tool, announcing that these combined reforms "could shave months off reconstruction timelines and save homeowners as much as $30,000" by avoiding mandates like rooftop solar and electrification requirements.

"Could" was doing extraordinary work in that press release. One year out, fewer than 15 percent of destroyed homes had received rebuild approvals, a number so alarming that SBA Administrator Kelly Loeffler publicly called out "local bureaucracy" as the primary impediment to recovery and extended disaster loan disbursement deadlines to prevent approved funds from expiring before homeowners could use them. Bass's celebrated "first rebuilt home" in November 2025 turned out to have had its permit secured before the fires started, as part of a pre-fire planned demolition that had nothing to do with post-disaster recovery, and the actual first completed post-fire rebuild, by Thomas James Homes, had also submitted its original plans before January 7.

Billions of dollars in venture capital and industrial R&D have poured into construction robotics over the past decade, with the market projected to grow at 20 percent annually through 2030, and the roster of partnerships reads like a who's-who of global industrial automation: Cosmic with ABB; Admares with Porsche Consulting, Siemens, NVIDIA, and EDAG Group, all collaborating on a planned AI-powered smart factory in Australia described as a "fully integrated, next-generation manufacturing environment for industrialised housing." Almost none of that investment has targeted the administrative layer that determines whether building can begin, and what the LA fires exposed in the harshest possible terms is a city where a robot can frame a house in ten days but where a homeowner waits 95 business days for paperwork.

What You Should Do If You Are Rebuilding

If you are a homeowner in a disaster zone, or building in any jurisdiction where permit timelines exceed your construction timeline, evaluate the regulatory calendar before you evaluate the builder. A Cosmic microfactory or a Cover panelized steel system will compress your construction phase by months, but if your permit is going to take 95 business days and your insurance resolution is still pending, the speed of your construction technology is a secondary concern at best and a distraction at worst. Ask your jurisdiction for its current average processing time, how many applications are queued ahead of yours, and whether expedited review paths exist for like-for-like rebuilds, which in LA means the rebuilt structure cannot exceed 110 percent of the original building's height and area; exceeding that threshold drops you into a full discretionary review with an entirely different timeline and an entirely different level of bureaucratic patience.

On cost, Cosmic's $550 to $700 per square foot looks dramatically cheaper than the $800 to $1,000-plus traditional LA builders are charging, and on a 2,500-square-foot home, the difference is $625,000 to $750,000 in real savings, a number that sounds almost absurd until you remember that Pacific Palisades rebuilding costs are themselves absurd. But those figures assume the microfactory can deliver consistently at that price point in production, and independent verification of per-unit economics at scale beyond the current 35-family deployment does not yet exist. Pilot pricing and production pricing are often different animals.

On financing, $2.5 billion in approved SBA loans sitting undisbursed because homeowners cannot get permits is not a construction problem and no robot will fix it. If you hold an SBA disaster loan, verify that the extended disbursement deadline covers your projected permit-plus-construction timeline. If it does not, contact SBA Disaster Assistance at (800) 659-2955 before your approved funds expire.

Where This Analysis Breaks

Cosmic's cost and timeline claims have not been independently audited at scale, and the 35 families currently served represent a pilot, not a production run with the kind of throughput data that would let anyone calculate reliable per-unit economics. LA County's 95-business-day average for permits includes all new residential construction, not only fire rebuilds, though the SBA and multiple reporting outlets have cited fire rebuild permitting as operating at comparable or slower speeds. County completion figures cover only unincorporated areas and may undercount city-jurisdiction finishes, though every data source examined paints the same picture of a recovery that has barely begun nineteen months after the fires. Dallas's eight-day permit median reflects a fundamentally different regulatory structure, building code framework, and disaster recovery context.

A strong counterargument exists, and it deserves its full weight: construction speed and permitting speed are not fully independent variables, because faster, more standardized construction systems like Cosmic's panelized approach produce plans that are more uniform, more predictable, and theoretically simpler to review, and if every rebuild used a pre-approved modular system with pre-certified fire, seismic, and energy compliance, permit review could in principle collapse from months to days, eliminating the bottleneck entirely by making the construction method itself the permit. But that outcome requires LA's regulatory apparatus to create a fast lane for pre-certified systems, which is exactly the kind of regulatory engineering that the construction-tech industry has spent the last decade ignoring in favor of faster hammers, smarter robots, and press releases about microfactories.

What these fires revealed is not that construction technology has failed, because it plainly has not, but that an industry pouring billions into automating the physical act of building has left the administrative act of authorizing construction almost entirely unautomated, and that in a crisis where speed is measured in displaced families per month, the gap between the two is not theoretical. It is the distance between a robot that works and a bureaucracy that does not.
