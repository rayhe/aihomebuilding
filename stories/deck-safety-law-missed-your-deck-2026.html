California Just Forced Every Apartment Balcony Inspected. Your Deck Wasn't on the List.

By Catherine Chen · September 12, 2026

Thirteen people stood on a fifth-floor balcony in Berkeley on the night of June 15, 2015, celebrating a 21st birthday. Then the balcony fell. Six people died and seven were injured when the wood-framed structure, weakened by years of hidden dry rot, tore away from the building. From the street, nothing had looked wrong.

California answered with two laws. Senate Bill 721 forces owners of apartment buildings with three or more units to hire a licensed architect or structural engineer to inspect every exterior elevated element, balconies, decks, stairways, walkways, more than six feet above the ground. AB 2579 extended the first inspection deadline from 2025 to January 1 of this year. Senate Bill 326 imposed the same duty on condo and co-op HOAs, written into the Davis-Stirling Act at Civil Code section 5551, with a deadline of January 1, 2025 and re-inspections every nine years instead of six. Both deadlines have now passed, and owners who missed them are out of compliance, with consequences waiting at sale, refinance, and disclosure.

Read the fine print and you will find the hole. SB 721 covers rental buildings. SB 326 covers common-interest developments. Neither covers the single-family house with the 25-year-old deck out back. Most American decks sit on single-family lots, and nobody in Sacramento has scheduled their inspection.

Thirty Million Expired Decks

The North American Deck and Railing Association estimates that roughly 30 million decks across North America have outlived their practical service life. More than 40 million U.S. decks are at least 20 years old, built before current codes required ledger boards to be bolted rather than nailed to the house. Industry data consistently pins the vast majority of deck collapses on one failure: the ledger board separating from the house. When a deck goes, it goes fast and without warning. NADRA says more than 75 percent of the people on a collapsing deck are injured or killed.

Between 2016 and 2019, about 2,900 injuries came from deck, porch, and balcony collapses, according to the Consumer Product Safety Commission. Industry estimates run around 6,000 structural-failure injuries a year. In September 2019, a deck collapsed on the Jersey Shore and injured 22 people at once. With maintenance, the average wood deck lasts 10 to 15 years. A lot of the decks standing right now were built when a ledger attachment was a row of nails and a prayer.

Bolts, Not Nails: What the Code Says

Few code provisions are this unambiguous. Ledger boards get lag screws or through-bolts. Not nails. Hardware has to be corrosion-resistant, because pressure-treated lumber eats ordinary fasteners for breakfast. A ledger retrofit is a few hundred dollars in hardware and a Saturday for a competent carpenter, which makes it the cheapest life-safety upgrade on most houses. Your feelings and your weekend plans are not the code's concern. It cares that the board holding up your deck is bolted to the house.

Machines Enter the Inspection Business

Meanwhile the inspection business is getting an AI layer, and it is worth knowing exactly what these tools do and do not do. Spectora, the home-inspection software company, launched AI tools in June: a photo-first workflow where the inspector shoots first and the software drafts the report for confirmation, an AI scheduling agent that books jobs while the inspector is under a crawlspace, and a connector that lets inspectors query their own business data through Claude or ChatGPT. CEO Peter Osberg spent more than a year building them with working inspectors, and his line is worth quoting: a tool that gets things wrong doesn't save time, it creates more work.

On the insurer side, Nationwide partnered with a Florida company called Chrp last December on AI-powered virtual home inspections. Policyholders complete a guided photo survey on a smartphone; Chrp's platform checks the images against more than 400 potential failure points, from corroded plumbing to outdated electrical panels, and produces a mitigation report. Nationwide's internal data says 70 percent of non-catastrophic claims start inside the home, with average fire losses above $88,000 and water losses around $15,000. Worth noting: when the AI finds a hazard, your case gets escalated to a Nationwide representative. Remember: the camera points both ways.

For a homeowner, the most interesting tool is the free one. Alpine Building Performance, a Colorado inspection company, released Alpine Intelligence in March, a ChatGPT-powered forecaster that predicts what a full inspection will find. Founder Andrew Sams built it with partner Mason Minor after the pandemic boom left agents writing offers $60,000 over asking on houses they had walked through for five minutes. Agents started asking him to come to showings. He was slammed. Now the AI goes to the showing.

What the Camera Cannot See

None of this replaces the licensed professional the law requires, and the law's reasoning survived contact with the evidence. In Berkeley, the dry rot was sealed inside the structure. No smartphone photo would have caught it. AI photo screening catches the visible warning signs, staining, deflection, corroded flashing, missing hardware. It cannot see inside a ledger board or a sealed balcony cavity, which is exactly where the catastrophic failures hide. Spectora's AI makes the inspector faster; it does not make the inspector optional. Alpine's tool is a forecaster, explicitly not the inspection. And Chrp works for your insurer first. Useful, but remember who signs the report and who reads it.

What to Do About Your Deck

  1. Buying a California apartment building or condo: the inspection deadlines have passed, so demand the SB 721 or SB 326 report at closing. A missing report is a disclosure problem for the seller and negotiating leverage for you.
  2. Owning a single-family home with a deck seven years or older: you are outside the law and on your own. Run NADRA's annual checklist. Probe suspicious wood with an ice pick; if it sinks more than a quarter inch, or the wood feels spongy and breaks off without splintering, you have rot. Then check whether your ledger is bolted or nailed.
  3. If the ledger is nailed: fix it before the next party. A through-bolt retrofit is the cheapest life-safety money you will spend on the house, and it addresses the single most common collapse mode.
  4. Renting: SB 721 gave your building a January 1, 2026 deadline. Ask the landlord for the report. A law is only as good as the tenants who invoke it.

California spent a decade building an inspection regime for the balconies that killed six people in Berkeley. It works, as far as it goes. It just doesn't go to your house. Your deck has the same wood, the same weather, and in many cases the same nails. No state inspector is coming to check it. An AI will take a look for free. Bolts are up to you.
