Your Heat Pump Qualified for an $8,000 Rebate. You Installed It a Month Too Early.

By Priya Greenwood, September 13, 2026, Sustainability & Green Building

New Hampshire's energy office posted the rule in plain English: rebates are only available for projects started after the program officially launches, and they were anticipating a spring 2026 opening, which means the calendar on your contract matters as much as the equipment on your roof. Any homeowner who replaced a dying furnace in February, reasonably assuming a federal program signed into law in 2022 would cover it, gets nothing, and the cruelty is that they did exactly what the program wanted, just too soon, with identical equipment and identical income; only the invoice date disqualifies them.

That is the defining feature of the High-Efficiency Electric Home Rebate program. It is a $4.275 billion federal effort to put heat pumps, panel upgrades, and insulation into low- and moderate-income homes. And the money is real: up to $14,000 per household, applied at the register, with households under 80 percent of area median income getting 100 percent of project costs covered and those between 80 and 150 percent getting half. Per the New Hampshire program schedule: $8,000 toward a heat pump for space heating and cooling, $4,000 toward an electrical panel upgrade, $2,500 for wiring, $1,750 for a heat pump water heater, $1,600 for insulation and air sealing. But every dollar is gated by sequence. Nobody will warn you before you sign the work order.

Consider what the order of operations is worth on a single $14,000 heat pump installation, where a household at 75 percent of area median income gets the $8,000 rebate in full and pays only $6,000 out of pocket. A household at 100 percent of AMI gets 50 percent of costs, which is $7,000 against the $8,000 cap, and pays $7,000, while the same household that hired a contractor one month before its state's launch pays the full $14,000. One signature date, eight thousand dollars. No appeals process.

California makes the sequencing even more explicit: its Phase I rules require homeowners to complete income verification, then have their contractor submit a reservation request, then get approval, and only then install. Install before your reservation clears and you are simply ineligible. Funding is first come, first served, and only TECH-certified, HEEHRA-trained contractors can issue the discount. Your trusted HVAC guy of fifteen years can do the install perfectly and still not qualify to hand you the discount. That is where the contractor knowledge gap turns expensive. Nearly half of the 1,154 contractors the Building Performance Association surveyed in all 50 states said they would need to learn more just to decide which rebate pathway to pursue. A separate Department of Energy guidance document admits the quiet truth about remodels: when homeowners hire siding companies, they do not ask about insulation, and the siding companies do not suggest it, because suggesting it means coordinating with another trade and risking delays. Those rebates were designed to fix exactly this, by giving remodelers a reason to bundle efficiency work. Your contractor at the kitchen table remains the last person who reliably knows they exist.

It gets worse before it gets better. The federal 25C tax credit for heat pumps, worth up to $2,000 a year, expired for equipment placed in service after December 31, 2025. For years homeowners stacked that credit on top of rebates as a bonus. Now the HEEHRA rebate is not the bonus; it is the whole game. And unlike a tax credit, it has to be fronted by the contractor at the point of sale, which means small shops that cannot float $8,000 per job will quietly opt out of participating.

Then there is the geography problem: the money flows through state energy offices, and states launched on their own timelines or not at all, with New York and Wisconsin first out of the gate, New Hampshire targeting spring 2026, and most other states somewhere between application and silence. As of the last public accounting, only a fraction of the 50 states and territories expecting to participate had approved, launched programs, and the rest were somewhere between application and silence, which means the federal promise and your local reality can be years apart. If your state has not launched, there is no rebate to claim, no matter what the federal statute promises, which is why checking your state's status is step zero, and it is the step most people skip.

Here is the actionable sequence. The order is everything. First, check whether your state's program is live through your state energy office or the DOE's state tracker, not through your contractor, who has no obligation to track it and every incentive to keep the job simple. Second, run your income against the area median income tiers: under 80 percent gets you full coverage up to the per-measure caps, 80 to 150 percent gets you half, over 150 percent gets you nothing from this program. Third, find an enrolled contractor through your state's certified list, California's TECH finder being the model. A non-enrolled contractor cannot issue the point-of-sale discount, which is the only form the money takes. Fourth, complete the income verification and get the reservation approved before anyone touches your house. Only then schedule the install. If you are mid-project right now, stop and check the sequence before the next invoice posts, because work already performed cannot be unwound into eligibility.

One more thing worth saying plainly: this program targets the households most vulnerable to predatory sales, the same households the rebates were written to protect. Consumer advocates at the California Public Utilities Commission warned that the same generous rebates that help low-income families are exactly what high-pressure door-to-door contractors exploited under PACE financing, where some homeowners saw property tax bills multiply by a factor of twenty. Their recommendation was blunt, do not let contractors market these rebates, require vetting and certification, and publish the licensed names in a place a homeowner can actually find, because the information gap cuts both ways and you should verify the contractor through the state's list before you verify anything else.

What this analysis could not pin down: state-by-state launch status as of this month, because launch dates keep slipping and the most recent comprehensive public accounting is dated. Those survey numbers are from 2022 to 2023, before any state launched, so contractor knowledge has likely improved, though the structural incentives have not: fronting $8,000 at point of sale still breaks a small shop's cash flow, and no amount of awareness fixes arithmetic. That $14,000 installed-cost figure for a heat pump is representative of a typical whole-home system, not a national average; ductless mini-splits run less, and homes needing panel and wiring upgrades run more, which is precisely why the panel and wiring rebates exist as separate line items.

The uncomfortable bottom line is that a $4.275 billion program designed for households that can least afford mistakes punishes the most common mistake of all, doing the work in the wrong order, and the penalty lands hardest on the families the money was meant to rescue. The rebate does not reward need; it rewards paperwork done in sequence. Treat the reservation like a building permit, something you secure before the work begins, and the money is straightforward; treat it like a coupon you clip after the fact, and eight thousand dollars evaporates on a technicality.
