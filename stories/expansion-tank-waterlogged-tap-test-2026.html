Tap Your Water Heater's Expansion Tank. If the Whole Thing Thuds, It's Dead.

By Jake Kowalski, September 13, 2026, Construction Technology

Go look at your water heater: above it, on the cold water line, hangs a small tank about the size of a football, probably painted blue or gray. Knock on it with your knuckle: the top half should ring hollow and the bottom half should thud solid. If the whole thing thuds solid, you just diagnosed a dead expansion tank in four seconds, putting you ahead of most homeowners, who have never touched the thing.

That little tank has exactly one job, created by what changed in your plumbing. When your water heater fires and fifty gallons of cold water get hot, the water expands, and in an older plumbing system the extra volume just shoved itself back out through the meter into the street main, no harm done. Modern systems do not work that way anymore. Your water utility has very likely installed a backflow preventer, a check valve, or a pressure-reducing valve on your service line, which turns your home's plumbing into a sealed box. With nowhere for the expanded water to go, every heating cycle, day after day, pushes the pressure higher.

Inside the expansion tank, a rubber bladder divides the vessel in two, one side connected to your plumbing and the other holding air pre-charged to match your house's water pressure, and that air acts as a spring: it compresses when the heated water needs room, then pushes the water back out when things cool down. Elegant, cheap, and invisible when it fails: when the bladder ruptures or the air charge bleeds away, the tank fills solid with water, a condition plumbers call waterlogged, and from the outside it looks like a working one, with no drip, no noise, no warning light: just a steel football that stopped doing its job months ago.

Run the numbers and the stakes get obvious fast. Heat fifty gallons of water from 50 degrees to 120 degrees and the water's density drops from about 999.7 to 988.1 kilograms per cubic meter, roughly 1.17 percent more volume, about six-tenths of a gallon, which sounds like nothing until you remember water barely compresses: its bulk modulus is around 319,000 PSI, so in a perfectly rigid sealed system that six-tenths of a gallon would theoretically spike pressure by something like 3,700 PSI. Real pipes flex, so you never see thousands of PSI in practice, but the calculation shows the scale of the force, and it explains why the temperature-and-pressure relief valve on your heater, set to open at 150 PSI, starts dripping when the tank dies. That drip is your system screaming, and most people just put a bucket under it.

Code is unambiguous about all of this: Section P2903.4 of the International Residential Code requires a means of controlling thermal expansion pressure wherever a pressure-reducing valve, backflow preventer, or check valve creates a closed system, and the field guides plumbers carry state it more bluntly: the T&P relief valve is not to be used for thermal expansion control. That relief valve is the last line of defense, not the daily pressure manager, and forcing it to cycle on every heating run wears out the one device standing between a failed thermostat and a genuinely catastrophic overpressure event. The Arapahoe County building department's clarification puts the installer's duty plainly: find the check valve or PRV, and if one exists, the system is closed and expansion protection is required.

So here is the full inspection, and it takes less time than reading this paragraph took. First, the tap test you already did: hollow up top, solid down low, and a full solid thud means waterlogged. Second, press the little tire-style Schrader valve on the tank's air side. A hiss of air means the bladder is intact, though you should still check the pre-charge with a tire gauge against your house's static pressure: Amtrol's installation manual warns it must be set with the tank isolated and empty, a step installers skip constantly. Water coming out of the air valve means the bladder is ruptured, and the tank is done. Third, screw a ten-dollar pressure gauge onto a hose bib before bed and read it in the morning. A big overnight climb toward triple digits means expansion pressure has nowhere to go. Fourth, look at the discharge pipe running off the T&P valve: full pipe size, downhill the whole way, no caps, no valves, no threaded end, terminating where you would actually see it drip. Capped or uphill discharge piping is a genuine emergency, not a someday project.

Honestly, though, not every house needs an expansion tank, because if you are on a well, or your service line has no check valve, no PRV, and no backflow preventer, your system is open and the expanding water pushes harmlessly back toward the main. Meanwhile, water utilities keep adding backflow preventers at meters for perfectly good public-health reasons, and each one silently converts another open system into a closed one with no notice to the homeowner. Your plumbing changed and nobody told you.

Skeptics also point out, fairly, that the T&P valve exists precisely to prevent disaster, and a dripping relief valve is doing its job, true as far as it goes, but chronic overpressure is doing slow damage the whole time the valve holds: it shortens water heater life, it voids most manufacturer warranties, and the first things to let go under sustained spikes are usually the braided stainless supply hoses under your sinks, which fail at 2 a.m. and run until morning. Worst case, a simultaneous thermostat failure and a stuck relief valve turns a water heater into a pressure vessel. Rare, but the physics is not theoretical. The MythBusters demonstration that launched a fifty-gallon heater several hundred feet into the air was entertainment, but the energy it demonstrated was real.

What this story cannot tell you: nobody has measured what fraction of installed expansion tanks are waterlogged right now, because nobody inspects them. The five-to-ten-year lifespan figure comes from plumber trade experience, not a controlled study, though the failure mechanism is well understood, with one detailed teardown analysis tracing nearly every premature failure to wrong pre-charge at installation or slow bleed-down afterward. The 3,700 PSI figure is a rigid-vessel textbook calculation to convey scale, not a field measurement. And whether your specific home is a closed system takes five minutes at the meter to determine, which this article cannot do for you.

Almost offensively cheap: a two-gallon thermal expansion tank retails for forty-five to seventy dollars. A plumber will swap one in under an hour, typically one hundred fifty to three hundred dollars all in, and the critical step most installers skip is setting the pre-charge to match your house pressure before the tank goes on the pipe, so ask for that explicitly. Then put a recurring reminder on your phone: once a year, tap the tank. Hollow up top, solid down low. Four seconds a year for the part that keeps every heating cycle in your house from hammering your pipes, your valves, and your water heater toward an early grave.
